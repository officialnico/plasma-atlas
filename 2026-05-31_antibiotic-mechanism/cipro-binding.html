How does cipro find

It doesn't search and it isn't alive. It's a rigid little shape — about 41 atoms — that heat flings around at random, colliding with everything millions of times a second. It sticks only where the shape and the electric charges both happen to match. Four tabs, from the random bumping to the exact geometric grip.

Each pink molecule is cipro; the grey ones are all the other junk floating in a cell. None of them aim. They're shoved around purely by thermal motion (Brownian motion) and ricochet off each other and the walls. The slot on the right is the binding site — the cut DNA–gyrase complex. When a cipro happens to drift in facing the right way, the matching shape and charges hold it and it locks. Grey molecules can enter the same slot and just fall back out — nothing holds them. Watch the counter: thousands of pointless collisions for each real bind. That randomness is the mechanism.

healthy enzyme Why one tiny change defeats it
The grip depends on two specific amino-acid residues — Ser83 and Asp87 — offering the right atoms in the right spot for the water–metal bridge. Flip the switch to swap a single residue (Ser83 → Leu). Leu can't hold the water bridge: one anchor of three is gone, the grip falls apart, and the drug drifts back out. The bacterium is now resistant — even though everything else about the drug is unchanged. This exact mutation is common in Campylobacter across Southeast Asia, which is why cipro sometimes underperforms there.

And you? Your cells run a different enzyme (human topoisomerase II) whose pocket lacks this Ser/Asp geometry, so the water–metal bridge can't form and cipro binds it far too weakly to matter at normal doses. Same reason the drug grips bacteria and lets you go: it's all shape and charge, no intent on anyone's part.
