Bind it, and it gets lighter.

A uranium‑235 nucleus swallows a neutron and becomes uranium‑236 — yet U‑236 weighs less than the U‑235 and the neutron did apart. The missing mass didn't vanish. It left as energy. Watch it leave.

So which side is heavier?

The separate ingredients always outweigh the thing they become once it's bound — because binding spills energy out, and energy has weight.

01

Things that attract sit lower once together

The neutron and the nucleus pull on each other through the strong nuclear force, just as the rock and the Earth pull on each other through gravity. A bound pair sits lower in that pull than two free pieces do — like a rock resting at the pool's bottom versus one held in the air.

02

Falling into the bond releases energy

To drop from "apart" to "bound," the system must shed the difference. The nucleus flings out a gamma photon; the rock throws up a splash, ripples, a little heat and sound. In both cases energy radiates outward, away from the system.

03

Energy that leaves takes mass with it

This is the whole trick: E = mc² says energy and mass are the same currency. When 6.5 MeV of binding energy flies off as a gamma ray, it carries 0.00703 u of mass with it. The leftover U‑236 is genuinely, measurably lighter than the parts that made it.

The seam in the analogy: a rock's splash radiates to the whole Earth, and its mass change is far too tiny to weigh. But the logic is identical — bound state sits lower, binding spills energy, and the energy that escapes is mass that left.

Masses are atomic masses in unified mass units (u). Electrons cancel on both sides, so they don't disturb the balance.  0.00703 u × 931.49 MeV/u ≈ 6.5 MeV — close to the real neutron‑capture gamma energy of U‑236.
