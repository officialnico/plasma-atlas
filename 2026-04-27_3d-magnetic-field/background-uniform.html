What "background uniform" means

Splitting the plasma into the steady part and the wiggly part

The plasma is doing two things at once:

1. The background: the slow, average state — average temperature, average density. Mostly steady.
2. The fluctuations: the wiggles on top — turbulent eddies, hot blobs, cold blobs. This is what GENE is actually solving for.

Total plasma = background + fluctuations. We split them apart because they live on completely different scales.

Animated · Total = background + fluctuations

Top: the total temperature you'd measure walking along some direction. Wiggly. Middle: the smooth average — the background. Bottom: what's left when you subtract the background out — the fluctuations. GENE only solves for the bottom part, treating the middle part as a fixed input.

01"Uniform" means the background doesn't depend on the direction

Now the key question for each direction: does the background change when you walk in that direction?

Walk in different directions · what does the background do?

In x: the background sloped — temperature drops as you walk outward. Walk a little way in x and the average temperature is genuinely different. Background is not uniform in x.

In y: the background is flat — wherever you stop, the average temperature is the same. The fluctuations are different (you're at a different point in the wiggle pattern), but the average doesn't care. Background is uniform in y.

A useful analogy

Imagine waves on a beach. The wave heights wiggle up and down — those are the fluctuations.

If you walk along the shore (parallel to the waterline), the average water level is the same wherever you are. Background uniform → like y.

If you walk into the ocean (perpendicular to shore), the average water level rises as you go deeper. Background not uniform → like x.

In both cases there are still wiggles (waves), but only walking-along-the-shore preserves the average. That's what "uniform background" means.

02Why uniformity matters for Fourier

Here's the connection to GENE: Fourier modes only decouple when the background is uniform.

If the background changes as you move, then the equations the fluctuations obey have *coefficients that depend on position*. A sine wave moving through a region with changing coefficients gets distorted — its wavelength shifts, its amplitude varies — which means it picks up other Fourier components. The k=3 mode bleeds into the k=4 mode. They couple.

If the background is the same everywhere, then the equations have *constant coefficients*. A sine wave stays a clean sine wave forever. The k=3 mode is k=3 forever. No bleeding, no coupling. Each mode is its own little decoupled world.

Animated · sine wave through uniform vs non-uniform background

Top: clean sine wave moving through uniform background — stays a clean sine wave. Bottom: same wave moving through non-uniform background — gets distorted, wavelength shifts, amplitude varies. The first one stays a single Fourier mode. The second one becomes a mixture.

The whole story in one paragraph

"Uniform background" means the slow average plasma state doesn't depend on that coordinate. y has a uniform background because the flux surface is symmetric — slide along y and nothing changes on average. x has a non-uniform background because that's where the gradient lives (hot core to cold edge). Uniform background → equations have constant coefficients → Fourier modes don't mix → each k_y is its own independent simulation. That's why GENE Fouriers y and not x.
