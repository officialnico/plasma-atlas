Eddies, y, and Fourier — the whole picture

Three separate things that often get conflated

There are three different questions tangled together, and once you separate them, the picture becomes much clearer:

1. What shape are the eddies?
2. Why do we Fourier-transform y specifically?
3. Why does it matter computationally?

01What shape are the eddies?

The eddies have a clear shape, and it's not "binormal-shaped." The eddies are parallel-elongated: long along B, short across B. They look like noodles or spaghetti running along field lines.

Eddy shape — long along B, narrow perpendicular

Toggle the views. In 3D, you see the eddy as a stretched tube along B. In the perpendicular cross-section, you see it from the end — it's roughly circular, about as wide in x as in y. The eddies don't prefer x or y. They're isotropic in the perpendicular plane.

Common confusion

"We Fourier-transform y because the eddies are formed in the y direction." → This is wrong. Eddies extend equally in both x and y perpendicular to B. The reason for Fouriering y is mathematical, not because of eddy geometry.

02Why Fourier in y, then?

Three independent properties of y, none of which are about eddy shape:

x
background uniform? no — gradient
naturally periodic? no
modes decouple? no
y
background uniform? yes
naturally periodic? yes
modes decouple? yes ✓
z
background uniform? no — |B| varies
naturally periodic? no (twisted)
modes decouple? no

This is purely about where the math works, not about eddy shape. y wins all three.

03Why does it matter?

If you store wiggles as Fourier modes in y, and the background is uniform in y, then each k_y mode is its own little decoupled simulation. You can put each k_y on its own processor. They don't need to share data with each other (except for nonlinear interactions, which we'll set aside). That's a massive parallelization win.

Each k_y is its own world — parallel computation

Eight strips, each one a different k_y mode. They evolve at their own pace, with their own frequency and growth rate, completely independently of each other. GENE distributes these across compute nodes and runs them in parallel.

Try this with x: you can't. The k_x modes don't decouple — they mix together because the background is non-uniform. So you'd have to do everything together, on one processor, with constant communication. Painful.

04Putting all three together

The actual chain of reasoning

1. Drift-wave physics produces eddies that are elongated along B and small in the perpendicular plane. Both x and y matter for resolving these eddies.

2. The flux surface is statistically symmetric in y (rotational symmetry around the binormal). So the background plasma profile depends on x but not on y.

3. Statistical symmetry in y means y-translation is a symmetry of the equations, which means k_y is a good quantum number — different k_y modes don't mix at the linear level.

4. Therefore: store y as Fourier (each k_y on its own processor), store x and z as real-space grids (because the background varies there). This is the GENE architecture, full stop.

One-line summary

Eddies have shape because of physics. y gets Fourier-treated because of symmetry, which is a separate fact. The two are related (both come from the geometry of the field) but they are not the same fact.
