16 points? Really?

The flux tube trick — and why a tiny periodic box is all GENE needs

You caught something real. A field line in a typical tokamak has irrational pitch — it never closes on itself. If you tried to follow one all the way around and resolve every wiggle, you'd need millions of points. But GENE doesn't do that.

01The flux tube

GENE picks a tiny pencil-shaped region — a flux tube — that hugs one field line for just one poloidal turn. That's the entire simulation domain. Everything else on the flux surface is assumed to look statistically the same.

So the tube is ~5 cm × 5 cm wide and ~30 m long. The eddies inside it are ~5 mm. You only need enough gridpoints to resolve a few eddies in each direction — that's where the small numbers come from. nky0=16 means you're tracking sine waves with wavelengths from the box size (5 cm) down to about 5 mm. Plenty for the physics.

02Periodic boundaries

The flux tube is assumed periodic in y. Whatever wiggle exits the right side re-enters from the left. This is a physics assumption: the turbulence is statistically the same everywhere along y, so a small periodic box represents the whole flux surface.

Eddies wrapping around the periodic y boundary

Watch the eddies. They drift in y, and when they hit the right edge they appear on the left. Same physics, just relabeled. From the simulation's perspective, the box is infinite in y, tiled with copies of itself — but you only ever store one copy.

03Resolution: how many points do you actually need?

The eddies are ~5 mm. The box is ~5 cm. So you need maybe 10–32 gridpoints in y to resolve them. That's where numbers like nky0 = 16 or nky0 = 32 come from. You're not resolving the whole torus — you're resolving the eddies inside a small box that's representative of the surface.

The full picture

The torus is huge. The flux tube is a tiny representative slice of it. The flux tube is periodic in x and y by physics assumption (the surface is statistically uniform there). It's "periodic with a twist" in z because after one poloidal turn the field line doesn't quite return to where it started.

So GENE's "16 points" doesn't mean 16 points around the whole torus — it means 16 points across a 5 cm-wide periodic box that stands in for the whole surface. Multiply that by the assumption of statistical homogeneity and you get a simulation that's millions of times cheaper than the full thing.

This is also why full-flux-surface or "global" GENE simulations are way more expensive — they drop the periodic-box assumption and resolve the entire surface, which means orders of magnitude more gridpoints. Most everyday GENE work is flux-tube simulation precisely because of this trick.
