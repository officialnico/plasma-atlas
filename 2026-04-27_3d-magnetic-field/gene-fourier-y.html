Why Fourier in y?

The three directions look different to a translation — only y is symmetric

A Fourier transform is the natural representation when the physics is invariant under translation. Plane waves exp(i k·r) are eigenstates of d/dr only when the medium itself doesn't depend on r. So the question for each coordinate is simple: walk a step in this direction. Did anything change?

Walking a step in each direction — does the background change?
x · radial
→ background changes. NOT Fourier.
y · binormal
→ identical. FOURIER ✓
z · along B
→ |B|, curvature vary. NOT Fourier.

Walk in x and the temperature drops, the density drops — the gradient is the direction. Walk in z and the magnetic geometry changes: |B| varies, the curvature flips between good and bad. Walk in y and... nothing. Same plasma, same field strength, same gradients. The flux surface is symmetric around its perpendicular contour.

Linear eigenmodes — do different k modes couple?

Because y is the symmetry direction, different k_y modes evolve independently — solve each one in parallel, no coupling. This is the computational jackpot: a 3D problem becomes a stack of 2D problems indexed by k_y.

In x, modes look superficially similar but they're not independent — the inhomogeneous background mixes them together. GENE still uses pseudo-spectral methods in x for taking derivatives, but the modes don't decouple, so it's not the same kind of win.

The GENE recipe

y → Fourier. Symmetric direction, modes decouple, embarrassingly parallel.

x → real space (with pseudo-spectral derivatives). Inhomogeneous, modes don't decouple, but the radial domain is small in flux-tube simulations.

z → real space along the field line. Geometry varies poloidally so a single k_z is meaningless; you need the full z-profile to capture how |B| and curvature change.
