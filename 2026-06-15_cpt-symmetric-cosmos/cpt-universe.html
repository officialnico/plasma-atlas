00The framing

Two ways to face a mystery

When physicists hit something they can't explain — like why the early universe looks so smooth and orderly — there are two instincts. One is to add: a new field, a new particle, an extra dimension, a whole multiverse. The other is to subtract: assume the rules you already trust are the whole story, and see how far they stretch.

The mainstream took the first road (you'll meet it as inflation). Turok and his collaborator Latham Boyle took the second. Their wager is that if you take a symmetry physicists already consider sacred and apply it to the entire universe — including the Big Bang itself — almost everything else falls into place for free. This page builds that idea from the ground up.

The throughline to hold onto: every step below is an exercise in not inventing new physics. Whenever the theory needs something, it tries to reach for something we already know exists.
01Ground floor

Five things to have in your pocket

If you've met all of these already, this is just a quick tune-up so we're using the same words. Skim it.

Antimatter

Every particle has an opposite twin

For the electron there's the positron — same mass, opposite charge. Matter and antimatter are mirror partners; when they meet they annihilate into pure energy. Hold this image: opposite, not different.

The Standard Model

Our catalogue of fundamental pieces

A tidy table of the basic matter particles (quarks, electrons, neutrinos…) and the forces between them. It's extraordinarily well-tested. Turok's whole stance is: maybe this catalogue is nearly complete.

Neutrinos

The ghostly, almost-massless ones

Neutrinos barely interact — trillions pass through you each second. Here's the catch we'll use: neutrinos seem to come in only a "left-handed" version. Their natural mirror partner, the right-handed neutrino, has never been seen. That gap matters later.

Gravity

Not a force — a curve in spacetime

Einstein's picture: mass and energy bend the fabric of space and time, and things "fall" by following that curve. Beautiful for big stuff; troublesome when you try to make it quantum, as we'll see at the end.

The Big Bang

Space has been expanding for ~13.8 billion years

Rewind the expansion and everything piles into a hot, dense point — the apparent "start". Run it forward and you get galaxies and the faint afterglow we still detect today. The nature of that starting point is exactly what's in dispute.

02The three mirrors

C, P and T — and why all three at once is special

Physicists love asking: "If I flip the universe in some way, do the laws of physics still hold?" There are three classic flips. Think of each as a kind of mirror.

C — charge conjugation

Swap every particle for its antiparticle. Matter ↔ antimatter. + becomes −.

P — parity

Mirror-flip space, like looking at the world in a mirror. Left becomes right; a clockwise spin becomes counter-clockwise.

T — time reversal

Run the movie backwards. Every motion reverses direction.

Here's the surprise nature handed us last century: applied individually, these mirrors are not perfect — the laws of physics actually do change a little under C, under P, and even under the pair C-and-P together. But there's a deep theorem that says if you do all three together — CPT — the laws are exactly, perfectly unchanged. No exception has ever been found. It's about as close to sacred as physics gets.

03The mainstream answer

Inflation — and why Turok distrusts it

Two things about the early universe cried out for explanation. It's remarkably smooth (distant regions that never touched have the same temperature), and it's remarkably flat (its geometry is finely balanced). The leading fix, from the 1980s, is inflation: a sliver of a second after the Big Bang, space supposedly underwent a burst of runaway expansion, stretching any wrinkles smooth and flat.

It works well — and Turok's objection isn't that it fails to fit the data. It's that it fits too easily:

To make inflation happen you must invent a brand-new field (the "inflaton") and hand-tune its properties. Different choices give different predictions, so the framework can be dialled to match almost any observation — which makes it hard to ever prove wrong. It also tends to spawn an unobservable multiverse, and it predicts a specific hum of primordial gravitational waves that, so far, telescopes have not found.

For someone betting on simplicity, that's a lot of new machinery doing a lot of adjustable work. Turok wanted to explain the same smoothness and flatness without any of it.

04The idea itself

The CPT-symmetric universe

Here's the move. Remember CPT is the one mirror nature respects perfectly. So Boyle and Turok asked a daring question: what if the universe as a whole is its own CPT mirror image?

In that case the Big Bang stops being a wall where everything begins. Instead it becomes the mirror surface itself — a reflection point. On our side sits the universe we live in: matter, time flowing forward. On the far side sits its perfect CPT reflection: an antimatter twin with time running the opposite way.

This one idea quietly solves several problems at once:

The "where did the antimatter go?" puzzle dissolves

A long-standing mystery is why our universe is almost all matter, with barely any antimatter — when the Big Bang should have made equal amounts. In this picture you don't have to destroy the antimatter or explain an imbalance. The matching antimatter is simply on the other side of the mirror. Across the whole two-sided universe, the books balance perfectly.

The infinite "singularity" gets tamed

Normally the Big Bang is a singularity: a point of infinite density where the equations break down. But a mirror needs a clean surface to reflect across. Turok uses a hidden symmetry of the equations (a scaling or "conformal" symmetry — roughly, the laws don't care about overall size right at that point) to smooth the singularity into a well-behaved boundary you can actually reflect through. The "edge" becomes a seam, not a cliff.

Smoothness without inflation

And the original motivation — explaining why the universe is so smooth — comes out of the symmetry and ordinary thermodynamics, with no inflaton field and no tuning required. The thing inflation was invented to fix is handled by the structure itself.

05The payoff

What you get for free

A good theory shouldn't just tidy up old problems — it should make new, checkable claims. This one does, and the headline is a genuine bargain.

  • dark matterThe missing right-handed neutrino is the dark matter. Remember the never-seen mirror partner of the neutrino? The symmetry of the model requires these particles to exist. One of them turns out to be heavy and stable — which is exactly the recipe for dark matter, the invisible stuff holding galaxies together. No new mystery particle invented; the gap in the existing table fills itself.
  • a clean testIt predicts essentially no primordial gravitational waves. This is where it parts ways with inflation, which expects a detectable hum. So far telescopes haven't found that hum — consistent with Turok, and a real way the two pictures could be told apart by future experiments.
  • economyVery few free numbers. Turok argues the whole cosmos can be pinned down by a small handful of constants — no multiverse, no extra dimensions, no string-theory landscape of possibilities.
06The hard frontier

The unfinished part: making gravity quantum

To carry this all the way down to the Big Bang, you eventually need a quantum theory of gravity — and that's where physics has been stuck for decades. Einstein's gravity and quantum mechanics, forced together naively, produce nonsense infinities. (The technical word is that gravity is non-renormalizable: the infinities can't be tidied away.) This is Turok's most recent work, with his student Sam Bateman.

Curvature-squared gravity

There's an old, tempting fix. Einstein's equations involve the curvature of spacetime. If you write a version where the curvature appears squared — curvature-squared (or "quadratic") gravity — something lovely happens: the infinities tame themselves. The theory becomes renormalizable and well-behaved at tiny scales, all while staying in our familiar four dimensions. No strings, no extra dimensions needed.

So why isn't this the textbook answer already? Because it comes with what was long considered a fatal disease — and to see it, we need one piece of quantum mechanics.

A 60-second crash course: states, Hilbert space, and "norms"

In quantum mechanics, the state of a system is written as an arrow (a vector) living in an abstract space of all possible states. That space is called a Hilbert space — think of it as the official arena where well-behaved quantum states are allowed to live.

Every such arrow has a norm — essentially its length, squared. And here's the crucial rule: that number is a probability. Probabilities must be positive. A length-squared that comes out negative is gibberish — like being told an event has a −30% chance of happening.

The ghost problem — and Turok's twist

Curvature-squared gravity's flaw is exactly this: it generates negative-norm states, nicknamed "ghosts." For decades that was treated as proof the theory must be wrong, and physicists abandoned it.

Turok and Bateman's gambit is to stop running from the ghosts. Instead of declaring them fatal, they embrace and reinterpret them, using a more general mathematical arena — a Krein space — that's built to handle these indefinite, possibly-negative lengths in a systematic way. Their deeper claim is that the assumption forcing us into an ordinary Hilbert space may have been too restrictive all along. Loosen it carefully, and the "fatal" ghosts might be features, not bugs — the price of admission for a finite, four-dimensional quantum gravity.

This part is genuinely unfinished and contested — it's a live research bet, not a settled result. But it's the same instinct as everything before it: don't add new physics to escape the problem; look harder at an assumption everyone took for granted.
07Tying it together

Turok's conclusion

"Maybe the universe isn't complicated and we're missing pieces. Maybe it's simple, and we're adding pieces we don't need."

Step back and the whole program is one consistent move, repeated. The universe is its own CPT mirror, so the Big Bang is a reflection rather than a beginning. That single symmetry pays for the smoothness inflation was invented to explain, balances the matter–antimatter books by putting the antimatter across the mirror, and demands a right-handed neutrino that conveniently is the dark matter. The remaining frontier — quantum gravity — he attacks the same way: not by adding strings or dimensions, but by reviving curvature-squared gravity and daring to keep the "ghosts" everyone else threw away.

The unifying belief underneath all of it: the laws of nature are simpler and more economical than we fear, we may already hold most of the pieces, and the real work is fitting them together rather than inventing more.

One honest caveat to carry out the door: this is a minority view. Most of the field still works within inflation, string theory, or other programs. Turok's universe-in-a-mirror is best understood as a bold, unusually testable bet against the grain — not the consensus.
