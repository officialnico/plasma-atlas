Step 00 · The setup

What are we even looking at?

Working through the Boltzmann equation:

$$n_e(r) = n_0 \cdot e^{\,e\phi(r)/k_B T}$$

And honestly, every symbol in there is doing something subtly different from what you might guess at first glance. Across this walkthrough we'll lock down each piece.

The setup is always the same: one positive ion is sitting somewhere, and a sea of free electrons is responding to it. The formula tells you how the electrons distribute themselves around the ion.

The basic picture orange = ion · teal = electrons

The orange dot in the middle is the ion. The teal dots are electrons. They're not stuck in place — they jitter around, but they statistically pile up near the ion because of attraction. The Boltzmann formula tells you exactly how much they pile up.

Two different "e"s — annoying but real

Inside the exponential, you'll see two different things called "$e$":

  • $e$ as a base (the outer one, like in $e^x$): Euler's number, ≈ 2.718. A pure math constant.
  • $e$ as a charge (in the exponent, multiplied by $\phi$): the elementary charge, ≈ 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ coulombs. The charge of one proton (and the magnitude of an electron's charge).

Same letter, totally different things. Context tells you which.

Step 02 · Geometry

Where is the ion? What's at r?

This is the confusion you flagged earlier. Let's nail it.

  • The ion sits at the origin (r = 0). It's a single fixed positive charge. The "source."
  • $r$ is some distance away from the ion. A point in space where you want to evaluate things.
  • $\phi(r)$ is the electric potential at distance r — at your destination, NOT at the origin.
  • $n_e(r)$ is the electron density at distance r — also at your destination.

So when you "walk to distance r," everything in the formula refers to where you ended up. The ion stays put at the origin. You moved.

Step 03 · The peak

Why is density highest at the ion?

Reasonable confusion: "the ion is positive, so why are electrons piling up there?"

Because opposite charges attract. Electrons are negative, the ion is positive — so the ion pulls them in. The closer you get, the stronger the pull. So electrons cluster near the ion.

The Boltzmann formula confirms this: at small r, $\phi$ is large, the exponent is large, so $n_e$ is much bigger than baseline.

subtlety

The formula technically predicts density → infinity as r → 0. That's not physical — real ions have finite size, and quantum mechanics + electron-electron repulsion prevent the collapse. The formula is reliable for r more than a few atomic radii out, which is the regime we care about.

Step 04 · The drop-off

$\phi$ shrinks with r — so density does too

You correctly noticed: density decreases as you walk away from the ion. The orange theory curve drops off. That's right.

Why? Because $\phi(r)$ itself decreases with r. The ion's potential is largest right next to it and falls off as you move away:

$$\phi(r) = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r}$$

That $1/r$ in the denominator means: bigger r → smaller $\phi$.

Since $\phi$ is what's in the exponent of Boltzmann, and $\phi$ shrinks with r, the whole exponent shrinks with r, and so does density.

Two curves, side by side $\phi(r)$ shrinking, density shrinking

Top: $\phi(r)$ — drops as you move out. Bottom: $n_e(r)$ — drops too, more steeply (because exponential of a decreasing thing decreases faster).

Step 05 · The ratio

The dimensionless ratio $e\phi/k_BT$

Inside the exponential lives this thing: $\dfrac{e\phi(r)}{k_B T}$.

It's a ratio of two energies:

  • Top: $e\phi(r)$ — the electric potential energy of an electron at distance r (in joules)
  • Bottom: $k_B T$ — the typical thermal energy (also in joules)

Joules ÷ joules = pure number. No units. That's what "dimensionless" means.

The number tells you which energy is bigger:

  • Ratio > 1: electric pull stronger than thermal jitter (deep inside, attraction wins)
  • Ratio < 1: thermal jitter stronger than pull (far out, electrons ignore the ion)
  • Ratio = 1: they're equal — this is the crossover

Orange = $e\phi(r)$. Teal = $k_B T$ (a flat line). Where they cross is where the ratio = 1. That crossover distance is roughly the screening scale — the famous Debye length in slightly disguised form.

Step 06 · Probability

It's probabilistic — particles vs density

The Boltzmann formula doesn't track individual electrons. It tells you the average density at each location — which is the same thing as the probability of finding any given electron there.

For one electron jittering around for a long time, it spends more time near the ion (where density is "high"). For many electrons in one snapshot, more of them happen to be near the ion. Two views, same math.

Step 07 · Many ions

What about multiple ions?

Boltzmann doesn't care how many ions there are. It only knows about $\phi$. The trick is: $\phi$ is built by adding up contributions from every ion.

$$\phi(\vec{r}) = \sum_{\text{all ions}} \frac{q_i}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 |\vec{r} - \vec{r}_i|}$$

Each ion creates its own potential hill. The total potential at any point is just all the hills added together. Then you plug that total into Boltzmann, and you get electron density at that point.

Step 08 · Putting it all together

The full picture

Here's the whole pipeline, in three arrows:

ions you place  →  potential $\phi(\vec{r})$  →  density $n_e(\vec{r})$

Step 1 is geometry: you decide where the ions are.

Step 2 is electromagnetism: superposition gives you the total potential at every point.

Step 3 is statistical mechanics: Boltzmann turns potential into density.

The Boltzmann formula handles only Step 3. It's blind to ions. It just sees $\phi$ as input and outputs $n_e$.

The full equation, decoded one last time

$$n_e(r) = n_0 \cdot e^{\,e\phi(r)/k_B T}$$

"The electron density at distance r equals the baseline density, multiplied by an exponential whose exponent is the electric potential energy at that location, divided by thermal energy."

Sanity checks

  • Far away ($r \to \infty$): $\phi \to 0$, exponent $\to 0$, $e^0 = 1$, density → baseline. ✓
  • Close to ion ($r \to 0$): $\phi$ huge, exponent huge, density huge. ✓
  • Hot plasma (big $k_BT$): exponent shrinks, density barely deviates from baseline. Thermal motion wins. ✓
  • Cold plasma (small $k_BT$): exponent blows up, density spikes massively near ion. Attraction wins. ✓
you've got it

If those four checks all make sense to you, you fully understand the Boltzmann distribution as it applies to plasma screening. Everything else is bookkeeping.
