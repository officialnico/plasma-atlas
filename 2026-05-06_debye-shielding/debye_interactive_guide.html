Step 01The electric force

Two charges push or pull each other. The strength of the push/pull is given by Coulomb's law:

$$F = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \cdot \frac{q_1 q_2}{r^2}$$

The further apart they are, the weaker the force. The key fact: it falls off as $1/r^2$ — fast, but never to zero. In principle, a charge has some pull on every other charge in the universe.

Same-sign charges repel, opposite signs attract. The signs are just labels (Benjamin Franklin's, from the 1750s) — the math handles the direction automatically when you multiply $q_1 q_2$.

Step 02From force to field

The force on a test charge depends on which test charge you used. To describe the location itself — independent of any test particle — we define the electric field:

$$\vec{E} = \frac{\vec{F}}{q_{\text{test}}}$$

It's force per unit charge. The field is a property of empty space near a source charge. Once you know $\vec{E}$ at a point, the force on any charge $q'$ placed there is $\vec{F} = q' \vec{E}$.

Visually, we draw fields as arrows. Around a positive charge, arrows point outward (it would push another positive charge outward). Around a negative charge, arrows point inward.

Step 03Potential energy

Lift a ball, you store gravitational PE: $U = mgh$. Push two same-sign charges together against their repulsion, you store electric PE.

For two point charges:

$$U(r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \cdot \frac{q_1 q_2}{r}$$

Notice this falls off as $1/r$, not $1/r^2$. That's because energy is the integral of force over distance — integrating $1/r^2$ gives $-1/r$.

sign reading

Same signs: $U > 0$. High when close — they want to fly apart to lower their energy.

Opposite signs: $U < 0$. Most negative when close — they want to stay together (low PE = good).

Step 04Electric potential — the hill

Same trick as before: divide out the test charge. Define electric potential:

$$\phi = \frac{U}{q_{\text{test}}}$$

For a single source charge $q$ at distance $r$:

$$\phi(r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \cdot \frac{q}{r}$$

Units: volts. Yes, the same volts as in batteries.

The hiking-map picture

Think of $\phi$ as elevation on a topographic map. A positive charge creates a hill — high $\phi$ near it, sloping down to zero far away. A negative charge creates a valley.

The map doesn't care who walks on it. To find the energy of a charge $q'$ at any spot, just multiply: $U = q' \phi$.

Potential as a 3D hill drag to rotate

Rotate the hill. The peak is the source ion. Height = potential. The "ground level" far away is φ = 0. Notice it never quite reaches zero — there's always a tiny slope, however far you go.

Step 05Two charges feel opposite landscapes

Here's the clever consequence. Both kinds of test particles see the same $\phi$ map — but their potential energy is $U = q'\phi$, which has a different sign depending on whether $q'$ is positive or negative.

  • Positive test charge near a positive ion: $U = (+)(\text{high }\phi) = $ high PE. It rolls down the hill, away from the ion.
  • Electron (negative) near the same positive ion: $U = (-)(\text{high }\phi) = $ very negative PE. The hill is a valley for it. It rolls into the ion.

Same landscape, flipped upside down depending on your sign.

Step 06A plasma is a soup

A plasma is an ionized gas — a soup of free positive ions and free electrons jiggling around. Examples: the sun, neon signs, fusion reactor cores, lightning.

Two key features:

  • The charges are free to move (unlike in a solid or neutral gas).
  • They have thermal energy ~$k_B T$ per particle, where $k_B$ is Boltzmann's constant.

Globally, the plasma is neutral — equal numbers of + and −. But locally, that balance can shift, and that's where screening comes from.

Step 07Thermal motion vs. attraction

If electrons feel attraction to the test ion, why don't they pile up perfectly and cancel its charge? Because they're hot.

Each electron has thermal kinetic energy ~$k_B T$ trying to fling it away. Its potential energy from the ion is $U = -e\phi$ (negative — favorable). The competition:

  • Close to the ion: $|U| \gg k_B T$. Attraction wins. Electron sticks around.
  • Far from the ion: $|U| \ll k_B T$. Thermal motion wins. Electron ignores the ion.

There's a crossover distance where the two energies are roughly equal. That distance — where attraction stops winning — is the Debye length $\lambda_D$.

Step 08The Boltzmann distribution

To make this quantitative, use a result from thermal physics. Particles in thermal equilibrium follow:

$$n(U) = n_0 \, e^{-U/k_B T}$$

It says: places with higher PE have exponentially fewer particles. Same formula governs air molecules in gravity (denser at sea level, thinner at altitude).

For electrons near a positive test ion, $U = -e\phi$, so:

$$n_e(r) = n_0 \, e^{+e\phi(r)/k_B T}$$

Higher $\phi$ → exponent positive → more electrons. Far away, $\phi \to 0$ and density returns to baseline $n_0$.

Putting it all together

Combine the Boltzmann densities with Poisson's equation from E&M:

$$\nabla^2 \phi = -\frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}$$

(charge density sets the curvature of the potential). Linearize for $|e\phi| \ll k_B T$, do the algebra, and out pops:

$$\nabla^2 \phi = \frac{\phi}{\lambda_D^2}, \quad \lambda_D = \sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon_0 k_B T}{n_0 e^2}}$$

The length scale $\lambda_D$ emerged naturally — it's the only combination of the constants in the problem with units of length.

Step 09The screened potential

Solving that equation gives the Debye-Hückel potential:

$$\phi(r) = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r} \, e^{-r/\lambda_D}$$

It's the bare Coulomb $1/r$ potential, multiplied by an exponential damping factor. The exponential is what cuts off the ion's reach.

Step 10What sets the Debye length

$$\lambda_D = \sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon_0 k_B T}{n_0 e^2}}$$

Physical reading:

  • Hotter plasma → bigger $\lambda_D$. Thermal motion fights clustering harder, so screening reaches further before working.
  • Denser plasma → smaller $\lambda_D$. More electrons available, so they finish screening in less space.

Real numbers

Plug in real values and you get wildly different scales depending on the system:

  • Saltwater (electrolyte): $\lambda_D \approx 1$ nanometer
  • Fluorescent bulb plasma: $\lambda_D \approx 10$ micrometers
  • Earth's ionosphere: $\lambda_D \approx 1$ millimeter
  • Solar wind: $\lambda_D \approx 10$ meters
  • Interstellar medium: $\lambda_D \approx 10$ meters
why "plasma physics"

Screening happens anywhere with mobile charges — electrolytes, metals, semiconductors, biological fluids. The reason it's called the plasma length is sociological: being much larger than $\lambda_D$ is part of the definition of a plasma. If your charged-particle system is smaller than one Debye length, it's not a plasma — it's just some ions.
