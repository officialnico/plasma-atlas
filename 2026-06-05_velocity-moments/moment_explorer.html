velocity-space moments · a working intuition

What it means for the moment count to climb as collisionality falls

A gyrokinetic plasma is described by a distribution of particle velocities. You can store that distribution as a stack of moments — and how many you need is set by a tug-of-war between phase mixing and collisions. This page builds the idea from scratch, ends at the result that decides whether a Hermite–Laguerre code is fast, and explains why you can measure it in a tokamak. Everything here is live; the headline plot solves the real model.

01 · the object

A distribution, stored as moments

At each point in space the plasma has a velocity distribution f(v) — how many particles move at each speed. It’s usually a bump (a Maxwellian) centred on the mean flow. A moment of that distribution is just a weighted average of the bump, and the first few are quantities you already know:

g₀ = ∫ f dv  →  density  (how many particles)
g₁ = ∫ v·f dv  →  flow  (average velocity)
g₂ = ∫ v²·f dv  →  temperature  (the spread)
g₃ = ∫ v³·f dv  →  heat flux  (lopsidedness)

The pattern: the nth moment weights the curve by vⁿ before integrating. Low moments capture the gross shape (where it sits, how wide); higher moments pick up finer and finer wrinkles. Crucially, the full list of moments contains the same information as the curve itself — so a code can store the coefficients instead of the curve. It’s the velocity-space analogue of a frequency spectrum: a smooth tone is a few low frequencies; a sharp click needs many high ones. A smooth Maxwellian needs a handful of moments; a distribution with sharp velocity structure needs many. “Moment count” = how far up the ladder you keep before truncating — and that number sets the code’s speed.

⚠ two different things both called “moment”

This word does two unrelated jobs in gyrokinetics — easy to collide:

magnetic moment μ

A property of one particle: μ = ½mv⊥²/B, its perpendicular gyration energy. It’s a velocity-space coordinate — a physics quantity about magnetism.

moment of f

A coefficient describing the whole distribution’s shape (the list above). Nothing to do with magnetism. This is the “moment count” one.

They’re false friends. The only link is bookkeeping: when we expand f, the perpendicular direction happens to use polynomials in the variable μ (next section). So μ is an axis; the moments are coefficients along it.

Smooth distribution → a few moments nail it. Sharpen the feature and the spectrum extends to high moment number, and you need far more terms to keep the error down. That “number you need” is the whole game.

02 · two directions, two bases

Hermite and Laguerre: the same trick, in the two directions of velocity space

Here’s the piece that makes Laguerre click. After gyrokinetics averages away the fast spiral around the field line, a particle’s velocity needs two coordinates, and they have different shapes:

v∥ — motion along the field. Runs from −∞ to +∞. Its equilibrium shape is a Gaussian (a bell curve), because thermal motion along the field goes both ways.

μ — perpendicular gyration energy. Runs from 0 to +∞ (you can’t have negative energy). Its equilibrium shape is a decaying exponential, not a bell curve.

So velocity space is a half-plane: a two-sided bell-curve axis crossed with a one-sided exponential axis. That asymmetry is the whole reason you don’t use one polynomial family for both.

matched basis = pick the polynomial family whose built-in weight already has the right shape
v∥ is Gaussian → Hermite (weight e−v²)  ·  a Maxwellian in v∥ = 1 Hermite term
μ is exponential → Laguerre (weight e−x on [0,∞))  ·  a Maxwellian in μ = 1 Laguerre term

Laguerre isn’t exotic — it’s the identical idea to Hermite (expand in the family matched to the equilibrium), just for the half-line/exponential direction instead of the whole-line/Gaussian one. Hermite : v∥  ::  Laguerre : μ. You build the full distribution from a grid of products — one Hermite piece × one Laguerre piece — so the velocity cost is L × M coefficients (L up the Hermite ladder, M up the Laguerre ladder).

And why it matters here: trapping lives in μ. Whether a particle is caught in a magnetic well depends on its pitch — μ relative to the field strength — so a trapped-particle feature is a sharp feature in the μ direction. A sharp μ feature needs many Laguerre terms, exactly as a sharp v∥ feature needs many Hermite terms. The asymmetry that makes Laguerre the worry: the Hermite (v∥) direction gets smeared by turbulent stirring, but nothing rescues the Laguerre (μ) direction, and in the core collisions are too weak to help.

03 · the source of structure

Phase mixing manufactures fine velocity structure — for free

Particles with different velocities stream along the field at different speeds. A smooth perturbation gets sheared in the position–velocity plane into ever-thinner filaments — the velocity-space analogue of stirring cream into coffee. In moment language, this is a cascade: free energy injected at low moments marches steadily up the ladder. The front reaches moment number n ≈ (k∥ v_th t)² — it never stops on its own.

Left: the perturbation in (parallel position z, velocity v) shearing into stripes. Right: its moment spectrum climbing. Left alone, a spectral code would need unbounded moments — which is exactly why something has to arrest it.

04 · the headline result

Collisions arrest the cascade — and as collisionality drops, the cutoff climbs

Collisions smooth velocity space. They damp moment n at a rate that grows with n, so they preferentially kill the fine structure at the top of the ladder. The cascade pushing energy up and collisions removing it at the top reach a steady balance, with a cutoff moment m_c: above it, the spectrum collapses and you can safely truncate.

m_c  ~  ( k∥·v_th / ν )2/3   —   spectrum decays as n−1/2 below the cutoff  [Zocco–Schekochihin ’11 · Schekochihin+ ’16]

Lower collisionality ν means collisions only bite higher up, so m_c rises — you must keep more moments. This is what “the moment count behaves as collisionality drops” means. 05 · why trapped-electron turbulence is the worst case

Trapped electrons live at low collisionality — the bad corner

The magnetic field strength varies along a field line, forming wells. A particle with too little parallel velocity can’t climb out — it’s trapped, bouncing back and forth. Trapped electrons drive trapped-electron-mode (TEM) turbulence, a dominant transport channel in optimized stellarators.

Two things make TEM the stress test for a moment code. First, collisions de-trap particles, so trapping (and TEM) is strongest at low collisionality — exactly where m_c is already high (§3). Second, the trapped–passing boundary is a sharp feature in velocity space, sitting in the μ / Laguerre direction — extra structure that the smooth basis must resolve, and which collisions are too weak to smear. Low collisionality therefore hits the moment count twice.

06 · the transferable part

Why the collisionality scaling is geometry-independent

The cutoff m_c ~ (k∥v_th/ν)2/3 is built from local quantities: a parallel streaming rate k∥v_th and a collision rate ν. Both exist in any magnetised plasma. The competition between cascade and collisions runs the same way whether the field lines close into a tokamak or wind around a stellarator — it doesn’t know the global shape of the device.

What geometry does change is the supporting cast: the well structure, the drift resonances, the trapped fraction, and therefore the μ / Laguerre increment and some prefactors. Those shift the curve a little; they don’t change its slope in collisionality. So you can measure the dominant effect — how the moment count scales as ν drops — in cheap tokamak geometry, and only the stellarator-specific increment needs the real 3-D field.

07 · the reference case

What a CBC is

The Cyclone Base Case (CBC) is a standardised tokamak plasma — a fixed set of geometry and gradient parameters defined in a multi-code study by Dimits and collaborators in 2000. It’s the “hello world” of gyrokinetics: a simple circular tokamak flux surface, ion-temperature-gradient-driven, that essentially every code runs to check it agrees with the others. Because everyone uses the identical inputs, CBC results compare apples-to-apples — it’s the canonical correctness benchmark.

For the moment-count question, a CBC run with kinetic electrons and a collisionality scan tests the geometry-independent mechanism in the cheapest, most-trusted setting — which is precisely what a CPU Hermite–Laguerre code (GYACOMO) can do, before any stellarator-specific work.

Swap the adiabatic electrons for kinetic ones and add a density/T_e gradient and you can drive TEM in the same trusted geometry — the base case for the collisionality scan.

The one number: as ν falls, m_c climbs as (kv/ν)2/3. If it climbs into the tens, a Hermite–Laguerre code’s speed advantage erodes.

That scaling is local, so the dominant effect is measurable in a CBC with a CPU code. Trapped-electron turbulence sits at low ν and adds a μ/Laguerre feature with no collisional smoothing — the worst corner. The stellarator-specific increment on top is what needs the 3-D geometry.
