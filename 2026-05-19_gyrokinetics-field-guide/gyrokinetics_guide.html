Chapter 01

Kokkos · one source, many silicons

A C++ library that lets the same loop run on a CPU core, a GPU thread, or anything in between.

The setting: you are writing a simulation code. You have a loop. You want it to run fast on a workstation laptop, a many-core CPU node, and on an NVIDIA or AMD GPU, without rewriting it three times.

Kokkos is a C++ template library (originally from Sandia National Labs) that introduces two ideas:

Execution space — where the work happens. A CPU thread pool, a CUDA device, a HIP device. You pick one at compile time.

Memory space — where the data lives. Host RAM, device memory, unified memory. Kokkos manages the placement.

You write a loop using Kokkos::parallel_for, you describe your array with a Kokkos::View, and the same source compiles to OpenMP, CUDA, HIP, or SYCL depending on a flag.

The same parallel_for(N, ...) mapped to different hardware. Serial does one element at a time. OpenMP fans out across ~8 CPU threads. CUDA launches thousands of lightweight GPU threads in blocks. The user's source code is identical.

The C++ skeleton

For concreteness, the kernel that adds two vectors:

Kokkos::View<double*> a("a", N), b("b", N), c("c", N);

Kokkos::parallel_for("axpy", N, KOKKOS_LAMBDA(int i) {
    c(i) = a(i) + b(i);
});

Compile with -DKokkos_ENABLE_CUDA=ON and that lambda becomes a CUDA kernel. Compile with -DKokkos_ENABLE_OPENMP=ON and it becomes an OpenMP parallel region. You did not change a character of the loop body.

Buzzword defused

Performance portability: the property that one source code achieves good (not necessarily peak) performance on multiple hardware architectures. Kokkos is the most widely used C++ tool for it; the alternatives are RAJA (similar idea, also DOE), SYCL (a standard, less mature), and just writing the code three times (very common, very painful).

Chapter 02

Spectral vs grid · two ways to spell a function

When you discretize velocity space, you can store the function at points — or store its expansion coefficients.

A gyrokinetic code evolves a distribution function $f(\mathbf{x}, v_\parallel, \mu, t)$ — the number of particles per unit phase-space volume. The position $\mathbf{x}$ usually gets a grid. The velocity coordinates $(v_\parallel, \mu)$ are where the choice gets interesting.

($v_\parallel$ is the velocity along the magnetic field. $\mu = m v_\perp^2 / 2B$ is the magnetic moment — the perpendicular kinetic energy normalized by the field strength. We won't need more than that.)

The grid (finite-difference / finite-volume) representation

Pick $N$ points in $v_\parallel$, store $f$ at each. Derivatives become differences between neighbors. The data are values of the function.

The spectral representation

Pick $N$ basis functions $\phi_n(v_\parallel)$ — often Hermite polynomials times a Gaussian — and write

$$ f(v_\parallel) = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} c_n \, \phi_n(v_\parallel). $$

Store the coefficients $c_n$ instead of the values. Derivatives become matrix multiplications (often tridiagonal: each Hermite coefficient $c_n$ couples only to $c_{n-1}$ and $c_{n+1}$ under differentiation, which is fast and clean).

Why does anyone care? For smooth functions, a spectral expansion converges faster than any power of $N$. Ten Hermite modes can match a hundred grid points. The catch: sharp features (steep gradients, discontinuities) hurt the spectral basis far more than they hurt the grid.

true function grid reconstruction Hermite spectral reconstruction

For the smooth target, the spectral curve locks onto the truth almost immediately. For the step-like target, the spectral expansion oscillates near the jump (the Gibbs phenomenon) while the grid simply samples the values — slower convergence everywhere but no overshoot.
Why Hermite?

Because plasma distribution functions sit on top of a Maxwellian $e^{-v_\parallel^2/2v_t^2}$, and Hermite polynomials are the natural orthogonal basis with that Gaussian weight. The zeroth coefficient is density, the first is momentum, the second is energy — the moments you care about are literally the leading coefficients of the expansion.

Chapter 03

Eulerian vs semi-Lagrangian · two ways to move it

Once the function is discretized, how do you push it forward in time?

Take the simplest possible problem: pure advection of a profile $f(x,t)$ with velocity $u$,

$$ \partial_t f + u \, \partial_x f = 0. $$

The exact solution is $f(x, t) = f(x - u t, 0)$ — the initial profile slides to the right at speed $u$. Two strategies for computing this on a discrete grid.

Eulerian

Hold the grid still. At each time step, ask: how much "stuff" flowed across each cell boundary? Update the cell average accordingly.

Limited by the CFL condition: information can't cross more than one cell per time step. If the cell size is $\Delta x$ and the velocity is $u$, you need $\Delta t < \Delta x / u$.

Conserves mass exactly if you do the bookkeeping right. Plays badly with very fast characteristics — they force a tiny $\Delta t$ on the whole simulation.

Semi-Lagrangian

For each grid point $x_i$ at time $t + \Delta t$, ask: where did this point come from? Trace the characteristic backward to $x_i - u \Delta t$. Read $f$ there by interpolation. Copy.

Not limited by CFL — you can take huge time steps. The departure point can be far away.

Mass conservation is not automatic; it depends on the interpolation scheme. Diffusion comes in through interpolation error.

Figure 3.1 · Pure advection, two schemesInteractive
exact Eulerian (1st-order upwind) semi-Lagrangian (cubic)
Push the CFL number above 1 and the Eulerian scheme explodes (numerical instability). The semi-Lagrangian scheme keeps marching forward — that is its headline feature.
Why "semi-"?

A fully Lagrangian method follows actual particles wherever they go (no grid). A semi-Lagrangian method follows characteristics backward only for one time step, then snaps the answer back onto a fixed grid. You get the long-time-step advantage of Lagrangian motion and the bookkeeping advantage of a regular grid.

Chapter 04

Landau damping · a wave that dies without collisions

Lev Landau, 1946: a plasma wave can lose energy even when nothing is colliding with anything. The mechanism is purely geometric.

Consider a Maxwellian plasma — electron velocities distributed as a bell curve $f_0(v) \propto e^{-v^2/2v_t^2}$, where $v_t$ is the thermal speed. Launch a small electric wave with frequency $\omega$ and wavenumber $k$. Its phase velocity is $v_\phi = \omega/k$.

The picture. Electrons travelling near the phase velocity surf the wave. Those moving slightly slower than the wave get pushed forward — the wave gives them energy. Those moving slightly faster get pulled back — they give energy to the wave.

For a Maxwellian, $f_0(v)$ is a decreasing function of $|v|$ past the peak. So there are more slow surfers than fast ones at any phase velocity in the bulk. Net effect: the wave loses energy. The result is a damping rate $\gamma$:

$$ \gamma \;\approx\; -\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{8}}\,\frac{\omega_p}{(k\lambda_D)^3}\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2(k\lambda_D)^2} - \frac{3}{2}\right) $$

where $\omega_p$ is the plasma frequency (the natural oscillation rate of the electron fluid) and $\lambda_D$ is the Debye length (the screening distance — beyond this, electric fields are shielded by the plasma's own rearrangement). The product $k\lambda_D$ controls everything: if the wave is long compared to $\lambda_D$, damping is exponentially weak. If short, damping is fast.

Figure 4.1 · Damping rate vs wavelengthInteractive

At small $k\lambda_D$ the wave rings for many periods before fading. At larger $k\lambda_D$ the resonant region overlaps a fatter part of the Maxwellian and damping is dramatic. The "Distribution & resonance" view shows where on $f_0(v)$ the phase velocity lands.
Why this is profound

Landau damping is reversible at the microscopic level — the underlying equation (Vlasov) is time-reversible. Yet the wave demonstrably decays. The "entropy" hides in fine-scale structure (filamentation) of $f$ in velocity space: the distribution develops finer and finer wiggles that average to zero when you compute the macroscopic field. A simulation needs enough velocity resolution to follow these wiggles, which is the entire reason gyrokinetic codes care so much about velocity-space discretization (see Chapter 2).

Chapter 05

Ion-temperature-gradient · ITG growth

The flagship instability of tokamak core plasmas. It is the reason fusion reactors are turbulent.

A tokamak has a hot core and a cool edge. The ion temperature decreases outward — a gradient. A density gradient is usually there too. Define two scale lengths:

$$ L_T = \left|\frac{T_i}{\nabla T_i}\right|, \qquad L_n = \left|\frac{n}{\nabla n}\right|, \qquad \eta_i = \frac{L_n}{L_T}. $$

$L_T$ is the distance over which the temperature falls by a factor of $e$. $L_n$ is the same for density. The ratio $\eta_i$ measures how much steeper the temperature gradient is than the density gradient.

The instability. A small perpendicular perturbation in the plasma causes pressure to redistribute. Because of the temperature gradient, hotter pockets (denser in energy) develop where they shouldn't. Magnetic field curvature in the tokamak does the rest: it drives the pockets apart, amplifying the original perturbation. Growth.

The instability turns on when $\eta_i$ exceeds a threshold $\eta_c$ (typically around 1, depending on geometry). Past threshold, the growth rate $\gamma$ (in units of $v_{ti}/L_n$) rises with $\eta_i$ until other effects (finite Larmor radius, parallel dynamics, magnetic shear) cap it.

The Larmor radius is the radius of the tight helical circles ions trace as they spiral around magnetic field lines. "Finite Larmor radius effects" means: when the perturbation wavelength gets comparable to that circle, ions effectively average the perturbation over their orbit and feel less of it — this damps short-wavelength modes.

Below $\eta_c$ the growth rate is zero — the plasma is stable. Above threshold a peak emerges at a finite perpendicular wavenumber (the most unstable mode), bounded on the small-wavelength side by finite-Larmor-radius cutoff and on the long-wavelength side by the threshold itself.
Why it matters operationally

ITG is the dominant turbulent transport channel in the core of a tokamak. The energy you put into the plasma leaks out through ITG-driven heat flux. Whether a future power plant achieves ignition depends on how steep a temperature profile you can sustain against ITG turbulence — equivalently, how far above $\eta_c$ you can sit before the heat flux runs away. Codes like GENE, GS2, GKW, and CGYRO exist to compute exactly this.

Chapter 06

Rosenbluth–Hinton · the residual that refuses to leave

A 1998 result by Marshall Rosenbluth and Fred Hinton: drop an axisymmetric flow into a tokamak, and only a fraction of it survives forever. The fraction is calculable.

First the players.

Zonal flow. A flow pattern in the plasma that depends only on the minor radius — it goes around the torus in shear bands. It does not radiate any electric field outside itself. Crucially: it is not unstable and it shears apart eddies that would otherwise drive turbulence. Zonal flows are turbulence-suppressing.

GAM (Geodesic Acoustic Mode). The natural oscillation of a zonal-flow-like perturbation. When you put one in, it doesn't just sit there — it oscillates at a frequency $\omega_{\text{GAM}} \sim c_s / R$ (sound speed over major radius) and damps as the oscillation phase-mixes.

Rosenbluth and Hinton asked: after the GAM oscillations die away, how much zonal flow is left?

The answer is the famous residual ratio,

$$ \frac{\phi_{\text{residual}}}{\phi_{\text{initial}}} \;=\; \frac{1}{1 + 1.6 \, q^2 / \sqrt{\varepsilon}}. $$

Here $q$ is the safety factor — how many times a magnetic field line goes the long way around the torus per single short-way loop (typical core values: 1 to 4). And $\varepsilon = r/R$ is the inverse aspect ratio — minor radius over major radius (typical core values: 0.1 to 0.3).

The bigger $q$ and the smaller $\varepsilon$, the smaller the surviving fraction. In the tight-aspect, low-$q$ machines, more of the zonal flow lives; in the conventional aspect, higher-$q$ machines, less of it does.

Why this is a benchmark

Every new gyrokinetic code, on the day it is born, runs the Rosenbluth–Hinton test. You set up the initial flow, you run, you measure the residual, you compare to the formula. If your code agrees, the long-wavelength axisymmetric physics is right. If it doesn't, something fundamental is wrong (wrong neoclassical polarization, wrong trapped-particle response, wrong geometry coefficients). It is the gyrokinetic equivalent of "Hello, world."
