On the Helicity
of a Magnetic Field

A geometric account of the integral $\mathcal{H} = \int_V \mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{B}\,dV$ — what it measures, why it is conserved, and how to evaluate it in practice.

Domain · classical electromagnetism, MHD Prereqs · vector calculus Reading time · ≈ 12 min
— The Object of Study —
$$\mathcal{H} \;=\; \int_{V} \mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{B}\;\, dV$$

A scalar attached to every magnetic configuration. It counts, in a precise sense, how thoroughly the field lines are linked, twisted, and woven through one another.

0. a primer for the curious

What is a vector field, anyway?
And what does it mean for one field to be the curl of another?

If you have not met vector potential before, the equation $\mathbf{B}=\nabla\times\mathbf{A}$ is opaque. This primer makes it visible. By the end you should be able to look at a vector field $\mathbf{A}$ and see, at every point, what magnetic field $\mathbf{B}$ it produces.

1. A vector field is an arrow at every point.

Pick a region of space. At each point in it, attach an arrow. Each arrow has a direction and a length. That collection — billions of arrows, one per point — is a vector field.

The magnetic field $\mathbf{B}$ is one such collection: at every point of the room you're in, there is some $\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{r})$, a tiny arrow pointing in some direction with some magnitude. The vector potential $\mathbf{A}$ is another such collection, living in the same space.

2. Curl: how much is the field swirling?

The curl of a vector field is itself a vector field. At every point, the curl tells you how much the original field is rotating right there — like dropping a microscopic paddlewheel in and seeing how it spins.

The direction of the curl vector is the axis of spin (right-hand rule: curl your fingers along the rotation, thumb points along the curl). The magnitude is the rate of spin.

Two crucial mental images:

  • Uniform field: every arrow identical. A paddlewheel doesn't spin — there's no shear. Curl = 0.
  • Sheared field: arrows on the left point up but stronger on the right than the left, or vice-versa. The paddlewheel spins. Curl ≠ 0.

3. The vector potential: a flow whose swirl is the magnetic field.

Now put the pieces together. The magnetic field is divergence-free: $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}=0$. A theorem of vector calculus says any such field can be written as the curl of some other field. We name that other field $\mathbf{A}$:

$$\mathbf{B} \;=\; \nabla\times\mathbf{A}.$$

Read aloud: "the magnetic field is the curl of the vector potential." Which means: $\mathbf{A}$ is a vector field whose local swirling, at every point, equals the magnetic field there.

A picture worth keeping. Imagine $\mathbf{A}$ as a flow of fluid. Wherever that flow has shear — wherever a tiny paddlewheel dropped in would spin — there is a magnetic field $\mathbf{B}$, pointing along the spin axis, strength equal to the spin rate. $\mathbf{A}$ is the flow; $\mathbf{B}$ is the spin.

4. Why this matters for helicity.

The integrand of helicity is $\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{B}$. Now you can read it: "how much is the flow $\mathbf{A}$ pointing along the swirl axis $\mathbf{B}$ that it itself produces?"

For a straight wire, $\mathbf{A}$ points along the wire (axial) and $\mathbf{B}$ circles around it (azimuthal). They're perpendicular: dot product zero, helicity zero. No twist.

For a twisted flux tube, $\mathbf{A}$ acquires a circling component to match the twist — and now $\mathbf{A}$ has a piece along $\mathbf{B}$. The integrand lights up. That is what helicity is detecting: alignment between a flow and the very swirl it generates.

With that in hand, the rest of the monograph follows. Onward.

i. dramatis personæ

The cast: a field B, its potential A, and a volume V

Before integrating anything, fix in mind what each symbol stands for. The integrand $\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{B}$ is a dot product of two vector fields at every point of space — and that, already, is unusual.

$\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{r})$ — the magnetic field. Divergence-free: $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}=0$. Its integral lines are closed loops; they never end.

$\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{r})$ — the vector potential. Defined by $\mathbf{B}=\nabla\times\mathbf{A}$. It is not unique: replacing $\mathbf{A}\to\mathbf{A}+\nabla\chi$ gives the same $\mathbf{B}$. This ambiguity is called gauge freedom, and it matters for helicity.

$V$ — the volume of integration. Most often a region whose boundary $\partial V$ the field lines do not cross: $\mathbf{B}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}=0$ on $\partial V$. Without this, $\mathcal{H}$ depends on the gauge and loses meaning.

The three pieces are entwined: $\mathbf{B}$ is what we measure; $\mathbf{A}$ is a bookkeeper for $\mathbf{B}$; and $V$ is the stage. The helicity asks, “how much does $\mathbf{A}$ point along $\mathbf{B}$, summed over the whole room?”

ii. reading the integrand

What does $\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{B}\,dV$ mean?

A dot product is a projection. So at each tiny cell of volume $dV$, the quantity $\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{B}\,dV$ measures the component of $\mathbf{A}$ along the direction of $\mathbf{B}$, weighted by the field strength and the size of the cell.

If the field lines are straight and untwisted, $\mathbf{A}$ can be chosen perpendicular to $\mathbf{B}$ everywhere, and the integrand vanishes. The helicity is zero.

If the field lines spiral, $\mathbf{A}$ acquires a component along $\mathbf{B}$ — and that component cannot be transformed away by any gauge choice. The integral picks up a contribution at every twist.

And if two separate flux tubes are linked, like two rings of a chain, each tube's $\mathbf{A}$ wraps around the other, producing a contribution proportional to the linking number. This is the geometric heart of helicity.

iii. derivation

How to actually do the integral

In general the recipe is: pick a gauge for $\mathbf{A}$, parameterise the volume, compute the dot product as a scalar field, and integrate. The skill is in choosing coordinates that make the integrand simple.

  1. Choose a gauge for $\mathbf{A}$.

    $\mathbf{B}$ does not determine $\mathbf{A}$. Fix the freedom by imposing a condition — most often the Coulomb gauge:

    $$\nabla\cdot\mathbf{A} = 0.$$

    Inside a closed volume with $\mathbf{B}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}=0$ on the boundary, this makes $\mathcal{H}$ gauge-invariant.

  2. Solve for $\mathbf{A}$ from $\mathbf{B}=\nabla\times\mathbf{A}$.

    Using the Coulomb gauge, $\mathbf{A}$ satisfies a Poisson equation: $\nabla^2\mathbf{A} = -\mu_0\mathbf{J}$ if currents are present, or directly

    $$\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi}\int \frac{\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{r}')\times(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}')}{|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'|^3}\,d^3r'.$$

    This is the Biot–Savart-like expression for $\mathbf{A}$ given a known $\mathbf{B}$. For symmetric problems you avoid this and read off $\mathbf{A}$ by inspection.

  3. Form the scalar integrand.

    Compute the pointwise dot product. In a coordinate system $(u_1,u_2,u_3)$ with metric factors $h_i$:

    $$\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{B} = A_1 B_1 + A_2 B_2 + A_3 B_3.$$

    All components are taken in the same orthonormal frame.

  4. Express the volume element in chosen coordinates.

    For Cartesian: $dV = dx\,dy\,dz$. For cylindrical $(r,\phi,z)$: $dV = r\,dr\,d\phi\,dz$. For spherical $(r,\theta,\phi)$: $dV = r^2\sin\theta\,dr\,d\theta\,d\phi$.

    $$dV = h_1 h_2 h_3 \, du_1\,du_2\,du_3.$$
  5. Integrate over $V$ with the right limits.
    $$\mathcal{H} = \int\!\!\int\!\!\int (\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{B})\, h_1 h_2 h_3 \, du_1\,du_2\,du_3.$$

    Innermost integral first — typically the one with the simplest limits. Choose the order to defer the messiest dependence.

  6. Interpret.

    The result has units of $[\mathbf{B}]^2\cdot[\text{length}]^4$, or equivalently magnetic-flux-squared. For two linked flux tubes of fluxes $\Phi_1,\Phi_2$ with linking number $L$,

    $$\mathcal{H} = 2L\,\Phi_1\Phi_2.$$

    The integral has become a topological invariant.

iv. worked example

A force-free cylinder, end to end

Consider a uniform cylindrical column of plasma of radius $R$ carrying a force-free magnetic field — the workhorse of solar-physics modelling.

In cylindrical coordinates $(r,\phi,z)$, the field is:

$$\mathbf{B}(r) = B_0\Big[\,J_1(\alpha r)\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}} \;+\; J_0(\alpha r)\,\hat{\mathbf{z}}\,\Big].$$

This is the constant-$\alpha$ or Lundquist field: it satisfies $\nabla\times\mathbf{B} = \alpha\mathbf{B}$. The parameter $\alpha$ controls the twist.

Step 1 — read off $\mathbf{A}$.

Since $\nabla\times\mathbf{B} = \alpha\mathbf{B}$, we have $\mathbf{B} = \nabla\times(\mathbf{B}/\alpha)$. So a convenient choice is:

$$\mathbf{A} = \frac{1}{\alpha}\mathbf{B}.$$

This is the magic of force-free fields — $\mathbf{A}$ is just a rescaling of $\mathbf{B}$.

Step 2 — dot product.

$$\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{B} = \frac{|\mathbf{B}|^2}{\alpha} = \frac{B_0^2}{\alpha}\big[J_0^2(\alpha r) + J_1^2(\alpha r)\big].$$

Step 3 — integrate over the cylinder.

$$\mathcal{H} = \int_0^L\!\!\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^R \frac{B_0^2}{\alpha}\big[J_0^2 + J_1^2\big]\,r\,dr\,d\phi\,dz.$$

The $\phi$ integral gives $2\pi$, the $z$ integral gives $L$:

$$\mathcal{H} = \frac{2\pi L B_0^2}{\alpha}\int_0^R \big[J_0^2(\alpha r) + J_1^2(\alpha r)\big]\,r\,dr.$$

The remaining integral has a closed form using $\int_0^x t\,[J_0^2(t)+J_1^2(t)]\,dt = x^2 J_0^2(x) + x^2 J_1^2(x) - x\,J_0(x)J_1(x) \cdot 0 + \ldots$ — but for our purposes we can simply evaluate it numerically, which the figure on the right does.

v. why we care

Conservation, and the topological soul of H

In ideal magnetohydrodynamics — perfect conductivity, no resistivity — magnetic helicity is conserved exactly:

$$\frac{d\mathcal{H}}{dt} = 0.$$

Even with finite resistivity, helicity decays far more slowly than magnetic energy. This is Taylor's hypothesis: a relaxing plasma sheds energy as quickly as it can, but cannot shed helicity, so it settles into a minimum-energy state at fixed helicity — a linear force-free field.

The conservation has a geometric reason. Helicity counts linkages of field lines, and you cannot unlink two closed curves by smooth deformation. The integral $\int \mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{B}\,dV$ is just the analytic shadow of that topological fact.

Gauss linking integral. For two closed curves $C_1, C_2$ in space, the linking number is

$$L = \frac{1}{4\pi}\oint_{C_1}\!\!\oint_{C_2}\frac{(\mathbf{r}_1-\mathbf{r}_2)\cdot(d\mathbf{r}_1\times d\mathbf{r}_2)}{|\mathbf{r}_1-\mathbf{r}_2|^3}.$$

For two thin flux tubes carrying fluxes $\Phi_1$ and $\Phi_2$, this integer $L$ is precisely what helicity recovers: $\mathcal{H}=2L\,\Phi_1\Phi_2$.

vii. envoi

Coda

Helicity is the rare quantity in physics whose definition looks analytic and whose meaning is geometric. The integral $\int\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{B}\,dV$ sits at the joining of two views: vector calculus on one side, the topology of knots and links on the other. Once you see this, you cannot unsee it.

From here, the natural next steps are: cross-helicity (replace one $\mathbf{B}$ with the velocity field $\mathbf{u}$), relative helicity (for volumes where $\mathbf{B}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}\neq 0$ on the boundary), and the Chern–Simons form, of which helicity is the simplest abelian instance.
