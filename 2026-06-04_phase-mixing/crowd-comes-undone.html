01 — two facts about a runner

Where you are, and how fast you're going

That's all we track about each runner. Their position on the loop, and their speed. Nobody pushes anyone, there are no hills, no finish line — once they're moving, they just keep their speed. We call that free streaming: free of any force.

03 — the taffy rule

It stretches, but never shrinks

Turn on show the taffy ring and watch the little outlined blob on the chart. It tilts and pulls into a long thin shape — but its area never changes. Like a piece of taffy: pull it long and skinny and you still have exactly as much taffy.

That's a real law (physicists name it after a man called Liouville), and it has a practical payoff: no runners are ever created or destroyed. The crowd only rearranges. In the original code, that's why the "is mass conserved?" check passes perfectly.

Nothing is lost. The clump just gets spread so thin you stop noticing it.
04 — the payoff

Stand at the rail and count heads

Forget speeds for a second. Just walk the track and count how many runners are at each spot. At the start they're all piled in one place — a tall spike. As time goes on, that spike flattens out until runners are sprinkled evenly all the way around.

The strip below is exactly that head-count, updating with the animation above. The bump sags and spreads. The runners didn't vanish — they're just hidden in a fan of different speeds now.

05 — the computer's shortcut

Spin a dial instead of moving everyone

Here's the only "trick" worth knowing. A computer could move millions of runners one tiny step at a time — slow and clumsy. Instead it represents the crowd as a row of spinning dials, and fast-forwarding time just means spinning each dial.

The rule for how fast a dial spins is dead simple: a runner's dial spins at a rate set by their speed. The runner standing still (middle dial) never turns. Faster runners spin faster; backward runners spin the other way. That's it.

one dial per speed · faster runner → faster spin
06 — the missing line

Now the code reads like a sentence

The blank in your exercise was just "spin every dial by the right amount." In code:

# spin each speed's dial by (speed × time-step)
phase = np.exp(-1j * kx[:, None] * v[None, :] * dt)
np.exp(-1j * … )= "spin a dial" (this is the dial, in math costume) v= the speed — how fast that dial spins dt= how much time we're fast-forwarding kx= which "wavelength" of the crowd we're spinning (the code handles a whole set at once) [:, None] / [None, :]= a tidy way to pair every wavelength with every speed in one grid

Multiply the crowd by these dials, spin, and you've jumped time forward — exactly matching the slow runner-by-runner version the test compares against. Same answer, far faster.

the whole thing, in one line

A bunched crowd, left to drift, spreads itself thin — and a computer fakes that by spinning dials.

That's free streaming. Everything heavier (the Vlasov equation, Fourier transforms, Liouville's theorem) is just careful names for the runners you just watched.
