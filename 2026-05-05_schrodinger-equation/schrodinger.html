§ 01 / The Wave

A particle is not a point.

Before 1926, physicists drew electrons as little marbles. Erwin Schrödinger, working through a Christmas holiday in the Alps, wrote down something that changed the picture forever — an equation whose solutions were not trajectories, but waves of probability.

The Schrödinger equation does not tell you where a particle is. It tells you the shape of a smeared-out cloud — the wavefunction ψ — whose squared magnitude gives the probability of finding the particle at each point in space. The particle, in some sense, is everywhere it could possibly be, until something measures it.

"I don't like it, and I'm sorry I ever had anything to do with it." — Erwin Schrödinger, on quantum mechanics
The time-dependent equation 1926
$$ i\hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \Psi(x,t) = \hat{H}\, \Psi(x,t) $$

Read aloud: "the rate of change of the wavefunction in time equals the action of the energy operator on it." The left side is how the state evolves; the right side is the rules of the game — the kinetic energy, the potential, the forces in play.

§ 02 / Born's Rule

From wave to probability.

The wavefunction itself is complex-valued and has no direct physical meaning. Max Born proposed the bridge in 1926: square its magnitude, and you get the probability density.

Born's Rule The Probability Density
$$ P(x, t) = |\Psi(x,t)|^2 = \Psi^*(x,t) \, \Psi(x,t) $$

The narrower the packet (smaller σ), the more uncertain its momentum becomes — and the faster it spreads. This is Heisenberg's uncertainty principle, made visible.

§ 03 / Stationary States

Trapped, the wave finds resonance.

Confine an electron to a box, and something extraordinary happens: only certain energies are allowed. The wavefunction must vanish at the walls, and only standing-wave patterns survive — like a violin string clamped at both ends.

Time-independent equation Eigenvalue problem
$$ \hat{H}\, \psi_n = E_n\, \psi_n $$

Each integer n = 1, 2, 3, … selects a discrete energy level Eₙ. There is no n = 0; the particle cannot sit still, even at absolute zero. The minimum motion is called the zero-point energy — a permanent quantum jitter built into the fabric of the universe.

Music gives a clue here. A guitar string vibrates only at certain frequencies — the fundamental and its harmonics. The same mathematics, applied to matter, gave us the periodic table.

§ 04 / Tunneling

The wall is not absolute.

Send a wave packet at a barrier taller than its energy. Classically: it bounces. Quantum-mechanically: a piece of the wavefunction leaks through. The particle can appear on the other side. This is how the sun shines, how transistors switch, how alpha particles escape atomic nuclei.

When E < V₀, the wave decays exponentially inside the barrier. If the barrier is thin enough, a small but nonzero amplitude emerges on the other side — and that amplitude, squared, is the probability of tunneling. Try setting E below V₀ and shrinking the barrier width.

§ 05 / Superposition

A sum of selves.

The Schrödinger equation is linear: if ψ₁ and ψ₂ are both valid solutions, so is any combination αψ₁ + βψ₂. The system is in both states at once, and the squared amplitudes interfere — adding constructively in some places, cancelling in others.

This linearity is the deepest feature of quantum mechanics. It is why electrons diffract, why a photon can pass through two slits as a single coherent event, and why a quantum computer can encode 2ⁿ classical states in just n qubits. Mix two stationary states with different energies, and the probability cloud begins to breathe — sloshing back and forth at a frequency set by their energy difference.

"Anyone who is not shocked by quantum theory has not understood it." — Niels Bohr
