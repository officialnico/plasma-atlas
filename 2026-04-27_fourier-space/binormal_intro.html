Foundations Primer

The Binormal Direction

Why this weird third direction matters — and why GENE cares so much about it

This primer assumes nothing. We'll start with "why does a tokamak have a magnetic field at all" and end with "why GENE uses 24 Fourier modes in this direction." If you've been confused by the word binormal, this is for you.

Part I

Why Tokamaks Need a Magnetic Field

A fusion plasma is unbelievably hot — 100+ million degrees Celsius. Hotter than the core of the Sun. There is no material on Earth that can touch it without instantly vaporizing. So how do you hold it?

You don't touch it. You levitate it with magnetic fields.

This works because plasma is made of charged particles (ions and electrons), and charged particles in a magnetic field spiral around the field lines rather than crossing them freely. They're stuck to the field line, like a bead on a wire — but they can slide along the wire freely.

Animated · Particle stuck to a field line

The particle spirals tightly around the field line. Across the field — basically can't move. Along the field — slides freely.

The key fact This sets up the fundamental asymmetry of a magnetized plasma. There's one direction (along B) where physics is "easy" — particles stream freely. And there are two other directions (across B) where physics is "hard" — particles are tied to the field. Everything else flows from this.

Why a donut?

If field lines are straight and the bottle is a tube, particles just slide out the ends. So you bend the tube into a closed loop — a donut (torus). Now field lines wrap around forever and particles can't escape along them.

You've now solved the easy direction. But the hard directions — across B — still leak. Particles drift slowly perpendicular to B due to several mechanisms (gradient drifts, curvature drifts, turbulence). This perpendicular leakage is what fusion engineering is fundamentally about: the closer you get to confining plasma perpendicular to B, the closer you get to working fusion.

Part II

One Direction Along, Two Across

Now we have the basic geometry of the problem. At any point inside the plasma:

  • Along B (one direction): particles slide freely. This direction is "easy." We don't worry about it much for confinement.
  • Across B (two directions): particles are tied to field lines, but slowly drift. This is where confinement happens or fails.

"Across B" is a 2D plane (perpendicular to B at that point). And here's a question: are these two perpendicular directions equivalent?

In free space, yes — they'd be interchangeable. You could pick any two perpendicular axes in the plane and use them. But in a tokamak, the plasma itself breaks the symmetry: it's hot in the middle and cold at the edge. There's a temperature gradient, and that gradient picks out a special direction. Watch:

Animated · The temperature gradient breaks the symmetry

The plane perpendicular to B at any point isn't isotropic — it has a gradient. The radial direction is the direction of that gradient (outward, toward colder plasma). The binormal direction is the other perpendicular direction — the one along which temperature doesn't change.

This is the entire definition of "binormal" Take the plane perpendicular to B. The temperature gradient picks out one direction in that plane (radial). The binormal is the other one — the perpendicular direction along which temperature is constant.

That's it. It's not some arbitrary mathematical convention — it's the second perpendicular direction, defined by what's not happening (no gradient).

Why these two perpendicular directions behave differently

Even though both are "perpendicular to B," they're physically very different:

Why does the binormal direction look diagonal? Because field lines spiral. They don't just go around the donut the long way — they also wind around the tube's cross-section. That's why "along B" has both toroidal and poloidal components, and why the binormal — being perpendicular to it — also points in a slightly diagonal direction. In a flat 2D cross-section snapshot you can ignore this and treat binormal as roughly "vertical." But in 3D, it's tilted.

Part IV

Why Turbulence Forms — And Why It Has Binormal Structure

Before talking about why GENE cares about binormal specifically, we need to explain why turbulence even exists. Skipping this makes everything else feel arbitrary.

Step 1: the gradient is unstable

You've got hot plasma inside, cold plasma outside, and they're separated by some boundary. This is a wildly unstable configuration. Heat wants to escape outward. The plasma wants to flatten out the gradient. Any tiny perturbation that lets a little hot plasma move outward will be amplified, because it's energetically favorable.

This is analogous to a glass of water sitting on top of a glass of oil: technically stable, but the slightest jiggle and the water (heavier) starts to fall through the oil (lighter). The system reorganizes violently because there's free energy waiting to be released.

Step 2: the instability creates eddies

The way this instability manifests is: hot blobs form, push outward, while cold blobs form, push inward. They don't go straight — they swirl. Because of the magnetic field's effect on charged particle motion, the natural unstable mode is a vortex (a swirling flow). And vortices come with a characteristic size.

Animated · Hot/cold blobs swap radially — and form vortices

Watch how the blobs don't move in straight lines — they curl around each other. That curling is the eddy structure. The eddies are the turbulence.

Step 3: the eddies have a size in the binormal direction

Now here's the connection. An eddy isn't a point — it has size. Across the radial direction, the eddy stretches some amount. Across the binormal direction, it stretches some amount too. In fact, eddies tend to be roughly circular in the perpendicular plane, with diameter set by ion physics (a few times the Larmor radius — millimeters in real tokamaks).

So as you sweep along the binormal direction at fixed radius, you encounter alternating hot/cold patches:

Drag the slider to move along binormal. Watch the bottom panel — the temperature you'd measure as you walk along this direction goes up and down, up and down, in a wave-like pattern. The binormal direction is where the eddy structure shows up as oscillations.

This is why binormal is wave-like Because turbulent eddies are roughly periodic when you sweep across them. The pattern repeats — peak, trough, peak, trough — at a wavelength set by the eddy size. So describing what's happening in the binormal direction with waves (Fourier modes) is exactly the right tool. Different eddy sizes = different wavelengths = different k_y values.
Part V

The Punchline: Binormal Drives Radial Transport

Here's the part you might already half-know. Heat escapes radially — that's the direction of the gradient, the direction toward the wall. So why obsess over binormal?

Because radial transport is driven by binormal variation. Without wiggles in the binormal direction, there's no transport. Let me show you why.

The E×B mechanism

Turbulent eddies are essentially patterns of electric potential φ in the plasma. Some regions have higher φ, some lower. Wherever there's a gradient in φ, you get an electric field E pointing perpendicular to the contours of constant φ.

Now here's the magic of magnetized plasma: when you have an electric field E perpendicular to a magnetic field B, charged particles drift in a direction perpendicular to both. This is called the E×B drift, and it's the dominant transport mechanism in tokamaks.

Animated · How an eddy moves plasma radially via E×B

Watch carefully:

  1. The eddy is a blob of high potential φ. Around it, E points outward (down the gradient).
  2. The B field points out of the screen (along the field line).
  3. E×B (the drift direction) is perpendicular to both — and it goes around the eddy.
  4. On one side of the eddy, this drift carries plasma outward radially. On the other side, it carries plasma inward.
  5. If the outward and inward flows carry plasma at the same temperature, they cancel. But the outward side carries hot plasma and the inward side carries cold plasma. Net heat flow: outward.

Now the key question: where does binormal come in?

The radial component of the E×B drift is determined by how φ varies in the binormal direction. Mathematically:

The math E×B radial component ∝ ∂φ/∂y (the binormal derivative of the potential).

If φ doesn't change in the binormal direction (∂φ/∂y = 0), there's no radial E×B flow, and no transport. The binormal variation of φ is exactly what drives heat outward.

Said differently: an eddy has both a radial size and a binormal size. The fact that it has a binormal size — that you see oscillations as you walk in the binormal direction — is what makes radial transport happen. A perfectly uniform-in-y plasma would have zero turbulent transport, even with a huge radial gradient.

Animated · No binormal variation → no transport

The left panel has eddies (binormal variation): heat escapes. The right panel has stripes (no binormal variation): nothing moves radially.

The whole reason GENE cares Heat loss in fusion = radial transport. Radial transport ∝ binormal variation of φ. So to predict heat loss, you have to accurately resolve binormal variation. This is GENE's central mission.
Part VI

Why Fourier Space for the Binormal

Now, finally, the choice of Fourier space makes sense. We've established:

  1. Binormal is the perpendicular direction without a gradient — the symmetry direction.
  2. Turbulent eddies appear as oscillations in this direction.
  3. The plasma wraps around the donut, so binormal is naturally periodic — go far enough in y, you come back to where you started.
  4. What we care about (radial transport) is determined by binormal derivatives of φ.

These four facts make Fourier space the obvious tool. Here's why each one matters:

Periodicity

Fourier decomposition assumes the function repeats. In binormal, it does (you eventually wrap around the donut). In radial, it doesn't (you'd hit the wall). So Fourier works in binormal but not radial. This alone would be a sufficient reason.

Symmetry

Because nothing distinguishes one binormal location from another, the natural representation is wave-like — patterns that look the same shifted along y. That's exactly what Fourier modes are.

Eddies have characteristic sizes

Each eddy size corresponds to a single binormal wavelength. By going to Fourier space, you decompose the turbulence into a spectrum where each mode is one eddy size. This matches the physics: different size eddies behave differently (driven differently, damped differently). Fourier space lets you treat them separately.

Derivatives become trivial

Remember that radial transport ∝ ∂φ/∂y? In real space, taking that derivative requires comparing neighboring grid points — slow and approximate. In Fourier space, it's a single multiplication: ∂/∂y → multiply by i·k_y. Since you're computing this every timestep at every grid point, the speedup is enormous. And it's exact, not approximate.

Part VII

How GENE Actually Stores It

Putting it all together: in the binormal direction, GENE doesn't store "the value of f at 24 binormal positions." It stores "the amplitude of f's oscillation at 24 different wavelengths." These 24 wavelengths are k_y = 1, 2, ..., 24, where each integer represents one wavelength of binormal oscillation that fits in the simulation box.

Animated · The 24 binormal wavelengths GENE tracks

Each row is one mode. Small k_y = giant eddies (long wavelength). Large k_y = tiny eddies (short wavelength). The actual turbulence is a sum of all 24, each with its own amplitude.

One paragraph summary Plasma is held in a tokamak by spiraling on magnetic field lines. The temperature gradient (hot inside, cold outside) drives instabilities that form turbulent eddies. These eddies live in the plane perpendicular to B. The "radial" direction in this plane is the direction of the gradient (toward the wall). The "binormal" direction is the perpendicular direction along which there's no gradient. Turbulent eddies show up as wave-like oscillations in the binormal direction, and the binormal variation of these waves is precisely what drives heat outward radially. Because binormal is a symmetry direction that wraps around the donut, Fourier decomposition is the natural way to describe what happens in it — and that's why GENE uses k_y as a coordinate instead of y.
