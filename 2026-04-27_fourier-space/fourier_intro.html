Companion Primer

Fourier Space

From wiggly patterns to wavenumbers, from scratch

Part I

Why Think in Waves at All?

When you look at something messy — ocean chop, a jagged audio signal, or a turbulent plasma eddy pattern — your instinct is to describe it point by point: at this position the value is X, at the next position it's Y, and so on. This is called working in real space. It's the natural way to think about the world.

But there's a completely different way to describe the same thing: not by listing values at positions, but by asking "what mixture of simple waves would add up to this pattern?"

This second way is called Fourier space. Instead of positions, you're indexed by wavenumbers — labels for "how wiggly" each wave is. It sounds abstract until you see it.

Animated · The same pattern, described two ways

Top: a messy-looking wave in real space. Bottom: the exact same thing described as "one slow wave plus one medium wave plus one fast wave." Both descriptions are equivalent — they contain identical information. Fourier space isn't a different pattern, it's a different language for talking about the same pattern.

The music analogy A chord on a piano is a pressure wave in the air. You could record the wave point-by-point (real space: "at t=0.001s the air pressure is 1.2, at t=0.002s it's 1.4..."). Or you could say "it's a C + E + G" — three notes, each with a specific pitch and loudness. That's Fourier space. The chord is the same either way. Pitch = wavenumber. Loudness of each note = amplitude of that Fourier mode.
Part II

What a Single Wave Looks Like

Before combining waves, let's get comfortable with one wave. A simple sine wave is described by two things: its wavenumber k (how wiggly it is) and its amplitude (how tall).

A single wave: value(x) = A · sin(k · x)

Change k and the wave wiggles faster or slower. Change A and it gets taller or shorter. That's it.

k: 3 amplitude: 0.6

What does k actually mean?

Wavenumber k counts how many complete wiggles fit into your window. Small k means long, lazy waves (few oscillations across the screen). Large k means short, choppy waves (many oscillations).

WavenumberWavelengthLooks like
k = 1longone gentle hill and valley
k = 5mediumfive wiggles across the screen
k = 20shorttight choppy oscillation

Wavenumber and wavelength are inverse: k ∝ 1/λ. Bigger k = smaller wavelength = smaller spatial structures.

Intuition for plasma In turbulence, "small k" eddies are big swirling structures the size of the plasma itself. "Large k" eddies are tiny, sub-millimeter structures. Each k value you see in the Fourier description is a different size of eddy. That's what the ky axis in GENE tracks.
Part III

Adding Waves to Build Any Pattern

Here's the magic. Any pattern — literally any — can be built by adding up sine waves at different k values. This isn't an approximation. It's a mathematical theorem (Fourier's theorem, 1822).

The waves you add don't interfere destructively in some bad way. They just stack. Where two waves are both "up," the result is extra up. Where one is up and one is down, they partially cancel. The sum is the final pattern.

Interactive · Add waves one at a time to build a square-ish pattern

Watch how adding more and more wavenumbers sharpens the pattern. With just k=1, you get a smooth sine wave. Add k=3 and it starts looking boxier. Keep adding odd k values (5, 7, 9, ...) and you approach a square wave. Each new wavenumber adds finer detail.

The key insight Going to Fourier space means: instead of storing 1000 numbers (one per grid point), store the amplitude of each wavenumber. For smooth patterns, most wavenumbers have tiny amplitudes and can be ignored. You get away with maybe 20–30 numbers instead of 1000. This is a huge compression — and it's why simulations use Fourier space.
Part IV

Reading a Fourier Spectrum

Once you describe a pattern by its wave mixture, you can plot the amplitudes. This plot is called a Fourier spectrum or power spectrum. The x-axis is k (wavenumber), the y-axis is "how much of that wavenumber is present."

Interactive · Click a pattern, see its spectrum

A smooth pattern has a spectrum concentrated at low k. A choppy, detailed pattern has a spectrum that extends to high k. A pattern with one dominant periodic feature has a spike at that feature's k.

How to read the spectrum Tall bar at small k → big, slow structures dominate. Tall bar at large k → fine detail dominates. Spectrum that decays quickly with k → smooth pattern. Spectrum that stays flat for a while → turbulent/rough. Plasma physicists spend a lot of time looking at these.
Part V

Why This Helps GENE

GENE uses Fourier space for one of its spatial directions. But before we can talk about why that helps, we need to talk about which direction — because GENE only uses Fourier space for one of three spatial axes, and the choice isn't arbitrary.

Which direction? The binormal

A tokamak is a donut. At any point inside the plasma there are three physically meaningful directions you can move:

Interactive · The three local directions at a point in the plasma

Radial (x) — points outward from the donut's core (magnetic axis) toward the wall. This is the direction heat is trying to escape.

Along B (z) — points along the magnetic field line at that spot. Field lines spiral helically around the donut. Particles stream freely in this direction.

Binormal (y) — the third perpendicular direction. Defined by elimination: pick the one direction that's perpendicular to both the radial direction and the field line. This is where Fourier space lives.

Why these three? Because the physics respects them. The magnetic field picks "along B" as special — particles stream freely along it, are confined across it. The temperature gradient picks "radial" as special — that's where heat flows. The binormal is what's left over. Together they form a right-handed 3D coordinate system at every point.

Why Fourier in the binormal direction (and not the others)?

The radial and binormal directions are both "perpendicular to B" — but they behave very differently:

Radial (x)Binormal (y)
Gradient?Yes — hot inside, cold outsideNo — looks the same as you move in y
Periodic?No — wall on one side, core on otherYes — symmetry direction
GENE usesReal-space grid (128 points)Fourier grid (24 wavenumbers)

Fourier space requires periodicity. The binormal direction has it (it's a symmetry direction with no preferred location). The radial direction doesn't. So binormal gets Fourier; radial gets a normal grid.

Now — why is Fourier space worth the trouble?

Three big wins. The first is the most important:

1. Derivatives become multiplication

Every timestep, GENE has to compute derivatives of the distribution function in the binormal direction (this is part of how the Vlasov equation evolves f). In real space, computing a derivative means comparing values at neighboring grid points — subtract, divide by spacing, hope for the best. It's slow and introduces numerical error.

In Fourier space, derivatives become trivial:

derivative of sin(kx) = k · cos(kx)
Animated · Real-space derivative (left) vs Fourier (right)

Watch the left panel: to compute the derivative at any point, the green algorithm needs to read several neighboring values, subtract them, and average. Lots of operations per grid point, and rounding errors accumulate. The right panel shows the Fourier approach: every wave's amplitude just gets multiplied by its k. Three modes, three multiplications, done. Exact.

Why this is huge Derivatives happen many times per timestep, at every grid point, for the full simulation duration. Saving a 10× factor here compounds into a massive speedup overall. And being numerically exact (instead of approximate) means GENE can take bigger timesteps without instability.

2. Modes don't mix (in linear problems)

Here's another huge win. In the linear part of the equations, a wave at one k value evolves completely independently of waves at other k values. They don't talk to each other. Whatever happens to k=3 has no effect on k=5.

Animated · Each mode evolves on its own — no coupling

The top three panels show three separate modes (k=2, k=5, k=11) each evolving with its own dynamics — different growth rates, different oscillation rates. The bottom panel sums them. Notice that you could update each mode independently, then add them up at the end, and get the same answer. One coupled problem becomes many small independent ones.

In real space, every grid point depends on its neighbors at every step — they're all coupled together. In Fourier space, that coupling is gone (for the linear part of the physics). This is a different kind of computational win from #1, and it's the basis for parallelization.

3. You can throw away small waves

If a wavenumber has negligible amplitude, just drop it. In real space there's no equivalent — every grid point contributes some value, and you can't easily say "this point doesn't matter." In Fourier space, the "unimportant" parts are obvious: their amplitude bars are tiny. You can truncate the spectrum and lose almost nothing.

The cost Fourier space only works well for periodic directions — where the pattern wraps around. As established above, that's why GENE only uses it in the binormal direction. In the radial direction (which has a gradient and isn't periodic), GENE uses a real-space grid. That's why x is a real coordinate but ky is a Fourier index in your doc's coordinate list.
Part VI

Connecting Back to Your Doc

Now the line from your doc should click:

"ky — perpendicular wavenumber (binormal). This is NOT a position — it's a Fourier mode index for the direction perpendicular to both B and the radial direction. Instead of tracking 'where' in this direction, GENE tracks 'which wavelength.' Each ky value represents a different eddy size."

You've now seen all the pieces of this sentence: the binormal direction (Part V), what a Fourier mode index is (Parts II–IV), and why GENE tracks wavelengths instead of positions in y (the periodicity argument from Part V).

Translated: in the binormal direction, GENE doesn't use a position grid at all. It uses a Fourier grid — 24 different wavelength values. Each represents one "size" of eddy.

Each ky is literally a different size of eddy

Let's make "each ky value represents a different eddy size" concrete. Below is a ladder of modes from ky=1 (giant eddies that barely fit in the box) down to ky=24 (tiny eddies). These are the 24 "slots" GENE has along the ky axis. Turbulence is a mixture of all of them simultaneously:

Animated · The 24 eddy sizes GENE tracks

Each row is a single ky value — a single wavelength — shown as the swirling pattern you'd actually see in the plasma if only that one mode were active. The real turbulence is all of these overlapping at once, each with its own amplitude. When GENE stores f, it stores 24 amplitudes — one per row.

Two ways to store the same information

Here's the storage shift. Suppose you have a wavy pattern along the binormal direction at one fixed position and time. You could store it two ways:

Interactive · Drag to reshape the pattern — watch both representations update

Both panels contain exactly the same information. The top shows "the value at each of 24 positions" — what you'd naturally call a snapshot. The bottom shows "the amplitude of each of 24 waves" — the same snapshot written as a wave-mixture. Either description lets you perfectly reconstruct the other. GENE picks the bottom representation because, as Part V explained, it makes derivatives cheap and modes independent.

Why this trade is fair Information-wise you're not gaining or losing anything — 24 numbers in, 24 numbers out. What you gain is computational convenience. Everything the Vlasov equation does in the binormal direction (especially taking derivatives, which happens every timestep) is faster and more accurate in the wave representation.

Comparing eddy size to the orbit ring

One last idea that'll pay off when you hit the FLR section later. The particle's gyration orbit has a physical size (the Larmor radius ρ, a few mm for ions). Each turbulent mode ky has its own wavelength. So you can ask: how big is this eddy compared to the orbit?

The ratio is kyρ. Small ratio = eddy much bigger than orbit. Large ratio = eddy much smaller than orbit. Below, the orange ring is a single particle's orbit (fixed size). Slide to pick a mode and see how its wavelength compares:

This ratio — how the orbit size compares to the eddy size — turns out to be the single most important number for the FLR (Finite Larmor Radius) effects you'll meet later in doc #1. For now, just notice that the ratio naturally changes as you sweep through ky. That's the bridge between "Fourier grid point" and "physically meaningful size comparison."

One sentence takeaway Fourier space replaces "values at points" with "amplitudes of waves" — same information, radically more convenient for simulating smooth-ish periodic phenomena like plasma turbulence.
