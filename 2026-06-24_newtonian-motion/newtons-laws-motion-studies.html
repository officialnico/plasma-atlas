The Three Laws
of Motion

Each plate below is a strobed exposure: every moving body drops a ghost at a fixed beat of time, the way Marey and Muybridge once photographed motion. Equal spacing means steady speed; widening gaps mean acceleration. Read the laws by reading the gaps — starting with a heavy block coasting through empty space.

I.
Lex Prima Inertia

A body keeps its state of rest, or of uniform motion in a straight line, unless a force is impressed upon it. No force, no change — and the force that hurts you is born only in the collision.

While it coasts, the net force is exactly zero — yet it is far from harmless. Step in front and its motion must change in the width of your body, manufacturing an enormous force out of nothing. Inertia resists the change, not the motion.

II.
Lex Secunda F = m a

The change of motion is proportional to the force impressed. For the same push, the heavier body accelerates less — its gaps open more slowly across the plate.

4.0 m/s²

Same amber force on both. The light puck pulls ahead because acceleration is force divided by mass — triple the mass, a third of the acceleration. This is the same reluctance that swings a heavy particle onto a wider orbit.

III.
Lex Tertia Action & Reaction

To every action there is an equal and opposite reaction. The astronaut pushes the crate exactly as hard as the crate pushes back — but the lighter one flies off faster.

The two amber arrows are always the same length — forces come in equal, opposite pairs. The velocities differ because mass differs: momentum splits so that m·v matches on both sides. Laws two and three, working together.
