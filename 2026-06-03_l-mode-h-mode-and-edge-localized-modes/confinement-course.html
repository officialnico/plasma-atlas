01 · The magnetic bottle

You can't put a star in a box

Fusion needs plasma hotter than 100 million °C. Touch that to any wall and the wall vaporizes and the plasma instantly cools — game over. So a tokamak holds it with magnetic fields instead. Plasma is made of charged particles, and charged particles can't cross magnetic field lines freely — they're forced to spiral along them. Bend the field into closed loops inside a donut, and the particles circle around endlessly, never touching the wall.

Here's the cross-section — a slice through the donut. The nested rings are flux surfaces: shells the plasma sits on. Watch the teal dot ride a closed surface forever (confined), while edge dots that stray past the boundary get swept down to the bottom and out.

Three pieces of vocabulary, all visible above:

Separatrix — the last closed flux surface. Inside it, field lines loop back on themselves and plasma stays trapped. This line is "the edge." Cross it and you're on open field lines that lead out.

Scrape-off layer (SOL) — the thin region of open field lines just outside the separatrix. Anything that leaks across the edge ends up here and gets carried away.

Divertor — the plasma's exhaust system. The field is shaped so those open SOL lines funnel down through the X-point (where the field crosses itself) and slam into two heavily armored target plates at the bottom. The divertor removes exhaust heat and the leftover helium "ash" from fusion. It's the single most heat-stressed component in the whole machine — remember that; it's why the bursts later are dangerous.

Confinement time, τE. Heat still slowly leaks across field lines no matter what. τE is simply "how many seconds the energy stays trapped before leaking out." Bigger τE = better confinement = closer to net fusion energy. Everything from here is a fight to make τE bigger.

02 · L-mode → H-mode

The leak, and how to plug it

The thing draining your heat across the field lines is turbulence — swirling eddies that grab hot plasma from the core and shuffle it outward, eddy by eddy, like stirring milk into coffee. The little churning specks in the diagram below are those eddies; their chaos is the leak made visible.

The plot is pressure vs radius: hot dense core on the left, cool edge on the right. With low heating power you're in L-mode (Low confinement) — the leaky default. Eddies churn freely all the way to the edge, so the pressure just droops smoothly down to nothing. Worse, L-mode leaks faster the harder you push it ("power degradation").

Now raise the power past a threshold and something abrupt happens. A thin shell at the very edge goes quiet: layers of plasma there start sliding past each other at different speeds, and that sheared flow physically rips the eddies apart before they can grow. Turbulence in that shell collapses. That quiet shell is the edge transport barrier — watch the specks near the edge calm down and turn teal.

With the leak sealed at the edge, pressure piles up against the barrier like water behind a dam. That steep dammed-up edge is the pedestal. And because the whole interior sits on top of the pedestal, the entire profile lifts — like raising the foundation of a house raises every floor above it. That lifted, sealed state is H-mode (High confinement). The faint dashed amber line shows where L-mode would have been — the gap is your winnings.

One-liner: L-mode leaks everywhere; H-mode grows a quiet barrier at the edge that dams up a steep pedestal and lifts the whole plasma. H-mode was discovered on the ASDEX tokamak in 1982 and is the planned baseline for ITER.

so what breaks? ↓
04 · The breaking point

Why the pedestal can't grow forever

The pedestal keeps steepening as pressure piles up — but a steep edge stores a lot of strained energy, and eventually it goes unstable. The instability has a two-part name because two different things break at once. Both diagrams below are driven by the live pedestal (it builds and crashes on its own once you're in H-mode).

Ballooning (pressure-driven): in a donut the field is strong on the inner side (facing the hole) and weak on the outer side. The outer side has "bad curvature" — badly shaped for holding pressure in. Push the edge pressure gradient too high and the plasma bulges outward right there, like an inner tube ballooning out where the rubber is thinnest. Watch the outer-side bulge swell as the pedestal builds.

Peeling (current-driven): a steep pressure gradient automatically drives an electric current flowing along the edge (the self-generated "bootstrap current"). Too much edge current makes the outer magnetic layers want to kink and tear loose — peeling off like skin.

Top: the bulge · Bottom: the stability map
peeling–ballooning limit safe (stable) zone pedestal state

The elegant part: a steeper pedestal raises both the pressure gradient (feeds ballooning) and the edge current (feeds peeling) together. So on the stability map, the operating dot marches diagonally up-and-right as the pedestal builds, heading straight for the combined peeling–ballooning boundary — capped on top by ballooning, on the right by peeling. The instant it touches that boundary, the edge snaps. The dot crashes back toward the origin and the whole cycle restarts.

that snap has a name ↓
05 · The bursts

ELMs — the edge exhaling

That snap is an Edge-Localized Mode (ELM). When the pedestal hits the peeling–ballooning limit, the edge relaxes violently and expels a burst of particles and heat in under a millisecond — then rebuilds and does it again. The top trace is the pedestal pressure building toward the red limit and crashing; it's a sawtooth, the plasma's edge periodically exhaling.

Each crash dumps its slug of energy down onto the divertor — that's the lower trace spiking. Remember the divertor is already the most heat-stressed part of the machine. Crank the power (try the ⚡ preset): the biggest ELMs (called Type-I) get more frequent and bigger with power, and the spikes start crossing the material-damage line, lighting the warning. On a reactor a single big ELM can erode or melt the target.

so what do engineers do about it? ↓
06 · The whole picture

The trade-off, and what you now know

ELMs aren't purely bad. Each burst also flushes out impurities and helium ash that would otherwise pile up. A perfectly ELM-free H-mode tends to choke on its own impurities, radiate away its heat, and collapse. So the goal isn't zero ELMs — it's taming them: pacing them into many small, harmless bursts (e.g. firing in tiny fuel pellets), or suppressing the big ones with carefully applied magnetic perturbations. This is a central engineering problem for ITER.

  1. A tokamak traps plasma on closed magnetic flux surfaces; the separatrix is the edge and the divertor at the bottom handles exhaust heat and ash. Success is measured by confinement time τE.
  2. Turbulent eddies drain heat across the field. L-mode lets them churn everywhere — leaky, and worse the harder you push.
  3. Past a power threshold, sheared flow shreds the edge eddies into a quiet transport barrier, damming up a steep pedestal that lifts the whole profile: H-mode, ~2× the confinement.
  4. The pedestal can't grow forever — steepening raises both pressure gradient and edge current until it hits the peeling–ballooning stability limit.
  5. Hitting that limit triggers an ELM: a sub-millisecond burst that crashes the pedestal and blasts the divertor. Bigger and more frequent at higher power.
  6. The real game: keep H-mode's confinement while taming ELMs so the divertor survives.
