A particle is two numbers

At any instant a particle on a line is fully described by two numbers: where it is, x, and how fast it's moving, v. That's it. If you know both right now, the laws of motion tell you both a moment later.

2 stop thinking in time-plots

Phase space: the whole state as one point

Here's the move that unlocks everything. Instead of two separate graphs of x(t) and v(t) against time, plot x on one axis and v on the other. Now the complete state of the particle — both numbers at once — is a single point. This plane is called phase space.

As time runs, that point moves, tracing a curve. The curve is the particle's entire life story: no time axis needed, because the geometry already encodes the dynamics. Below, the same particle lives on the line (top) and as a point in phase space (bottom). Drag it.

3 the simplest real example

A mass on a spring traces a loop

Take the friendliest system in physics: a mass on a spring. Pull it further, it pulls back harder — the force is proportional to the stretch (Hooke's law), \(F=-kx\), where \(k\) is the spring's stiffness and the minus sign means "back toward home." Acceleration is force over mass, so \(a=F/m=-(k/m)\,x\). The combination \(k/m\) controls how fast it oscillates, so we give it a name, \(\omega^2=k/m\), and write \(a=-\omega^2 x\). Feed that into rung 1:

In the time view, x and v are sine waves a quarter-cycle out of step. But watch what that same motion looks like in phase space: the point goes round and round a closed loop — an ellipse. Forever. Press play.

The loop is closed because the spring conserves energy: the particle keeps returning to the same states. That closed loop is your first hint that something is being preserved.

4 the new bookkeeping

The Hamiltonian is just the energy

Now the word that tripped you up. I'm going to build it in three slow steps, because last time I skipped all three.

A Hamiltonian system is not new physics — it's a new starting point. Newton starts from forces. The Hamiltonian approach starts from a single function, written H, that holds the system's total energy (kinetic + potential), expressed using position x and momentum \(p=mv\) (momentum is just mass × velocity — we use \(p\) instead of \(v\), and that choice is the whole trick).

step 1 · build H, from energies you already know A mass on a spring stores energy two ways:
  • Kinetic energy (energy of motion) is the formula you've seen, \(\tfrac12 m v^2\). We want it in terms of momentum. Since \(p=mv\), we have \(v=p/m\), so \(\tfrac12 m v^2=\tfrac12 m\,(p/m)^2=\dfrac{p^2}{2m}\). That's the first term — nothing new, just \(\tfrac12 mv^2\) rewritten.
  • Potential energy (energy stored in the stretch) is \(\tfrac12 k x^2\). Why: the spring pushes with force \(kx\), and the work piled up stretching from \(0\) to \(x\) is the area under that force, \(\tfrac12 k x^2\). Second term.

Add the two and you have the spring's Hamiltonian. It is literally just total energy, written with \(p\) in place of \(v\):

Because \(H\) is the total energy, it's conserved — the number \(H\) never changes as the spring swings. That doesn't mean nothing moves: the energy is endlessly traded between the two terms. At full stretch the mass is stopped, so it's all potential; through the middle it's all kinetic; in between, a mix. The two slosh in anti-step while their sum (which is \(H\)) stays pinned. Here's that equation alive — the bar's total height is locked, only the split slides:

Don't swallow the equations as magic — read each as a plain sentence. (Reminder: \(\partial H/\partial p\) means "differentiate \(H\) with respect to \(p\), treating \(x\) as a frozen constant," and vice-versa.)

  • \(\dot x = \partial H/\partial p\): how fast position changes = how steeply the energy depends on momentum.
  • \(\dot p = -\partial H/\partial x\): how fast momentum changes = minus how steeply the energy depends on position. You may already know the rule force = −(slope of potential energy) — this is that rule, since only the potential part of \(H\) depends on \(x\). The minus sign is just "systems accelerate downhill in energy."

Where do these two come from, deep down? In a full course they fall out of the principle of least action. For us the honest test is the one we're about to run: feed in a system whose answer we already know — the spring — and check that Newton's law comes back out. A rule that regenerates known physics is the right rule.

Step 3 — turn the crank for the spring. We only need two derivatives of \(H=\dfrac{p^2}{2m}+\tfrac12 kx^2\). One at a time.

First equation, \(\dot x=\partial H/\partial p\). Differentiate with respect to \(p\), holding \(x\) fixed — so the \(\tfrac12kx^2\) term is a constant and vanishes:

So \(\dot x = v\). That's almost too obvious — "position changes at the rate of the velocity" — which is the point: the machinery didn't break the definition of velocity.

So \(\dot p=-kx\). Now connect it back to acceleration: momentum is \(p=mv\), so its rate of change is \(\dot p=m\dot v=ma\). Set the two expressions for \(\dot p\) equal:

That is Newton's second law for the spring — mass × acceleration equals the spring force \(-kx\). We started from an energy function and the equation of motion fell straight out. It's the same physics as rung 3, reached from the opposite direction. (Quick consistency check: \(a=-kx/m=-(k/m)x=-\omega^2x\), exactly rung 3's law, with \(\omega^2=k/m\).)

Because \(H\) stays fixed, the particle can never leave its energy level — so its phase-space path is a single closed ring (exactly the loop from rung 3). 5 picture the motion as a fluid

Every point in the plane has a velocity

Don't track one particle now — imagine every point of phase space as a possible state, each with its own marching order from rung 3: a state at (x,v) moves with phase-space velocity (ẋ, v̇) = (v, −ω²x). That's a vector field: an arrow at every point. The whole plane is flowing, like a fluid.

fig 5 · the flow field (arrows) + a drifting tile
the tile gets carried by the arrows — watch its area. area = 1.000

The key question for Liouville: does this flow squeeze a little patch of states together or spread it apart? The quantity that measures exactly that is the divergence of the velocity field — the sum of "how fast the \(x\)-velocity grows as you step in \(x\)" and "how fast the \(p\)-velocity grows as you step in \(p\)." If neighbours get pushed apart faster than they're pulled together, divergence is positive (the patch expands); if it's zero, the patch's area is frozen. Compute it term by term — no leap:

  • The flow velocity is (\dot x,\dot p)=\big(\partial H/\partial p,\ -\partial H/\partial x\big).
  • Divergence = \dfrac{\partial \dot x}{\partial x}+\dfrac{\partial \dot p}{\partial p}.
  • Substitute: \dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}\!\Big(\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial p}\Big)+\dfrac{\partial}{\partial p}\!\Big(-\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial x}\Big)=\dfrac{\partial^2 H}{\partial x\,\partial p}-\dfrac{\partial^2 H}{\partial p\,\partial x}.
  • Those last two terms are the same second derivative of \(H\) taken in opposite orders — and a standard calculus fact (the symmetry of mixed partial derivatives, Clairaut's theorem) says order doesn't matter, so they're equal and cancel exactly: divergence =0.
read itZero divergence means the flow is incompressible — it never compresses or rarefies. That cancellation came only from the plus/minus crossing in Hamilton's equations. No specific H needed: it's true for every Hamiltonian system. That is Liouville's theorem in disguise.
6 the theorem, made visible

Liouville: a blob keeps its area forever

Joseph Liouville (19th-century French mathematician). His theorem, stated for what we just built: under Hamiltonian flow, phase-space volume is conserved. Take a little blob of states and let it evolve — it can stretch, twist, and shear into a wild shape, but its area never changes. That's exactly what "incompressible flow" buys you.

Below: a square of states released into the spring's flow. It rotates and shears — but the running area readout stays pinned. (The integrator is symplectic, so it honors this to machine precision, which is itself a piece of the lesson.)

7 from countable points to a smear

Many particles become a density f(x,v)

A real system has zillions of particles, each a point in phase space. Tracking them individually is hopeless, so we describe them by a density: f(x,v,t) = how many particles are near position x with velocity v. Bright = crowded, dark = empty.

This is the object your code actually stores: a 2-D array, position along one axis, velocity along the other. It's the same phase plane as before — we've just gone from a few dots to a continuous heat-map.

8 the equation, assembled

Liouville → the Vlasov equation

Here's the link from rung 6, spelled out. Imagine a tiny packet of neighbouring particles drifting along together. Two things stay fixed as it moves: the number of particles in the packet (they travel as a group — none leak out) and, by Liouville, the phase-space volume the packet occupies. The density \(f\) is just number ÷ volume, so if both are fixed, \(f\) is fixed too — as seen by someone riding along with the packet. "Riding along" is the total time derivative \(df/dt\), so the whole content of Liouville is one line:

Now expand that with the chain rule. As the packet moves, \(f\) changes for two reasons: time ticks (the explicit \(t\)), and the packet's own \(x\) and \(v\) drift. The chain rule adds up all three contributions — the direct one plus one for each moving coordinate, each weighted by how fast that coordinate moves:

Substitute ẋ = v and v̇ = a from rung 1, and you've arrived:

This is the Vlasov equation. It's nothing but "phase-space density is conserved along trajectories," written out. Each term has a plain meaning:

  • \partial f/\partial t — how the density changes at a fixed spot.
  • v\,\partial f/\partial x — streaming: fast particles carry density to new positions.
  • a\,\partial f/\partial v — forcing: accelerations push density to new velocities.
strategyBecause the equation says "f is carried along trajectories," you can solve it by following the trajectories — the method of characteristics. For the streaming term that's exact, which is what makes the next step clean.
9 turn off the force

Free streaming: every velocity-row just slides

Set the force to zero (a=0). The Vlasov equation collapses to the free-streaming equation:

Why is that the solution? With no force, every particle keeps its velocity \(v\) forever and just coasts in a straight line. So a particle sitting at position \(x\) right now must have started, a time \(t\) ago, at \(x-vt\). Rung 8 told us it carried its density value unchanged the whole way. Therefore the density at \((x,v)\) now equals the initial density at \((x-vt,\,v)\) — which is exactly \(f(x,v,t)=f_0(x-vt,v)\). No new math, just "follow the coasting particle backwards."

Now read what the formula means as a picture. The velocity \(v\) doesn't change (no force), and the position advances by \(v\,t\). So in the \(f(x,v)\) image, each horizontal row of constant \(v\) slides sideways at its own speed — fast rows race ahead, slow rows crawl. A compact blob therefore shears into a diagonal streak. (And per Liouville, the streak's area is conserved — it gets thin because it gets long.)

10 why Fourier makes shifting trivial

A shift in x is a phase twist in k

The shift theorem is the only fact you need, and it's a one-liner once you see it. The Fourier transform breaks \(f(x)\) into pure waves \(e^{ikx}\). Shifting the whole function right by \(s\) means replacing \(x\) with \(x-s\) inside every wave: \(e^{ik(x-s)}=e^{-iks}\,e^{ikx}\). Each wave comes back unchanged except for a constant front factor \(e^{-iks}\). So shifting in \(x\) is the same as multiplying every Fourier component by that phase:

So the plan for one time-step: FFT along x, multiply every column by its phase, inverse-FFT. The phases for all (k_x, v) pairs form one array of shape (Nx, Nv) — the thing you're building.

11 hero

The streaming phase factor, fully built

Everything above lands here. The array is one outer product: wavenumber k_x down the rows, velocity v across the columns.

import numpy as np

# --- grids (swap in your own) ---
Nx, Nv = 256, 64
Lx = 2*np.pi
x  = np.linspace(0, Lx, Nx, endpoint=False)
dx = x[1] - x[0]
v  = np.linspace(-5, 5, Nv)        # velocity grid, shape (Nv,)
dt = 0.01

# --- wavenumbers for the x-FFT  (the 2*pi is the classic gotcha) ---
kx = 2*np.pi * np.fft.fftfreq(Nx, d=dx)     # shape (Nx,)

# --- the streaming phase factor, shape (Nx, Nv) ---
# shift theorem:  f0(x - v*dt)  <->  exp(-i*kx*v*dt) * f0_hat(kx)
phase = np.exp(-1j * kx[:, None] * v[None, :] * dt)   # (Nx,1)*(1,Nv) -> (Nx,Nv)

assert phase.shape == (Nx, Nv)
assert np.allclose(np.abs(phase), 1.0)        # pure rotation — Liouville fingerprint

And one streaming step on a field laid out as f[x, v]:

f_hat = np.fft.fft(f, axis=0)              # transform over x only
f     = np.fft.ifft(f_hat * phase, axis=0).real

The three traps, each tied to a rung you now understand:

  • The 2*np.pi on kx. fftfreq returns ordinary frequency, not angular wavenumber. Drop it and every row streams at the wrong speed (rung 10's k is angular).
  • The broadcasting axes. kx[:,None] is (Nx,1), v[None,:] is (1,Nv) → product (Nx,Nv), with k_x down the axis you FFT over. Swap them and the shape assert fires.
  • The sign. f0(x − v·dt) (moving right, rung 9) pairs with −1j under numpy's e^{-ikx} forward transform. Flip it and you stream backward in time.

The |phase|=1 assert is Liouville checking your work: if it drifts off 1, that's a bug, not physics.
