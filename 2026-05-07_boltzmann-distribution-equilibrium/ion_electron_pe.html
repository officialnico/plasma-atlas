Why is U negative?

Negative potential energy means "bound." The electron wants to be near the proton because they attract — so getting close is a downhill situation, energetically. The electron has less energy when it's near the proton than when it's far away. Less than what? Less than zero, our reference point at infinity.

To set the electron free — to drag it from where it is out to infinity — you'd have to do work on it, supplying that missing energy. The amount of work needed is exactly |U|. That's the binding energy.

opposite charges → attractive → U < 0
Like an electron bound to a proton. Bringing them closer releases energy (could come out as a photon, kinetic energy, etc). Pulling them apart costs energy.
like charges → repulsive → U > 0
Like two electrons or two protons. Bringing them closer requires doing work against the repulsion. Letting them go releases that stored work as kinetic energy as they fly apart.

How this connects to Boltzmann

The U you're computing right here is exactly what goes into n = n₀ · exp(−U/kBT). In a plasma at temperature T, electrons spend more time where U is more negative (near positive ions) and less time where U is more positive (away from them).

At room temperature, kBT ≈ 0.025 eV. Compare that to the U values you're seeing here — typically tens of eV near the proton. The ratio U/kBT is enormous, which is why bound atoms are so stable: thermal energy can't break them apart at room temperature. In a hot plasma at T ≈ 10⁴–10⁶ K, kBT grows to 1–100 eV, and now thermal energy can compete with binding — atoms ionize and the plasma forms.
