Boltzmann's wager was simple: if you can't track every particle, treat them statistically. Every microstate with the same total energy is equally likely. The distribution we call "equilibrium" is just the macrostate that contains the overwhelming majority of those microstates.

Below, particles in a 2D box collide elastically and exchange energy. Start them however you like — all moving the same speed, all stationary except a few zooming, whatever. Within seconds, their speeds will always settle into the same bell-shaped curve. That's not a coincidence. That's combinatorics.

Why does it always settle here?

Energy is conserved, but it gets shuffled at every collision. The number of ways to distribute a fixed total energy among many particles is overwhelmingly biased toward the bell-shaped speed distribution. Other arrangements aren't forbidden — they're just astronomically rare.

— 02

What does "useful" mean here?

Once you trust this shape, you can compute temperature, pressure, diffusion — without ever tracking a single particle. The whole edifice of thermodynamics rests on this: macroscopic laws emerge because microscopic chaos has only one statistically favored outcome.

— 03

So what keeps "going"?

Individual particles never stop colliding and swapping energy. But the distribution is stationary — it's a fixed point of the random-shuffling process. Nudge it and it rebounds. That's equilibrium: not stillness, but dynamic balance.
