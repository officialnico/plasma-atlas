A the formula
n(x) = n₀ · exp(−U / kBT)
n₀ is the reference bulk density (where U = 0). We're computing how much the local density differs from that reference.
B plug in your values
U = −2.0 eV
kBT = 1.0 eV
C form the ratio U / kBT
−2.0 / 1.0 = −2.0
This is the dimensionless number at the heart of Boltzmann statistics. It asks: how many thermal-energy-units tall (or deep) is this hill?
D apply the minus sign
−(U / kBT) = +2.0
Negative U (well) becomes a positive exponent → density rises. Positive U (hill) becomes a negative exponent → density falls.
E exponentiate
exp(+2.0) = 7.39
e²·⁰ ≈ 7.39 — every additional unit in the exponent multiplies by another factor of e ≈ 2.72.
→ density relative to bulk
n / n₀ = 7.39 ≈ 7× bulk

The exponential, in a single table

This is what makes the Boltzmann formula so dramatic. The exponent depends on U/kBT linearly, but the density depends on it exponentially. Each row below is one additional unit of well depth — and density grows by a factor of e ≈ 2.72 each step.

Why this answers your "spike" question

The density spike at the bottom of the well comes from nothing more than this exponential function. The formula n = n₀ · exp(−U/kBT) has no competing geometric factors — unlike the speed distribution, where v² suppresses things at v = 0. So as U dives below zero, the exponent climbs above zero, and exp() runs away with it.

Concretely: every additional kBT of depth = one additional factor of e ≈ 2.72 in density. That's why "moderately bound" states (a few kBT deep) give modest enhancement, while "strongly bound" states (10+ kBT deep) give astronomical pile-ups — the kind of pile-up that makes molecules stable, screens electric fields in plasmas, and explains why atmospheres thin out as you go up but don't disappear.

The clearest takeaway: Boltzmann's formula is brutally sensitive to the ratio U/kBT, and only that ratio. Two systems with completely different physical scales — eV-scale plasma vs. meV-scale molecular thermal motion — give the same density profile if their U/kBT ratios match. The exponent is what does all the work.
