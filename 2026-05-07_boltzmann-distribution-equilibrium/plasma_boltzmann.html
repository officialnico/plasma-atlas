The sheath is what's left.

A plasma is a soup of positive ions and electrons — overall neutral in the bulk. But put a charged wall in contact with it, and the species respond very differently. Electrons are light and fast; ions are heavy and slow. The lighter, more mobile species redistributes itself almost instantly to follow the Boltzmann relation — that's why your equation works.

Below: a 1D plasma chamber. The left edge is a wall held at potential φ_wall (you control it). Watch the electron density (cyan) and ion density (orange) settle into different exponential profiles. The region near the wall where they no longer balance is the sheath — and its width is the Debye length.

— 01 · ions

Heavy and inertial

Ions barely move during the time electrons rearrange themselves. On the electron timescale, ion density looks roughly uniform. That's why your formula n = n₀ exp(−qφ/k_BT) is usually applied to the electrons — they're the ones that thermalize quickly enough to obey it.

— 02 · electrons

Light and Boltzmann-distributed

Electrons are pushed away from a negative wall (their charge is −e, so −eφ_wall is large and positive → density crashes). At a positive wall, they pile up. Either way, the equilibrium profile is exponential. This is your equation in action.

— 03 · the sheath

Where neutrality breaks

Far from the wall, n_i ≈ n_e and the plasma is quasineutral. Within a few Debye lengths of the wall, electrons have fled and the region is dominated by ions — a positive space-charge layer that screens the wall from the bulk. That's the sheath.

Boltzmann density relation · electron equilibrium in a potential
ne(x) = n0 · exp(−(−e)·φ(x) / kBTe)
For electrons (q = −e), a negative wall (φ < 0) gives −qφ < 0, so the exponent is negative and density drops. The same equation governs ions in atmospheres, bound electrons in semiconductors, and the barometric formula.
