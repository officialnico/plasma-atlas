Mean-field theory:
how physics tames
10²⁰ things at once.

Tracking every particle in a system is impossible. So physicists made a deal with reality — they replace "every particle interacting with every other particle" with "every particle interacting with the average of all the others." This guide builds the intuition from scratch, with five animated scenes you can poke at.

I
— the problem mean-field theory solves

Tracking every interaction is impossible.

Below: a swarm of particles, each one pulling or pushing on every other one. Drag the slider to add particles. Watch the lines explode. With N particles, there are N×(N−1)/2 interactions. At N = 100, that's 4,950 lines. At N = 10²⁰ (a real plasma), it's 5 × 10³⁹. Untrackable.

8
— the real situation Each line is an interaction. With just 8 particles you already have 28 lines. The mathematical complexity grows as N² — quadratically — which means doubling the number of particles quadruples the work. No computer in any conceivable future could track every interaction in a real gas, plasma, or fluid. Physics needed an out.
II
— the surprising thing that saves us

Most of those interactions cancel out.

Here's a single test particle (yellow) in a sea of others (red). Each red particle pulls on the yellow one. Watch the arrows: each one is a force from one red particle. Now look at the green arrow — the net force, the sum of them all. It's tiny. When particles are spread roughly uniformly, pulls in opposite directions cancel.

— what to notice Drag uniformity to 100 — particles spread evenly — and watch the net force shrink to nearly zero, even though each individual particle is pulling hard. This is the entire reason mean-field theory works.
III
— the move that simplifies everything

Replace the swarm with its average.

Same setup, but watch what happens when we smear out the discrete particles into a smooth density field. The mean field is the average effect of the whole crowd, treated as a continuous fluid. The yellow test particle now responds only to that smooth average — not to individual particles.

scene 03   ·   from particles to fields smear: 0%
0
— the trick At smear = 0, you see individual particles. At smear = 100, they've melted into a continuous density. The force on the test particle is nearly identical in both cases — but the smooth version is mathematically much simpler to work with. Mean-field theory is just this swap, applied universally. Replace discrete particles with a smooth density (the "field"), then compute as if you're dealing with a fluid.
"The crowd is more predictable than any individual."
— the central claim of mean-field theory
IV
— putting it to work

Compare: individual vs. mean-field.

Two side-by-side simulations. Left: the test particle responds to every individual neighbor exactly. Right: the test particle responds only to the mean field (average density). Watch them evolve. In a system with many uniformly-distributed particles, the trajectories are nearly identical. Mean-field theory is essentially exact in the right regime.

— the magic With many particles, the two pictures match. The dashed yellow trail (mean-field) tracks the solid yellow trail (full N-body) almost perfectly. Mean-field is not an approximation — it's the actual physics in the limit of many particles. That's why it's used for everything from gases to magnets to neural networks.
V
— honesty about limits

It only works when the crowd is big and unstructured.

Mean-field theory has a clear regime of validity. Drop N too low, or let particles cluster too tightly, and the cancellation breaks down — individual interactions matter again.

✓ works when

Many particles (N is large)
Roughly uniform distribution
Weak coupling (thermal energy > interaction energy)
Far from critical points (no diverging fluctuations)

Examples: gases at room temperature, ordinary plasmas, dilute solutions, large neural networks, most magnetic materials above their Curie point.

✗ fails when

Few particles (small N — averaging doesn't happen)
Strong correlations (particles "know" about specific neighbors)
Strong coupling (interactions dominate over thermal motion)
Near phase transitions (fluctuations on all length scales)

Examples: ion lattices in white dwarfs, atoms in liquids near freezing, electron pairs in superconductors, critical opalescence, biological systems with specific networks.

VI
— back to your formula

This is exactly what your Boltzmann formula does.

The plasma version: each electron interacts with every other charge — billions of ions, billions of electrons. Mean-field theory replaces all those individual interactions with a single smooth potential φ(r), built from the average distribution of all the charges. Electron-electron contributions cancel (you saw this directly in the previous app's "show e⁻ contribs" toggle), so what's left is just the response to ions. The formula n = n₀ · exp(eφ/kBT) is mean-field theory in one line.

— the punchline Click an electron, then toggle "show electron contributions." You'll see many faint blue lines from other electrons — but their vector sum is small, because they cancel. The formula doesn't ignore them; it folds their average into n₀ and only computes the deviation caused by ions. That deviation is the physics. Everything else cancels.
