The ∇ operator,
decoded.

That weird upside-down triangle ∇ shows up everywhere in physics. It's not "the gradient of nothing" — it's an operator, a tool waiting to act on something. What it does depends on what you put after it. This guide walks through it from scratch, with no assumed background. Five sections, five interactive scenes, all the math made visible.

I
— foundation

A scalar field is just a number at every point.

Imagine a room. Stand anywhere — at every spot you can stand, there's a temperature. It's just one number per spot. That's a scalar field. Temperature, altitude, pressure, density, electric potential — all scalar fields. One number, anywhere you stand. Hover over the demo to see it.

The electric potential φ(r) is a scalar field — one number at every point. That's why it's so manageable. And the density n(r) is also a scalar field — just count electrons per volume, get a number.
II
— foundation

A vector field is an arrow at every point.

A scalar field is one number per point. A vector field is one arrow per point — something with both magnitude (how big) and direction (which way). Think of wind blowing across a map: every spot has a wind vector showing strength and direction. The electric field E is a vector field. So is fluid velocity, magnetic field, gravity, force.

scalar field

One number per point. Like temperature, altitude, density, electric potential φ. A "heatmap." Has no direction. You can ask "what's the value here?" and get a number.

vector field

One arrow per point. Like wind velocity, fluid flow, electric field E, force. You can ask "what's the magnitude here?" and "which direction here?" — both answers needed.

III
— first use of ∇

The gradient turns a scalar field into a vector field.

Start with a scalar field — a heatmap of values. The gradient at any point answers: "if I'm standing here and want to walk uphill as fast as possible, which way should I go and how steep is it?" The answer is a vector — direction (uphill) and magnitude (steepness). That's gradient. The notation is ∇f for "gradient of f."

∇f = vector pointing in the direction f increases fastest
∇ acting on a scalar produces a vector. Magnitude = steepness. Direction = uphill.
scene 03   ·   gradient = scalar field → vector field gradient at cursor: —
— the key picture The gradient arrows always point uphill on the heatmap (toward higher values). At the top of a hill, the gradient is zero (no uphill direction). At the bottom of a valley, it's also zero. On a slope, it points up the slope, with bigger arrows for steeper slopes.

The plasma connection

The relationship E = −∇φ says: the electric field at any point is the gradient of φ, pointing the OTHER way (because of the minus sign). That's why field lines go from high φ to low φ — field arrows point downhill on the φ heatmap. That's the entire content of E = −∇φ.
IV
— second use of ∇

The divergence measures spread or pile-up.

Now flip it. Start with a vector field (arrows everywhere). The divergence at any point asks: "is stuff spreading OUT from here, piling IN, or just passing through?" The answer is a single number — positive (spreading out, like a faucet), negative (piling in, like a drain), or zero (passing through). Notation is ∇·v — "del DOT v." The dot is a dot product, turning the operator into a scalar output.

∇·v = scalar that says how much the vector field v spreads out
∇· acting on a vector produces a scalar. Positive = source. Negative = sink. Zero = pass-through.
scene 04   ·   divergence — sources and sinks divergence at cursor: —
— what to look for The colored regions show divergence at each point: red = positive (source), blue = negative (sink), yellow = zero (no net spread). Notice the swirl case: arrows rotate around but don't pile up or spread out — divergence is zero everywhere even though the field is doing something. Also try uniform wind: same arrow everywhere, divergence zero — stuff flows through, but in equals out.

The plasma connection

The equation ∇·E = ρ/ε₀ (Gauss's law) says: the divergence of the electric field at any point equals the charge density there. Positive charges are sources (field spreads outward — positive divergence). Negative charges are sinks (field flows inward — negative divergence). Empty space has zero divergence. That's how electromagnetism encodes "charges create fields."
V
— combining the operators

Stack ∇ on ∇ to get Poisson's equation.

In plasma physics you'll see this: ∇²φ = −ρ/ε₀ — Poisson's equation. Looks intimidating. It's actually just the two operators stacked. ∇² means "divergence of gradient." Apply gradient to φ to get a vector (the field). Then apply divergence to that vector to get back a scalar (the local source strength). The result connects φ to charge density.

∇·∇φ = ∇²φ = −ρ/ε₀
scalar → (gradient) → vector → (divergence) → scalar
The scalar at the end equals the local charge density.

Why this matters

The same physical scene has three equivalent descriptions: as a potential field, as a force field, or as a charge distribution. The ∇ operators let you translate between them. Plasma physics is fluent in all three views, switching between them depending on what's easiest. That's why ∇ is everywhere.
"The ∇ operator is just a translator. It moves between scalar fields and vector fields, picking up information about how things change in space as it goes."
— summary card · pin this somewhere

Three operators. One symbol.

∇f (gradient): scalar → vector. "which way is uphill?"

∇·v (divergence): vector → scalar. "how much is this point a source?"

∇²f (Laplacian): scalar → scalar. "how much does the value here differ from the average around it?"

That's all. The notation is dense but the ideas are concrete: rates of change, spreading, and curvature in space.
