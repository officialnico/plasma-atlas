§ I — The Postulate

A clock made of light

Imagine a clock that ticks each time a photon bounces between two mirrors. Now set it in motion. The clock cannot be allowed to break the speed of light — and so something else must give.

Two observers watch the same photon bouncing between two mirrors. The first observer rides alongside the clock; the second watches it sail by. Both must agree on one thing: the speed of light is the same for everyone. That is the postulate from which all the strangeness follows.

The traveller sees the photon move straight up and down. The stationary witness sees it trace a longer, diagonal path — the clock has moved sideways during each bounce. A longer path at the same speed must take longer. And so the moving clock runs slow, not in appearance but in fact.

§ III — The Weight of Hours

Time bends with gravity

Mass curves the fabric of spacetime, and clocks held closer to a heavy thing run slower than clocks held farther away. The deeper the well, the heavier the hour.

This is the second great surprise of relativity, and the more profound of the two. Gravity is not a force pulling you down — it is the curvature of spacetime itself, and clocks at different depths in that curvature tick at different rates.

A clock at sea level runs measurably slower than one on a mountaintop. GPS satellites must correct for this every nanosecond, or your phone's location would drift by miles each day. Move the slider toward black-hole densities, and watch the well plunge — and the hours with it.

§ IV — The Cone of Causality

All possible futures

In spacetime, every event sits at the apex of a cone of light. Inside the cone lies what you can affect; outside lies the elsewhere — events forever beyond your reach.

Apparatus 04
— Past, future, and the elsewhere —
SPACETIME DIAGRAM

You stand at the centre. Time runs vertically; space horizontally. Light radiates outward at exactly 45° — neither faster nor slower. Everything you can ever influence in the future must lie within the upper cone; everything that could have influenced your present must lie within the lower one.

The grey region — the elsewhere — is forbidden territory. No signal can reach you from there in time. No signal of yours can ever reach it. Whole histories of the universe are happening there right now that you can neither learn of nor change. Move your cursor over the diagram to see what each region means.

§ V — Coda

What time actually is

What you have just watched is no thought-experiment, no philosophical conceit. Each effect has been measured. Atomic clocks flown around the world return with measurably different readings. GPS satellites correct for both special and general relativity, every microsecond, every day. Muons created in the upper atmosphere reach the ground only because their internal clocks slow at relativistic speed. The universe agrees with Einstein to fourteen decimal places.

And so the everyday picture — a single river of time flowing equally for all — is simply, beautifully, wrong. There is no universal now. Your time is yours; mine is mine; we can compare notes only when we meet again, and the totals will not match.

Time is a coordinate in a four-dimensional fabric that bends under mass and stretches under motion. It is the most personal thing you have, and the least universal. A traveller returns younger than her sibling; a clock at altitude races ahead of one at sea; a photon, moving always at the only speed there is, experiences no time at all.

— Whether time itself is fundamental, or whether it emerges from something deeper still, is a question for the next century of physics.
