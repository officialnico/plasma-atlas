Gaussian Moment Integrals

An interactive tool for understanding how integrals of bell curves work — and why the magic numbers (0, 1) come out the way they do.

The integral: ∫−∞∞ (1/√(2π)) e−v²/2 dv = 1
What it means: The total area under a standard bell curve equals exactly 1. This is the "normalization" of the Gaussian — it's calibrated so probabilities sum to 100%. In the moment exercise, this is the integral that gives you n(x,t) = n₀ from your distribution function.

The integral: ∫−∞∞ v · (1/√(2π)) e−v²/2 dv = 0
Why zero? Multiplying by v makes the integrand odd — the negative-v half mirrors the positive-v half but with opposite sign. The two halves cancel exactly. This is why the mean velocity of an unshifted Maxwellian is zero.

Look at the shape: it's antisymmetric. Whatever positive area sits to the right of v=0, the same magnitude of negative area sits to the left. They cancel when you add them up — that's exactly what "the integral is zero" means.

The integral: ∫−∞∞ v² · (1/√(2π)) e−v²/2 dv = 1
What it means: Multiplying by v² makes the integrand even (and always non-negative). Both halves contribute positively. The total area equals 1 for the standard Gaussian — this is the variance, which equals σ².
v²·f(v) — the integrand
area inside slider range
Notice the integrand is zero at v=0 (because v² is zero there) and rises before falling — it's the bell curve "weighted" by v². The peaks are at v = ±1, which is exactly σ. This is why σ² is called the "second moment" — it captures the spread of the distribution.

The integral: ∫−∞∞ v · (1/√(2π)) e−(v−u₀)²/2 dv = u₀
What it means: If you shift the Gaussian so its peak is at u₀ instead of 0, the mean velocity becomes u₀. Slide the drift slider to see the curve translate, and watch the integral track the peak.

When u₀ = 0, v·f is odd and the integral is 0. As u₀ shifts away from zero, the symmetry breaks — one half of v·f gets bigger than the other, and the integral picks up a value equal to u₀. The substitution w = v − u₀ that you did in Exercise 4 is the algebraic version of this picture.
