The "stiffness" of empty space

ε₀ (epsilon nought, or epsilon zero) is a fundamental constant of nature. It tells you how much an electric field is "resisted" or "spread out" by the vacuum itself. Despite the vacuum having no matter in it, it still has electromagnetic properties — and ε₀ measures one of them.

You can think of it three ways:

As a coupling
It links electric charge to the field that charge produces.
As a stiffness
A larger ε₀ means the vacuum "absorbs" more field per unit charge.
As a baseline
All other materials are measured relative to ε₀.

It also connects electricity, magnetism, and light through one of the most beautiful relations in physics:

c = 1 / √(ε₀ · μ₀)

Here c is the speed of light and μ₀ is the permeability of free space. The fact that light's speed is determined by these two electromagnetic constants is what told Maxwell that light is an electromagnetic wave.

Coulomb's law — where ε₀ shows up first

The force between two point charges in a vacuum is:

F = (1 / 4πε₀) · q₁q₂ / r²

The factor 1/(4πε₀) ≈ 8.99 × 10⁹ N·m²/C² is also called Coulomb's constant, k. So a small ε₀ means a big k — i.e. a big force per coulomb. Try it:

Parallel-plate capacitor

A capacitor's ability to store charge depends directly on ε₀. For two parallel plates of area A separated by distance d in a vacuum:

C = ε₀ · A / d

So ε₀ is literally the capacitance, per unit area, of a 1-meter gap of vacuum. That's why its units are farads per meter.

Vacuum vs. dielectric

When you place charges in a real material instead of vacuum, the material's molecules polarize and partially cancel the field. We capture this with a relative permittivity εᵣ, and the effective permittivity becomes ε = εᵣ · ε₀.

μ₀ — the magnetic counterpart

If ε₀ tells you how the vacuum responds to electric charge, μ₀ (mu nought) tells you how the vacuum responds to electric current. It's the magnetic permeability of free space:

μ₀ ≈ 1.25663706 × 10⁻⁶ N/A²

Equivalently, μ₀ ≈ 4π × 10⁻⁷ H/m (henries per meter). Until 2019 this was its exact defined value; today it's measured, but extremely close to that number.

μ₀ first appears in Ampère's law, which gives the magnetic field around a long straight wire carrying current I:

B = μ₀ I / (2π r)

The deepest connection: light

The single most beautiful fact about ε₀ and μ₀ is that they together determine the speed of light:

c = 1 / √(ε₀ · μ₀)

Plug in the numbers: 1 / √(8.854 × 10⁻¹² × 1.257 × 10⁻⁶) ≈ 2.998 × 10⁸ m/s. That's exactly c. When Maxwell noticed this in the 1860s, he realized light is an electromagnetic wave — propagating through the vacuum at a speed set entirely by these two electromagnetic constants.

ε₀ controls
How charges create electric fields. Bigger ε₀ → weaker electric force.
μ₀ controls
How currents create magnetic fields. Bigger μ₀ → stronger magnetic effect per amp.

They're partners: change either one, and the speed of light would change with it.

Why physicists care about ε₀

It's a structural constant — it appears anywhere electricity touches geometry:

Coulomb's law
F = q₁q₂ / (4πε₀ r²) — sets the strength of static electric force.
Gauss's law
∮ E · dA = Q / ε₀ — total electric flux is charge divided by ε₀.
Speed of light
c = 1 / √(ε₀μ₀) — links electromagnetism to light.
Energy in fields
u = ½ ε₀ E² — energy density stored in any electric field.
Fine-structure constant
α = e² / (4πε₀ ℏc) — hides inside this dimensionless number that governs atomic structure.

Since the 2019 SI redefinition, ε₀ is no longer exactly defined — it's measured experimentally, with a tiny relative uncertainty around 1.5 × 10⁻¹⁰. So it's a real number we go and measure, not a definition we choose.
