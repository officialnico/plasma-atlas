01 — the wrong tool

Velocity isn't periodic.

Here's the thing about a plasma. At every point in space there's a spread of particle velocities — some slow, some fast, most near average. Plot how many particles have each velocity and you get a bell curve. It rises in the middle and dies away to nothing at the edges. It does not repeat.

Fourier's sine waves are built for things that repeat forever — periodic signals. Try to describe a lonely bell-shaped bump with sines and you fight the tool the whole way: the waves wrap around, they ring past the edges, they never quite settle to zero. Watch how badly it goes.

Even with twenty sines you get wiggles in the tails that shouldn't be there. The basis and the shape disagree about the world. We need building blocks that are already bell-shaped — that die away at the edges on their own.

02 — the building blocks

Meet the Hermite shapes.

A Hermite building block is just a polynomial times a Gaussian — a bell curve multiplied by 1, then v, then v²−1, and so on. The Gaussian makes every one of them die away at the edges automatically. The polynomial gives each a different number of wiggles.

The 0th is a plain bell. The 1st is a bell tilted into a wiggle. Each one up adds exactly one more bump. Toggle them on and off:

These are the sines-and-cosines of velocity space. Any reasonable bell-ish distribution can be built by mixing them — and because they're born bell-shaped, the fit is honest right out to the tails.

03 — the punchline

The coefficients are physics.

This is the part that makes everyone fall in love with the basis. When you write a distribution as a mix of Hermite shapes, the amount of each shape is a physical quantity you already know. They aren't abstract numbers. They're the things a fluid model tracks.

Drag a coefficient — watch the gas respond
drift the whole gas 0.0
heat it / cool it 0.0
skew the spread 0.0

Slide c₁ and the whole bell drifts — that's bulk velocity, the wind in the gas. Slide c₂ and it fattens or pinches — that's temperature. Slide c₃ and it goes lopsided — that's heat flux, energy leaking one direction.

This is the whole game. The low coefficients are density, flow, temperature, heat flux — the quantities of fluid physics. The high ones store the fine kinetic detail a fluid model throws away. The basis sits exactly on the seam between fluid and kinetic.
04 — why it's clean

Nobody steps on anyone.

Why can each coefficient mean one clean thing? Because the shapes are orthogonal — the same property that lets Fourier modes not interfere. Multiply any two different Hermite shapes together, add up the result, and you get exactly zero. Multiply one by itself and you get a clean positive number.

That means reading off "how much of shape 3 is in this gas?" doesn't get contaminated by shapes 2 or 4. Each measurement is independent. Hover the grid — bright means overlap, dark means none:

Overlap between shape m and shape ndiagonal = self, rest = 0

A perfect dark grid with a glowing diagonal. Every shape is blind to every other. That orthogonality is what turns "fit this curve" from a tangled simultaneous-equation mess into one independent number per shape.

05 — how they talk

The ladder, and the one move.

Plasma physics keeps doing one operation over and over: multiply the distribution by velocity v — because particles at velocity v stream through space at speed v. Here's the magic. Multiply a Hermite shape by v and you don't get a mess. You get exactly its two neighbors: the one below and the one above.

v × shape n  =  (a bit of n−1)  +  (a bit of n+1)

So the shapes form a ladder. Energy poked into the bottom rung climbs up, one rung at a time, neighbor to neighbor. No shape reaches across to a distant one. Watch a pulse injected at the bottom climb:

v · (shape n) couples only to n±1climbing ↑

This is why the basis is cheap on a computer. The core operation only ever touches neighbors — a tidy, local, regular update a GPU devours. No giant transform, no global reshuffling. Just "pass it up the ladder."

06 — the killer feature

One dial: fluid ↔ kinetic.

Here's the payoff that no velocity-grid can match. How many rungs of the ladder you keep is a knob. Keep just a few and you've got a smooth, cheap, fluid-like gas. Keep many and you resolve every sharp kinetic feature exactly. Same code, one slider, the entire spectrum from fast-and-rough to slow-and-exact.

Rebuilding a spiky distribution with N rungs
4 Hermite modes — fluid · cheap · smooth

At N=4 you've basically got a fluid model — it catches the gist and runs in a blink. Crank toward N=40 and the sharp real shape snaps into focus. You pay only for the fidelity you need, and you dial it per-problem. A velocity grid forces you to pay for the finest detail everywhere, always.

This single dial is the reason a code like GX — and the proposed code — can be fast. Most plasma turbulence barely strays from a bell curve, so a handful of rungs nails it. The grid-based codes haul around hundreds of velocity points to express the same thing.
07 — the one catch

A ladder has to end somewhere.

In a real plasma the ladder is infinite — energy climbs forever into finer and finer velocity detail and never returns. That one-way escape is a famous effect with a name: Landau damping, a wave dying with zero friction by handing its energy to particles surfing at its speed.

But your computer's ladder stops at the top rung. Do nothing and energy hits the ceiling and bounces back down — fake, unphysical, ruins everything. So you add a closure: a rule at the top that says "energy that climbs here leaves and doesn't come back," faking the missing infinite ladder.

drain at the top → the wave dies correctly
wall at the top → the wave bounces back, wrong

Get that closure slightly wrong and the whole thing blows up — which, for what it's worth, is exactly the bug I spent four tries on before building this. A flipped sign, a missing drain. The cheapest part of the physics is the easiest to get quietly, plausibly wrong. (You may have already watched this live on the green oscilloscope.)

08 — hero level

And Laguerre is the same idea, twice.

Velocity in a magnetic field has two directions that matter: along the field line, and around it (the spiraling gyration). Hermite handles the along-the-line direction. Laguerre — a sister family of polynomials — handles the spiral. Same trick, same orthogonality, same neighbor-ladder, just shaped for the round direction instead of the straight one.

Stack them together — Hermite for parallel velocity, Laguerre for the gyration — and you've spanned the whole velocity space with two ladders of physically-meaningful, orthogonal, neighbor-coupled, dial-able coefficients.

Hermite × Laguerre — the full velocity grid of modesspinning live
That's the engine. Born bell-shaped, so it fits a plasma honestly. Coefficients are physics, so a fluid model is just the low rungs. One dial from cheap to exact. Neighbor-only coupling, so a GPU flies.

That is the entire reason a modern gyrokinetic code reaches for Hermite–Laguerre instead of a velocity grid — and why the new code design bets its whole speed story on it. The open question that design can't dodge isn't whether the basis works — you just watched it work — it's whether it stays this cheap in the twisted 3D geometry of a stellarator. That's the part nobody's proven yet. Everything you scrolled through is the part that's solid ground.
