Why a magnetic field makes a particle go in a circle

A magnetic force always points sideways to the motion. A sideways force can only turn you — it can never speed you up or slow you down. Turning by the same amount forever, at constant speed, is a circle.

02 — the reason

A sideways force does no work

"Speed" only changes when a force pushes along the direction of travel — with the motion to speed up, against it to slow down. The magnetic force has zero component along the motion, so it adds no speed at all. Every instant it does the one thing left to it: it nudges the velocity arrow sideways, rotating it without stretching it. Do that continuously and the tip of the velocity arrow sweeps a circle — and so does the particle.

What sets the size

More momentum is harder to bend, so a faster (or heavier) particle carves a wider loop. The radius is exactly the perpendicular momentum divided by charge times field.

What sets the rhythm

The time per lap doesn't depend on speed at all — go faster and the loop just grows to match. The rate is fixed by the field and the particle's mass (its inertia).

03 — test it yourself

Tilt the force off perpendicular

Here's the proof. The slider tips the force slightly off the right angle, giving it a small component along the motion — which now does work. The instant it does, the speed changes, the radius changes with it, and the clean circle unwinds into a spiral. Only at a perfect right angle does the path close.

Perfectly perpendicular — does no work — closed circle.
This is the whole of single-particle gyromotion. Feed the magnetic force F = q v × B into the same F = Δp/Δt you already know, and out falls a circle whose radius is the momentum and whose frequency is the inertia. Everything else in plasma physics — the helix along the field, drifts, the magnetic moment — is built on top of this one circle.
