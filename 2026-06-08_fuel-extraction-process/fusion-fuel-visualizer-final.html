The goal · star-power on Earth

To run a fusion reactor,
you need to feed it
two rare cousins of hydrogen.

Smash a deuterium nucleus into a tritium nucleus hard enough and they fuse — making helium, firing out a high-speed neutron, and releasing a huge burst of energy. This single reaction is what powers the Sun and what we're trying to bottle on Earth.

²H+³H→⁴He+n+17.6 MeV ⚡
proton neutron deuterium tritium helium

The catch: one of these fuels is everywhere, and the other barely exists. The next four steps show how we get each one — and the clever trick that lets a reactor brew its own fuel.

Step 2 · the easy one

Deuterium: filtered out of water

You don't really "make" deuterium — it's already floating in every glass of water, about 1 atom in every 6,400 hydrogens. The job is to concentrate those rare heavy ones until you have nearly pure heavy water (D₂O), then split it into deuterium gas.

METHOD A

Girdler sulfide

Water and H₂S gas trade places between hot & cold towers. Deuterium prefers the cold water, so stage after stage it piles up. The industrial workhorse.

METHOD B

Electrolysis

Run electricity through water: light hydrogen bubbles off faster, leaving deuterium behind in the liquid. Energy-hungry, so used as a final polish.

METHOD C

Distillation

Heavy water boils at a slightly higher temperature, so careful boiling slowly separates it. Small differences, repeated thousands of times.

Final step: pass current through the purified D₂O and collect D₂ gas. Deuterium is stable, cheap, and harmless — the easy half of the fuel. ✅

Step 3 · the hard one

Tritium: it has to be built

Tritium is radioactive. Half of any batch decays away every 12.3 years, so almost none survives in nature — it must be manufactured. The recipe: fire a neutron at lithium-6.

THE BREEDING REACTION

Neutron + Lithium-6

⁶Li+n→⁴He+³H

A neutron splits lithium-6 into helium and a fresh tritium nucleus. This is done inside nuclear reactors, which provide the flood of neutrons. Some reactor types (like heavy-water CANDUs) also collect tritium as a byproduct.

Step 4 · the payoff

The reactor brews its own fuel

Here's the elegant part. Fusion fires out fast neutrons — exactly what's needed to breed tritium. So we wrap the plasma in a lithium blanket. Stray neutrons hit the lithium, make new tritium, and we feed it straight back as fuel.

Inside the plasma
D + T fuse → He + neutron
In the blanket
neutron + ⁶Li → fresh T
The target (TBR)
make > 1 tritium per one burned

Hitting that breeding ratio above 1 — slightly more tritium out than in — is one of the key things ITER and future reactors are built to prove. Crack it, and the only fuels you ever truly buy are water and lithium. 🌊⚛️

Why D-T at all? It ignites at a lower temperature than any other fusion fuel — so even though tritium is a headache to source, it's the realistic starting point for first-generation reactors. But how much more than 1 does the breeding ratio really need to be? →

To push TBR up, engineers enrich the blanket in lithium-6 and add neutron multipliers (beryllium or lead) that turn one neutron into two — so a single fusion neutron can breed more than one tritium. Real designs aim for roughly 1.05–1.15.

Step 6 · turning 1 neutron into 2

Neutron multipliers

Each D-T fusion makes only one neutron. But that neutron must breed at least one tritium — and some neutrons always get lost in the walls. With just one per fusion, the breeding ratio can't stay above 1. The fix: a material that throws back two neutrons for every one that hits it.

THE (n, 2n) REACTION · how it works

A fast neutron hits beryllium-9

n+⁹Be→2n+2 × ⁴He

Beryllium-9's last neutron is barely attached, so a fast neutron knocks it loose and the nucleus crumbles into two helium (alpha) particles. Reading the symbols: "2 × ⁴He" means two helium-4 nuclei — the small 4 is the mass number (2 protons + 2 neutrons each), not a count. So the products are 2 helium nuclei (the 4-dot clumps) plus 2 free neutrons (the lone dots). You can't split a neutron — but you can use its energy to shake extra neutrons out of a nucleus. And notice there's no beryllium left: its 4 protons end up inside those two helium nuclei. The beryllium didn't vanish — it became the helium. That's the "one-and-done" burnup that slowly consumes a beryllium blanket (lead, by contrast, just shuffles to a lighter lead isotope and keeps working).

WHY IT MATTERS · the payoff

Flip the multiplier on

OPTION A

Beryllium-9

Last neutron held by only ~1.7 MeV, so even mildly fast neutrons free it. Light, reflects neutrons well — but rare and gets brittle.

OPTION B

Lead

Needs the fresh ~14 MeV fusion neutrons to fire its (n,2n). Cheap and plentiful, often run as a liquid lead-lithium mix that breeds and multiplies.

Bonus: the (n,2n) reaction slows the neutrons down a little — which is fine, because slower neutrons are actually better at being captured by lithium-6 to make tritium. So one trick helps twice.

Step 7 · the lead route, up close

Do you need special lead?

No. You don't enrich lead at all. Natural lead — a mix of four stable isotopes — all does the (n,2n) trick. ²⁰⁸Pb is just the most common (and the famous "heaviest stable nucleus"), but ²⁰⁷, ²⁰⁶ and ²⁰⁴ multiply neutrons too. The thing you do enrich in a lead-lithium blanket is the lithium (up to ~90% lithium-6) — never the lead.

✗ WHY IT'S NOT THE DEFAULT

Weaker multiplier — its 7.4 MeV threshold means fewer (n,2n) hits, so beryllium boosts neutrons (and energy) more.
MHD drag — pumping a conductive liquid metal through strong magnetic fields causes huge pressure drops.
Tritium barely dissolves in lead-lithium → hard to extract, and it permeates out as a leak risk.
Corrodes steel at high temperature, and activates into nasty isotopes (e.g. polonium from trace bismuth).
Low lithium density → needs thicker blankets or heavy Li-6 enrichment.

SO WHAT IS ITER ACTUALLY USING? · 2024 baseline

ITER recently switched its plasma-facing first wall from beryllium to tungsten — beryllium is toxic, erodes badly, and traps tritium, and every future reactor plans on tungsten anyway. But tungsten is not a multiplier: ITER's main blanket only shields, it doesn't breed. ITER buys its tritium from outside suppliers.

Real breeding is tested in small Test Blanket Modules, which trial both routes from these slides: the HCPB module uses beryllium pebbles as the multiplier, while the WCLL / HCLL modules use liquid lead-lithium — lead as the multiplier, with Li-6-enriched lithium as the breeder. ITER is the proving ground for both.

The real-world fuel bets

Step 10 · making the fuel

How a plant makes its own fuel

This is the self-sustaining trick the breeding and multiplier steps were building toward. A D-T power plant is fed only water and lithium — the tritium is bred and burned inside the loop, so it never has to be mined or bought. Out comes harmless helium and electricity.

THE BOOTSTRAP CATCH (today)

Breeding only works once a plant is running — but you need a few kg of tritium to start it. Today that's borrowed from CANDU fission reactors (~2 kg/yr, Canada). The entire civilian stockpile is just ~25–30 kg, decaying 5%/yr, and one plant may need up to ~10 kg to start. Closing the loop is the only long-term fix.

HELION'S DIFFERENT LOOP

Helion doesn't breed tritium — it breeds helium-3. Its D-D side reactions make ³He (and tritium that decays into more ³He), which it captures and feeds back. The only thing going in is deuterium from water.

NO BREEDING NEEDED

The abundant fuels skip all of this. D-D machines just use deuterium from water; p-¹¹B (TAE) just buys hydrogen and boron. Nothing radioactive to make — the trade-off is much higher temperatures.

BREED OR BUY? · where the field actually stands

There is no real "just buy it" option for D-T. The whole civilian world holds only ~25–30 kg of tritium, topped up ~2 kg/yr by Canada's CANDU reactors and decaying 5%/yr — and a single commercial plant needs more than that entire stock. With ~28 of 43 fusion companies planning D-T, and ITER + DEMO already claiming most of the supply, there isn't enough to go around.

So the field isn't drifting toward a purchase market — it's racing toward self-sufficiency. The plan is sequential: borrow ~1–2 kg of startup tritium to bootstrap the first plant, then breed everything after. Breeding blankets are the central thrust of DEMO programs and ITER's test modules. The newer worry is that even startup may need breeding almost immediately — which is exactly why Helion and TAE picked fuels that dodge tritium entirely.

WHO SELLS IT? · today's supply chain

Tritium → almost all of it comes from Ontario Power Generation (Canada), pulled from CANDU heavy-water reactors at the Darlington Tritium Removal Facility — the world's largest — and sold through OPG's arm Laurentis Energy Partners (only ~100–300 g/yr). Korea (Wolsong) and a planned Romanian plant (Cernavodă) are the only other civilian sources. The big US supply is reserved for weapons. In 2025 OPG signed to supply the UK's UKAEA.

Helium-3 (Helion) → the very same vault: Laurentis harvests it from decaying tritium at Darlington and sells it via Air Liquide — the world's first non-military He-3. The US DOE stockpile is the only other source. Both are tiny, hence Helion's plan to breed its own.

Deuterium → a cheap commodity from ordinary heavy-water / isotope suppliers. Nobody worries about this one.

The punchline: nearly every D-T effort on Earth ultimately buys its tritium from one Canadian utility's reactors — and Helion's helium-3 traces back to the same place.

So "self-sustaining" means different things per fuel: D-T plants breed tritium in a lithium blanket, Helion breeds helium-3, and the abundant fuels simply don't need to. Only D-T forces you to build the whole breeding-and-multiplying machinery from the earlier steps.

WHY FUSION WANTS IT · clean, charged products
²H+³He→⁴He+p+18.4 MeV

The payoff is in the products: a helium-4 nucleus and a proton, both electrically charged. Charged particles can be steered by magnetic fields and their energy harvested directly as electricity — and they don't blast the walls the way D-T's neutron does. That's the low "neutron-mess" advantage. The price is a far higher ignition temperature, which is why it sits in the long game.

HOW RARE · 1 in a million

Almost none exists on Earth

Only about one helium atom in a million is ³He — and helium itself isn't abundant. It's a leftover from the Big Bang; being so light, almost all of it leaks out of the rocks and escapes into space. Total civilian supply is measured in tens of kilograms a year.

WHERE THE LITTLE BIT COMES FROM

No way to just make it

Tritium decay — almost all civilian ³He: stored tritium decays into it (³H → ³He), harvested at Darlington and sold via Air Liquide.
Weapons legacy — the US DOE stockpile, accumulated in aging warhead tritium. Finite and draining.
D-D breeding — Helion makes its own inside the machine from deuterium side reactions, dodging the supply problem.
The Moon — lunar soil holds solar-wind ³He (perhaps ~a million tonnes), but only parts-per-billion: a future-economy idea, not a supply you can order.

The punchline: helium-3's greatest strength and its biggest obstacle are the same fact — you simply can't get much of it. Clean charged-particle fusion, millikelvin cooling, neutron detection and lung imaging all want more than exists, which is exactly why the one company betting its reactor on ³He plans to breed its own rather than buy it.

Case study · the machine behind the ³He bet

Inside Helion's reactor

Helion's machine isn't a tokamak and it isn't a laser. It's a pulsed magneto-inertial device: it builds two rings of plasma, fires them together, compresses the result to fusion heat, and then harvests the energy directly as electricity — all in a flash, repeated about once a second. What makes it different

PULSED

Fires in short bursts ~once a second instead of holding a steady burn — so it never has to keep a star stable for hours.

DIRECT ELECTRICITY

The expanding plasma induces current in its own coils. No boiling water, no steam turbine — the reactor is its own generator.

SELF-BRED ³He

D-D side reactions make the helium-3 (plus tritium that decays to more), captured and fed back. Only deuterium is ever bought.

LINEAR FRC

A straight tube of self-confining plasma rings, not a doughnut — which is what lets it accelerate and collide them.

WHERE THEY ARE NOW · mid-2026

Polaris — the 7th-generation, ~60-ft prototype, running since late 2024. In early 2026 it became the first private machine to show measurable D-T fusion and hit 150 million °C — about 10× the Sun's core and roughly three-quarters of its commercial target.
Fuel-flexible — D-T was demonstrated as a stepping stone; reliable D-³He is the harder commercial goal it's now climbing toward.
Tiny Merge — a new ~⅛-scale testbed for fast, cheap iteration.

Orion — its first power plant, ~50 MW, under construction in Malaga, WA, tied to a deal to supply a Microsoft data center by 2028 — the most aggressive timeline in the industry.
Backing — chaired by Sam Altman; raised $465M in June 2026 at a $15.5B valuation, ~$1.5B total — now the #2 best-funded private fusion company.

The honest caveats: Helion needs plasma about twice as hot as a tokamak because ³He is hard to ignite, and it has not yet shown net electricity. Whether the direct-conversion efficiency, the reliable D-³He burn, and the ³He breeding rate all hold up at power-plant scale are still open questions — and the "aneutronic" label is only partial, since the D-D side reactions still leak some neutrons. It's a serious, exceptionally well-funded bet, not a finished result.

The supply question · make it, don't mine it

Could you just build a fuel factory?

Relying on power reactors for tritium is a bottleneck — so why not build dedicated plants whose only job is making fuel, as a clean, separate business? It's a real idea being designed right now. But it runs into one stubborn fact, and helium-3 is far harder than tritium.

The hard truths

AN ENERGY SINK

A fuel factory consumes energy to make fuel. It's only worth it because the fuel is precious — not because it's efficient.

THE DECAY CLOCK

Tritium fades ~5% a year, so you can't stockpile your way out. Production has to run continuously, forever.

THE PRICE

Dedicated fission tritium runs ~$200M per kg — and a 2-GW plant wants ~112 kg a year, versus ~25 kg total on Earth today.

THE BOOTSTRAP

Breeding only works once a plant is running. The very first plants still need borrowed tritium just to start.

IS IT THE FUTURE? · partly — and it's already starting

The master plan is borrow-then-breed: every D-T plant breeds its own tritium in a lithium blanket once it's running, so it never has to buy any. But lighting the first plants needs outside tritium — which is exactly why Los Alamos, the US weapons complex, and startups like Astral are racing to stand up dedicated production now. Dedicated factories are a real bridge for tritium.

For helium-3 it's different: there's no efficient way to make it at all. It stays a fusion byproduct (Helion breeding its own) or a far-off lunar resource. So fuel factories are a bridge for tritium — and a fundamental wall for He-3.

SO WHAT WAS "GOING ON" WITH ITER'S TUNGSTEN SWITCH?

Good catch — it is part of this story, just from the other side. ITER moved its first wall from beryllium to tungsten mainly because tungsten survives the heat far better and, crucially, retains much less tritium. Beryllium soaks tritium up and traps it in the wall — a safety hazard and a fuel-accounting nightmare when the whole planet holds only ~25 kg.

So the switch isn't a hidden factory plan; it's the same scarcity pressure from the opposite end. When tritium is this rare, you can't afford to lose it into your walls. The quiet truth running through this whole deck: the tritium economy — making it, breeding it, and not losing it — is the central engineering problem of D-T fusion. The wall material and the factory race are both answers to it.
