01 / what is a clock

A clock is just a thing that repeats.

Forget hands and digits for a second. Strip a clock down to its job: it does some identical thing over and over, and you count. A pendulum swinging. Your heart beating. An atom vibrating. Each repeat is one tick.

We're going to use a weird but honest clock: a pulse of light bouncing straight up and down between two mirrors. One round trip = one tick. Watch it.

A light pulse bounces between two mirrors. Each full up-and-down = one tick. 0 ticks so far.

Boring so far — on purpose. We picked light for one reason, and it's the strangest fact in physics. Next.

02 / the one weird rule

Light always moves at the same speed.

Here's the rule everything hangs on, and it's been tested to death: no matter how fast you're moving, you measure light going at the same speed — about 300,000 km/s, called c.

That sounds harmless. It is not. Throw a ball forward from a moving train and someone on the ground sees it go train speed + ball speed. Shine a flashlight forward from that train, and the ground observer measures the light at exactly c — not c + train speed. The speeds refuse to add up.

Something has to give to make that work. Speed is distance ÷ time. If everyone agrees on the speed of light but they're moving differently... then they can't all be agreeing on distance and time. Hold that thought and look at what it does to our bouncing clock.

03 / the payoff

A moving light clock ticks slow.

Two identical clocks. The left one sits still next to you. The right one flies past at speed v. Drag the slider and watch.

The catch: while the light in the moving clock travels up, the whole clock has slid sideways. So from your point of view the pulse can't go straight up — it has to travel a longer, slanted, zig-zag path to catch the mirror that moved. But light's speed is fixed at c. Longer path ÷ same speed = more time per tick. The moving clock falls behind.

04 / the actual math

It's just Pythagoras.

You've got calc 2 — this needs none of it, only a right triangle. Let the mirrors be a distance L apart. Drag the slider to set how fast the clock moves and watch the triangle that the light traces in your frame.

Light's path (the hypotenuse) is longer than the straight-up height L by exactly the factor γ.
·In the clock's own frame, light just goes straight up a height L. One tick takes:
t0 = Lc
·In your frame, during time t the clock slides sideways a distance v·t, so light travels the hypotenuse. Pythagoras:
(c·t)2 = L2 + (v·t)2
·Gather the t's:
c2t2 − v2t2 = L2  →  t2(c2−v2) = L2
·Solve for the moving-clock tick t, then divide top and bottom by c:
t = Lc2−v2  =  L/c1 − v2/c2  =  γ · t0
γ = 11 − v2/c2

That γ (gamma) is the whole story of motion-based time dilation. At everyday speeds v is tiny next to c, so v²/c² ≈ 0 and γ ≈ 1 — clocks basically agree, which is why you never notice. But as v → c, the bottom shrinks toward zero and γ blows up. The moving clock's tick takes γ times longer. Nothing broke the clock. The geometry of "same speed of light for everyone" just forced time to stretch.

05 / now gravity

Gravity is acceleration in disguise.

That handled motion. Now your original question: why does gravity slow time? Einstein's move is sneaky and beautiful. He said: standing still in gravity is physically indistinguishable from accelerating in a rocket in empty space. Feet pressed to the floor either way — you can't tell which from inside a sealed room.

So let's solve the easy one (the rocket) and then just rename it "gravity." A rocket accelerates upward. A light pulse leaves the floor heading for the ceiling. By the time it arrives, the ceiling has sped up and is racing away from where the light started — so the ceiling catches the light stretched out, redshifted, at a lower frequency.

Wave leaves the floor tight, arrives at the ceiling stretched. Frequency dropped → the ceiling sees the floor's clock running slow.

Here's the punchline. A wave's frequency is a clock — so many crests per second. If the ceiling receives fewer crests per second than the floor sent, the ceiling concludes the floor's clock is ticking slow. Now swap the rocket for gravity (they're the same):

Lower in gravity

Deeper in the well — sea level, the floor, near a heavy mass. Time runs slower here.

Higher up

Up a mountain, in orbit, far from mass. Time runs faster here, relative to below.

And exactly like before: it's not that the atoms get tired or sluggish. Everything down there keeps perfect step — heartbeat, atoms, light, aging — all running on whatever the local rate of time happens to be. You only catch the difference when you compare a low clock to a high one.

06 / it's real

This runs in your pocket.

None of this is a thought experiment you have to take on trust. The clocks on GPS satellites sit higher in Earth's gravity and zip around fast, so both effects hit them. Engineers don't just hope it works — they pre-correct the satellite clocks every single day for it.

~38 microseconds / day

That's how much faster GPS clocks tick than ground clocks, once you add gravity's speed-up to motion's slow-down. Ignore it and your phone's "you are here" dot drifts off by about 10 km after one day. The blue dot stays accurate because someone did this exact calculation. Atomic clocks are now so precise they measure the difference over a height change of a single foot.

The whole thing in one breath: a clock is anything that repeats. Light moves at one fixed speed for everyone. To keep that true, moving clocks and clocks deep in gravity must run slow — by the factor γ for motion, and by an analogous factor for gravity. Gravity doesn't drag on atoms; it bends the rate of time itself, and everything dutifully keeps step.
