The Three Directions in a Tokamak

How radial (x), parallel (z), and binormal (y) are defined — and what the magnetic field has to do with it

Interactive · A point on a flux surface with all three coordinate directions

All three directions are defined relative to the magnetic field at each point. You don't pick them freely — the field geometry determines them:

The three directions

Radial (x): Points outward from the magnetic axis toward the wall. Perpendicular to the flux surface. This is where the temperature gradient lives, and where heat escapes. You choose "outward" — that's the one free choice.

Along B (z): Follows the magnetic field line as it spirals helically around the torus. This direction is set entirely by the field — wherever B points, that's z. Particles stream freely along z.

Binormal (y): Whatever's left. Perpendicular to both B and the radial direction, lying in the flux surface. You don't choose it — it's forced by the other two. It's the direction "along the contour" of the flux surface, perpendicular to the field line. The turbulent eddies have their perpendicular structure in this direction.

Animated · Following a field line — watch how the three directions rotate

As you travel along the field line (blue), the binormal (green) rotates because the field line is helical — it twists as it goes around the torus. The radial direction (red) always points outward. The binormal is always perpendicular to both. At every point, the three directions form a local coordinate frame that rides along with the field.

Animated · Cross-section view — binormal wraps around, radial doesn't

In the cross-section, the binormal direction goes around the flux surface (green dot circling). Walk far enough and you're back where you started — periodic. The radial direction points across flux surfaces (red arrow) — from hot core to cool edge. No amount of walking in x brings you back. The field line (blue) goes into the screen at an angle because it's spiraling helically.
