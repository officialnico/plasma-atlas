Introduction to Gyrokinetics

From a spinning particle to the gyrokinetic equation, step by step

Part I

A Charged Particle in a Magnetic Field

Before gyrokinetics makes sense, you need to understand the motion it's simplifying. A single charged particle (say, an ion) placed in a uniform magnetic field does something very specific: it spirals.

The three motions

The spiral can be decomposed into independent pieces:

1. Gyration (the spin). The magnetic force is always perpendicular to the particle's velocity. This doesn't speed the particle up or slow it down — it bends its path into a circle. The particle orbits around the magnetic field line with a characteristic radius called the Larmor radius (ρ) and a characteristic frequency called the cyclotron frequency (Ω). For a deuterium ion in a tokamak, ρ is a few millimeters and Ω is about 100 million revolutions per second. This gyration is fast.

2. Parallel streaming. The magnetic force only acts on the component of velocity perpendicular to the field. The component along the field — v∥ — is completely unaffected. The particle slides freely along the field line at whatever v∥ it started with. So the total motion is: slide forward while spinning → a helix.

3. Drifts. When the magnetic field isn't perfectly uniform (and in a tokamak it never is), the center of the orbit slowly drifts sideways. These drifts are much slower than both the gyration and the parallel streaming. They're important for transport, but they're the slowest timescale.

Intuition Imagine throwing a football with a tight spiral. The football spins fast (gyration), moves forward (parallel streaming), and if there's a crosswind, it slowly curves sideways (drift). Three motions at three very different speeds.

The Larmor radius

The radius of the circular gyration orbit is:

Larmor radius ρ = mv⊥ / (qB)

where m is the particle mass, v⊥ is its perpendicular speed, q is its charge, and B is the magnetic field strength. Heavier particles, faster perpendicular speeds, or weaker magnetic fields all give bigger circles. For ions in a tokamak, ρ ≈ a few mm. For electrons, it's about 60× smaller (because electrons are much lighter).

The magnetic moment μ — and why it changes everything

A particle's total kinetic energy has two parts: the energy of its motion along B (½mv∥²), and the energy of its spin around B (½mv⊥²). We can ask: as the particle moves through a tokamak — where B varies from place to place — what happens to these two energies?

It turns out that nature enforces a strict rule. Define:

Magnetic moment μ = mv⊥² / (2B)

This ratio — spin energy divided by field strength — is almost exactly constant as the particle moves. This is called an adiabatic invariant, and it has units of energy per tesla (joules/T). It holds as long as B doesn't change much over one orbit — and in a tokamak, B varies over meters while the orbit is millimeters, so μ is conserved to extreme precision.

What happens when B changes: the energy trade-off

Here's where it gets interesting. Since total kinetic energy is conserved (the magnetic force does no work — it only bends paths, never speeds particles up or slows them down overall), we have:

Total kinetic energy (constant) E_total = ½mv⊥² + ½mv∥² = μB + ½mv∥²

Both E_total and μ are constant. So if B increases, the spin energy μB must increase — and the only place that energy can come from is the streaming energy ½mv∥². The particle slows down along the field line to pay for spinning faster.

And here's the same thing from the side — looking at the helix along the field line. Watch how the coils get tighter and faster as B increases (right), and the forward speed v∥ drops:

Animated · Side view — helix tightens in stronger B, particle slows along field line

Watch both readout panels: as B increases (right side), spin energy ½mv⊥² goes up, streaming energy ½mv∥² goes down by exactly the same amount, and the total stays locked. The orbit tightens (smaller ρ) but the particle spins faster (higher v⊥).

Why ρ shrinks even though v⊥ increases

This might seem contradictory: the particle spins faster, but the orbit gets smaller? The key is that ρ = mv⊥/(qB). When B doubles, v⊥ increases by a factor of √2 (to keep μ constant), but the denominator doubled. Since √2 < 2, the net effect is that ρ shrinks. The stronger magnetic field bends the path more tightly than the extra speed can compensate for.

The magnetic mirror: when the particle bounces back

Take the energy trade-off to its logical extreme. If the particle keeps moving into stronger and stronger B, eventually all of its streaming energy has been converted into spin energy: ½mv∥² → 0. At that point v∥ = 0 — the particle has stopped moving along the field line. It then reverses direction and streams back the way it came. The particle "bounces" off the strong-B region like a ball hitting a wall.

This is the magnetic mirror effect, and it's real. In a tokamak, B is stronger on the inboard side (closer to the center of the donut) than the outboard side. Some particles — the ones with high μ relative to their total energy — get trapped bouncing back and forth between the strong-B regions. These are called trapped particles, and they make up a significant fraction of the plasma.

Animated · Magnetic mirror — watch v∥ decelerate, stop, and reverse

Focus on the v∥ arrow (blue). In the weak-B center, the particle is streaming fast — long arrow. As it enters strong B on either side, v∥ shrinks because energy is transferring into the spin. At the red mirror lines, v∥ hits exactly zero — the particle has momentarily stopped moving along the field line. Then it reverses and accelerates back toward the center.

The bottom graph plots v∥ vs. position in real time. Notice it's not the gyration reversing — the spin always goes the same direction. It's the streaming along B that reverses. The "acceleration" you see isn't from a traditional force — it's the magnetic mirror force: −μ∇B. Where B is increasing (approaching the mirror point), this force pushes the particle back. Where B is decreasing (heading toward the center), the force lets it speed up again.

Where is the particle fastest vs slowest? Fastest v∥ (streaming along B): at the center, where B is weakest and the least energy is locked in spinning.
Slowest v∥ (zero!): at the mirror points, where B is strong enough that all streaming energy has been converted to spin.
Fastest v⊥ (spinning): at the mirror points, where B is strongest.
Slowest v⊥ (spinning): at the center, where B is weakest.
The gyration direction never changes — the particle always orbits the same way. Only the forward/backward streaming reverses.
The figure skater analogy (expanded) A figure skater spinning with arms out has low spin speed but a large "orbit." When she pulls her arms in (entering a stronger "constraint"), she spins much faster but in a tighter space. Her total rotational energy is conserved. If she were sliding across the ice while spinning, pulling in her arms would steal energy from her sliding motion — she'd slow down. Pull in far enough, and she stops sliding entirely and reverses. That's the magnetic mirror.

Why all of this matters for the simulation

Every one of these effects — the orbit tightening, the speed trade-off, the particle trapping — is handled automatically by one number: μ. If you know a particle's μ and its position (which tells you B), you immediately know its v⊥, its ρ, and its spin energy. You never need to simulate any of it.

This is the core payoff: μ being constant means the gyration is on autopilot. A gyrokinetic code like GENE just stores μ as a fixed label for each class of particles, and everything about their spin follows from that label plus the local B. The only things left to actually solve for are the guiding center position and v∥. That's what takes 6 dimensions down to effectively 4 — and that reduction is the entire basis for gyrokinetics.

Connection to trapped particles in GENE Trapped particles (the ones that mirror-bounce) behave very differently from passing particles (the ones with enough v∥ to make it all the way around). GENE captures this distinction naturally: for each μ value on the grid, some v∥ values correspond to trapped orbits and some to passing orbits. The Vlasov equation handles both — no special treatment needed. This is one of the things gyrokinetics gets right that simpler fluid models miss entirely.
Part II

The Problem with Full Particle Simulations

Suppose you want to simulate a tokamak plasma directly — track every particle's position and velocity, update them with the full equations of motion. This is called a full-orbit or particle-in-cell approach. Why not just do this?

The timescale problem

Fusion turbulence evolves on timescales of microseconds to milliseconds. But the ion cyclotron frequency is ~10⁸ Hz, meaning the gyration completes a full circle in ~10 nanoseconds. To resolve the gyration, your simulation timestep must be a fraction of that — say, 1 nanosecond.

To simulate 1 millisecond of turbulence at 1 ns timesteps, you need one million timesteps, and at each one you're resolving motion that has essentially no effect on the turbulence. You're spending 99.99% of your computation tracking an irrelevant fast oscillation.

Electron gyration~0.1 nsEven faster, even less relevant
Turbulence evolution~1–100 μsThis is what we care about
Transport (energy confinement)~0.1–1 sThe big-picture question
Animated · Full-orbit simulation (left) vs Gyrokinetic simulation (right)

On the left, the full-orbit approach must track every tiny gyration — thousands of expensive timesteps just to move the particle slightly along the field line. On the right, gyrokinetics skips the spin entirely and just moves the guiding center, covering the same physics in a fraction of the computational steps.

The bottom line Full-orbit simulation of turbulence in a real tokamak is computationally impossible with current (or foreseeable) hardware. You must average over the gyration. This averaging is gyrokinetics.
Part III

The Gyrokinetic Idea

The core insight of gyrokinetics is beautifully simple:

If μ is constant, the exact position of the particle on its gyration circle doesn't matter. Average over it.

Instead of tracking a point particle spiraling along a field line, you track a charged ring — the orbit itself, centered on a point called the guiding center. The ring has radius ρ, carries the particle's charge, and drifts through the plasma. But it doesn't spin in the simulation — the spin has been analytically removed.

What you gain

  1. One fewer dimension. The gyration angle (call it α) is gone. Your distribution function f no longer depends on it. In full kinetics, f depends on 6 variables (3 position + 3 velocity). In gyrokinetics, it depends on 5: the guiding center position (3 coordinates, though in practice you exploit the tokamak geometry to reduce this further), v∥, and μ. Since μ is constant, it's really just a parameter — you solve a separate equation for each μ value.
  2. Larger timesteps. You no longer need to resolve the cyclotron period. Your timestep is set by the turbulence timescale, which is thousands of times longer. This alone gives you a speedup of ~10³–10⁴.
  3. Coarser grids (perpendicular). You don't need to resolve the sub-Larmor-radius structure of the orbit. Your perpendicular grid spacing can be comparable to ρ rather than much smaller.
Technical note The averaging procedure is formally done via a Lie transform perturbation theory, order by order in the small parameter ε = ρ/L, where L is the macroscopic scale length. At zeroth order you recover the guiding center. At first order you get the gyrokinetic equation with FLR corrections — "FLR" stands for Finite Larmor Radius, meaning effects that arise because the gyration ring has a real physical size, not just a point. When turbulent structures are small enough to be comparable to the ring, different parts of the ring feel different forces, and things partially cancel out. This is covered in detail in Part VI below. The key point here: this averaging is mathematically rigorous — it's not just "ignore the spin."
Part IV

The Guiding Center & Drifts

The guiding center is the center of the gyration circle. As the particle spirals, the guiding center traces out a smooth path — it's the "average position" of the particle, stripped of the fast wobble.

In a uniform magnetic field, the guiding center just moves at v∥ along the field line. In a non-uniform field (like a tokamak), the guiding center additionally drifts perpendicular to the field. The main drifts are:

The ∇B drift: if the magnetic field is stronger on one side of the orbit than the other, the orbit is tighter on the strong-field side and wider on the weak-field side. This asymmetry causes a net drift perpendicular to both B and ∇B. Crucially, the drift direction depends on the charge sign — ions drift one way, electrons the other — regardless of where the particle is in the plasma. In a tokamak cross-section (with B stronger on the inboard side), ions drift downward and electrons drift upward.

This has nothing to do with gravity — gravitational forces are negligibly weak compared to electromagnetic forces at fusion temperatures. The drift happens because the lopsided orbit doesn't close into a symmetric circle. Each time the particle completes one orbit, it ends up slightly displaced. Which direction it displaces depends on which way the particle goes around — clockwise (ions) or counter-clockwise (electrons) — because the Lorentz force flips sign with charge.

The car on ice analogy Imagine driving a car where the left tires are on ice (low grip = weak B, wide arc) and the right tires are on asphalt (high grip = strong B, tight arc). You'll veer in a specific direction. A car driving the same loop in reverse would veer the other way. Ions and electrons "drive around the orbit in opposite directions" — so they drift opposite ways.
Animated · ∇B drift — orbit is lopsided in a non-uniform field
Why this charge separation is a huge problem If ions always drift down and electrons always drift up, positive charge piles up at the bottom and negative charge at the top. This creates a vertical electric field. That E field, combined with B, produces an E×B drift that pushes the entire plasma — ions and electrons together — outward into the wall. This is why you can't confine plasma with a simple toroidal magnetic field (a solenoid bent into a donut). The charge separation destroys confinement in microseconds.
Animated · The charge separation problem — and how the helical twist fixes it

The fix: helical field lines. In a tokamak, the magnetic field lines don't just go straight around the torus — they twist helically, winding around the short way (poloidally) as they go around the long way (toroidally). A field line that's at the top at one location is at the bottom half a turn later. Since particles stream freely along field lines, an ion that drifted down at the top gets carried to the bottom, where it drifts down some more but is now below the midplane heading back up via the field line twist. The charge separation gets short-circuited before it can build up. This helical twist — the rotational transform — is literally the reason tokamaks work.

The curvature drift: if the field line is curved (and in a torus, they all are), the particle's parallel motion along the curved line creates a centrifugal-like effect that drifts the guiding center sideways. This drift also separates charges just like ∇B drift (same direction, same problem), and the helical fix handles both.

Animated · Curvature drift — particle follows curved field line

The E×B drift: if there's an electric field, all particles — regardless of charge, mass, or speed — drift in the direction perpendicular to both E and B. This is the most important drift for turbulence, because the turbulent electric potential creates E×B flows that stir the plasma.

Animated · E×B drift — electric field pushes the orbit sideways

The combined picture

In a real tokamak, all three drifts act simultaneously. The ∇B and curvature drifts push the guiding center vertically (and depend on the particle's charge sign — ions go one way, electrons the other). The E×B drift pushes everything sideways, and it's the one that varies with the turbulence. The result is a complex trajectory:

Animated · All drifts combined — guiding center trajectory in a tokamak cross-section

The circular poloidal motion comes from the ∇B + curvature drifts (which are always present). The erratic sideways kicks come from the E×B drift driven by turbulent fluctuations. It's these E×B kicks that cause the radial transport GENE is trying to predict.

Intuition The guiding center is like the GPS tracker on a dog that's running in circles on a leash. The dog (the particle) spirals madly, but the tracker (the guiding center) moves smoothly down the sidewalk. Gyrokinetics says: just track the GPS dot.
Part IV-B

Turbulent Eddies & Radial Transport

Before we move to the equations, here's what the turbulence actually looks like in the plane perpendicular to B. Eddies — swirling vortex structures — form due to the steep temperature gradient between the hot core and the cool edge. They kick hot blobs of plasma radially outward, carrying heat toward the wall.

Animated · Turbulent eddies in the perpendicular plane

This radial transport of heat is the problem fusion scientists are trying to understand and minimize. Everything that follows — the Vlasov equation, the self-consistent loop, Landau damping — exists to predict how much heat these eddies carry out.

Part V

The Gyrokinetic Equation

Now we can write the equation that GENE actually solves. It's a Vlasov equation — but before the equation itself, let's understand what it's saying.

What is the distribution function f?

Imagine phase space: a space where every point represents a particle with a specific position and velocity. The distribution function f(x, z, ky, v∥, μ, t) tells you the particle density at each point in this 5D space at a given time. Where f is large, particles are crowded. Where f is small, they're sparse. It's a density map across position and velocity simultaneously.

What does f actually return?

A single number — the particle density at that specific combination of coordinates. It's always positive, and technically never exactly zero (a Gaussian bell curve asymptotically approaches zero but never reaches it). In practice, f ranges from large values at the center of the distribution to vanishingly tiny values in the tails:

Example return values for different inputs:

f(x=5, z=12, ky=3, v∥=0.0, μ=1, t=0)  = 0.8524    ← peak: many particles barely moving
f(x=5, z=12, ky=3, v∥=1.0, μ=1, t=0)  = 0.5134    ← moderate: some particles at this speed
f(x=5, z=12, ky=3, v∥=2.5, μ=1, t=0)  = 0.0439    ← tail: few particles this fast
f(x=5, z=12, ky=3, v∥=4.0, μ=1, t=0)  = 0.00003   ← deep tail: essentially zero

Same position, different time (after turbulence develops):

f(x=5, z=12, ky=3, v∥=0.0, μ=1, t=500) = 0.8491    ← slightly changed by turbulence
f(x=5, z=12, ky=3, v∥=1.0, μ=1, t=500) = 0.5208    ← turbulent fluctuations shifted f here

The function never returns zero — it returns smaller and smaller
positive numbers as you go further into the velocity tails.
Interactive · Phase space is NOT real space — it has velocity as an axis

This is the key conceptual shift: v∥ is a grid axis, just like position. GENE has grid points in v∥ ranging from negative (streaming one direction) to positive (streaming the other). Each grid point represents a population of particles at that particular speed. The distribution function f is defined at every combination of (position, v∥, μ) — and GENE must update f at ALL of those grid points every timestep.

Interactive · The 5D grid GENE actually computes on

This is why it's so computationally expensive: ~170 million grid points, all updated every timestep. It really is brute force across phase space. There are no "walls" in velocity space — a particle can have any v∥ or μ. You just make the velocity grid large enough that f has dropped to essentially zero at the edges (the tails of the Maxwellian). In position space, GENE typically simulates a thin radial slice called a flux tube with periodic boundary conditions, not the entire tokamak. Within the simulation domain, every coordinate combination is valid — you're right about that.

What one timestep actually looks like (Python pseudocode)

At its core, yes — it really is nested loops over every grid point. Here's a simplified version of what GENE does each timestep:

# The distribution function: a 5D array
# f[x, z, ky, vpar, mu] = number of particles at this point in phase space
# Most values are small but nonzero. At the velocity tails, f ≈ 0.

import numpy as np

# Grid sizes (typical GENE run)
nx    = 128   # radial positions
nz    = 48    # points along field line
nky   = 24    # perpendicular Fourier modes
nvpar = 48    # parallel velocity grid (negative to positive)
nmu   = 16    # magnetic moment values (each one is a fixed label)

# Initialize f as a Maxwellian (most particles near v∥=0, few in tails)
f = np.zeros((nx, nz, nky, nvpar, nmu))
for iv in range(nvpar):
    vpar = -3.0 + iv * 6.0 / nvpar   # v∥ from -3 to +3 (normalized)
    f[:, :, :, iv, :] = np.exp(-vpar**2 / 2)  # Gaussian: big at 0, tiny at ±3

# Timestep (set by turbulence, NOT gyration — that's the whole point)
dt = 1e-6  # ~1 microsecond

for timestep in range(100000):

    # ── Step 1: Compute φ from f ──
    # Integrate f over all velocities to get charge density at each spatial point
    charge_density = np.sum(f, axis=(3, 4))  # sum over v∥ and μ → shape (nx, nz, nky)

    # Solve Poisson-like equation for the electrostatic potential
    phi = solve_poisson(charge_density)  # φ at each spatial point

    # ── Step 2: Compute drifts from φ ──
    ExB_drift = compute_ExB(phi)   # E×B drift velocity (turbulence-driven)
    gradB_drift = compute_gradB()  # ∇B + curvature drifts (steady)

    # ── Step 3: Update f at every grid point ──
    for imu in range(nmu):            # μ is just a parameter (constant)
        for iky in range(nky):
            for iz in range(nz):
                for ix in range(nx):
                    for iv in range(nvpar):

                        # How fast is f flowing through this cell?
                        # Spatial flow: drifts move f in the x and z directions
                        spatial = (ExB_drift[ix,iz,iky] + gradB_drift[ix,iz]) \
                                  * gradient(f, ix, iz, iky, iv, imu)

                        # Velocity flow: mirror force + E∥ shift f in the v∥ direction
                        vpar_accel = mirror_force(iz, imu) + E_parallel(ix, iz, iky)
                        velocity = vpar_accel * df_dvpar(f, ix, iz, iky, iv, imu)

                        # Update: new f = old f - (outflow) × dt
                        f[ix, iz, iky, iv, imu] -= (spatial + velocity) * dt

    # ── Step 4: Measure turbulent fluxes ──
    # Correlate density fluctuations with E×B drift → heat transport
    heat_flux[timestep] = compute_flux(f, phi)

# Done! heat_flux tells you how fast turbulence leaks energy out.
What's happening in plain english Every timestep: (1) add up all the particles at each spatial point to find the charge density, (2) use that to compute the electric potential φ, (3) use φ to figure out the drifts, (4) update every single grid point of f based on how fast stuff is flowing through it, (5) repeat. After enough timesteps, measure the average heat flux — that's the answer.
Reality check Real GENE doesn't literally use Python for-loops — it uses Fortran/C with MPI parallelism across thousands of CPU cores, and the inner loops are replaced with vectorized array operations. The logic is the same, but running 170 million grid points × 100,000 timesteps requires thousands of CPU-hours on a supercomputer.

What does "particles are conserved" mean?

The Vlasov equation says: particles don't appear, disappear, or teleport. They flow continuously through phase space. If a region gains particles, it's because they flowed in from a neighboring region. If it loses them, they flowed out. That's it. It's a continuity equation for the density f — exactly like saying "the amount of water in any section of a river changes only because water flows in or out, not because water is created or destroyed."

Gyrokinetic Vlasov equation (schematic) ∂f/∂t + ẋ·∇f + v̇∥ · ∂f/∂v∥ = 0

Each term:

∂f/∂t — how the distribution function changes in time at a fixed point in phase space.

ẋ·∇f — advection of f by the guiding center velocity ẋ. This velocity includes the parallel streaming (v∥ along the field) and the perpendicular drifts (∇B drift, curvature drift, and the all-important E×B drift driven by the turbulent potential φ). This is what moves particles through real space.

v̇∥ · ∂f/∂v∥ — acceleration along the field line. Forces parallel to B (like the mirror force from a non-uniform B, or the parallel electric field) change v∥ and shift particles in velocity space.

= 0 — no sources or sinks. The right side is zero because this is a collisionless equation.

Do particles collide? Why is the right side zero?

In the basic Vlasov equation — no collisions. This is actually a good approximation for fusion plasmas: the particles are so hot and fast that they rarely get close enough to deflect each other significantly. The mean free path (average distance between collisions) in a tokamak core is hundreds of meters to kilometers — much longer than the machine itself. The turbulence moves heat vastly faster than collisions do.

When collisions do matter (for resistivity, long-timescale transport, or velocity-space diffusion), you add a collision operator to the right side: = C(f) instead of = 0. GENE can include this, but the dominant turbulent transport it studies is mostly collisionless.

The crucial point Notice what's missing: there is no ∂f/∂μ term and no gyration angle. μ is constant (so f doesn't flow in the μ direction), and the angle has been averaged out. These are the two simplifications that make gyrokinetics possible.

The self-consistent loop

The turbulent electrostatic potential φ enters through the E×B drift velocity in the ẋ·∇f term. This creates a feedback loop: f determines the charge density, which determines φ, which drives drifts, which change f.

This loop runs every timestep. Turbulence emerges from the interplay.

Part V-B

Fourier Decomposition & k⊥

GENE works in Fourier space for the perpendicular directions. Any turbulent pattern — no matter how messy — can be decomposed into a sum of simple sine waves at different wavenumbers k. Toggle the modes below to see how they combine:

Interactive · Toggle Fourier modes on/off

Why Fourier for this direction? Periodicity.

GENE uses Fourier space for the binormal direction because it's periodic — if you walk along it on a flux surface, you come back to where you started. But not every direction is periodic:

Animated · Why binormal is periodic but radial is not

The radial direction has a real gradient — the core is hot and dense, the edge is cool and sparse. Wrapping it periodically would mean "the core is the same as the edge," which is physically wrong. The binormal direction has no such gradient — the turbulence pattern just repeats around the flux surface. That's why sine waves (which inherently repeat) are the perfect basis for the binormal direction, and GENE uses ky to label them.

Higher k means more oscillations per unit length — smaller spatial structures. k⊥ specifically refers to the wavenumber in the direction perpendicular to the magnetic field. Since turbulent eddies are elongated along B but structured across it, k⊥ is the quantity that distinguishes one eddy size from another.

Part VI

The Gyro-Average & FLR Effects

This is where gyrokinetics gets its most distinctive feature. The particle is a ring of radius ρ. When a fluctuating field φ(x) acts on the ring, each point on the ring samples a different value of φ. The effective potential the guiding center feels is the average over all those samples:

Gyro-average ⟨φ⟩ = (1/2π) ∮ φ(X + ρ) dα
Interactive · Drag the slider to change wavelength
Long wavelength Short wavelength

Green dots on the ring see positive φ, red dots see negative. At low k⊥ρ, the entire ring sees nearly the same value — it acts like a point particle. Drag the slider right: now different parts of the ring see opposite signs, and they cancel. This is the Finite Larmor Radius (FLR) effect.

What this does in Fourier space

If φ is a single Fourier mode with perpendicular wavenumber k⊥, then the ring average produces:

Gyro-average of a Fourier mode ⟨φk⊥⟩ = J₀(k⊥ρ) · φk⊥

J₀ is the zeroth-order Bessel function of the first kind. It starts at 1 (no FLR effect) and decays as k⊥ρ grows:

Regimek⊥ρJ₀Physical meaning
Eddy ≫ ring≪ 1≈ 1Acts like a point particle
Eddy ~ ring~ 1~ 0.5–0.8Partial cancellation
Eddy ≪ ring≫ 1→ 0Ring averages the wave away

Intuition Imagine standing in the ocean with a hula hoop. Long ocean swells (large wavelength, small k⊥) lift the entire hoop uniformly — you feel the full wave. Short choppy waves (small wavelength, large k⊥) hit different parts of the hoop at different phases — they cancel out and the hoop barely moves. The hoop is the gyration orbit. The cancellation is the Bessel function.
Part VI-B

Landau Damping

Turbulence is driven by instabilities, but damping mechanisms absorb energy and limit how large the turbulence grows. The most important one in gyrokinetics is Landau damping — and it does not involve particles colliding.

A turbulent wave has a phase velocity — the speed at which the wave pattern moves along the field line. Particles streaming at v∥ near this phase velocity "surf" the wave and exchange energy with it.

Animated · Wave-particle resonance (no collisions)

Particles just slower than the wave (green) gain energy from it — they speed up. Particles just faster (red) lose energy to it — they slow down. Because the distribution function f(v∥) is a decreasing function at the wave's phase velocity (the slope df/dv∥ is negative there), there are more slow particles than fast ones. So more energy flows from wave to particles than the other way around. The wave damps — purely through this resonance, with zero collisions.

Part VII

The Coordinate System GENE Uses

Along the field line: a coordinate z (or sometimes θ or s) that parameterizes position along the magnetic field line. This is where parallel dynamics (streaming, Landau damping) happen.

Perpendicular to the field (radial): GENE uses a radial coordinate x, solved on a grid.

Perpendicular to the field (binormal): GENE works in Fourier space for this direction, using the wavenumber ky. This is one component of k⊥.

Velocity space: v∥ is on a grid. μ is on a grid. Each (v∥, μ) pair represents a class of particles.

Dimensions in GENE A typical GENE simulation grid might be: 128 (radial x) × 48 (parallel z) × 24 (ky modes) × 48 (v∥) × 16 (μ). That's about 170 million grid points — a 5D problem. This is why even the gyrokinetic simplification requires supercomputers.
Part VIII

What Gyrokinetics Keeps and What It Throws Away

Kept

Kinetic effects. The distribution function f can have any shape in v∥ — it doesn't have to be Maxwellian. This captures Landau damping, trapped particle effects, and velocity-space instabilities.

FLR effects. The finite size of the gyration orbit is retained through the Bessel functions.

Electromagnetic fluctuations (if desired). GENE can solve for fluctuations in the magnetic field too, not just φ.

Thrown away

The cyclotron frequency. Phenomena at or near the cyclotron frequency (like ICRH) need a different model.

Large fluctuations. The gyrokinetic ordering assumes δn/n ≪ 1. Edge-localized modes (ELMs) can break this.

The key trade-off Gyrokinetics gives up cyclotron-frequency phenomena and large perturbations. In exchange, it gains a computationally tractable simulation that retains kinetic physics (Landau damping, trapped particles, FLR) that fluid models miss. For core turbulence, this trade-off is overwhelmingly favorable.
Summary

The Gyrokinetic Chain of Logic

  1. Charged particles spiral in magnetic fields. The motion decomposes into fast gyration, parallel streaming, and slow drifts.
  2. The magnetic moment μ is conserved. The energy in the gyration is a constant of motion (to excellent approximation).
  3. Full-orbit simulation is too expensive. Resolving the cyclotron timescale for turbulence simulations is computationally prohibitive.
  4. Average over the gyration angle. Replace the point particle with a charged ring (guiding center + Larmor radius). Drop the angle α from the distribution function.
  5. Write the Vlasov equation for the guiding center. f(X, v∥, μ, t) evolves under parallel streaming, perpendicular drifts (including the turbulence-driven E×B drift), and parallel forces.
  6. The gyro-average produces Bessel functions. The ring "sees" a smoothed version of the fluctuating potential. In Fourier space, this smoothing is multiplication by J₀(k⊥ρ), encoding FLR effects.
  7. Self-consistent loop. f determines charge density → solve for φ → φ drives E×B drifts → drifts advect f → repeat. Turbulence emerges; measure the resulting fluxes.
One sentence Gyrokinetics is what you get when you realize the spin doesn't matter, average it out, and keep everything else.
