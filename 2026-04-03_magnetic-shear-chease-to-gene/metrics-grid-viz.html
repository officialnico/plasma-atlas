The left grid is what GENE "thinks" the world looks like — uniform squares where x (radial) and y (binormal) are nice and even. The right grid is what those same squares actually map to in real physical space, after applying the metric coefficients. The warping, shearing, and stretching you see on the right is exactly what g^xx, g^xy, and g^yy encode.

What each coefficient does to the grid

g^xx EFFECTRADIAL STRETCH
g^yy EFFECTBINORMAL STRETCH
g^xy EFFECT — THE SHEAR ONESKEW / TILT

g^xx stretches or compresses the grid radially (horizontally). On the outboard side, flux surfaces are closer together, so g^xx is larger — 1 unit of GENE's x maps to more real distance.

g^yy stretches the grid in the binormal (vertical) direction. It grows along the field line, partly because of shear.

g^xy is the one that tilts/skews the grid. This is magnetic shear showing up geometrically. At the outboard midplane (z=0) it's zero — the grid is upright. As you move along z, the grid tilts more and more. Crank the shear slider and watch the tilt grow. This is literally why shear tears apart turbulent eddies — it distorts them.

Watch it change along the field line

Drag the z position slider above and watch ALL the grids change. At z = 0 (outboard midplane), the real-space grid is relatively square. As you move toward z = ±π (inboard), g^xy kicks in and tilts everything. The grid gets progressively more sheared — that's the field lines on neighboring surfaces twisting apart from each other.

Summary: The metrics are a lookup table that says "at this z-position along the field line, here's how distorted GENE's nice uniform grid is relative to reality." GENE needs this to compute every spatial derivative correctly. Without it, a turbulent eddy that's circular in GENE-coordinates would actually be stretched and tilted in real space — and GENE wouldn't know.
