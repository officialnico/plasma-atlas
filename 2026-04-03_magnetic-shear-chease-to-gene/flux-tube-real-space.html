What you're seeing: The thin band of purple grid cells is GENE's flux tube — it wraps around the flux surface following a field line. The grid cells are GENE's computational boxes. Notice how they tilt and deform as you go around the surface. At the outboard midplane (right side) the cells are relatively upright. Moving toward the top and inboard side, shear tilts them progressively. That tilt IS g^xy — the metric cross-term — showing up in real space.

Top: GENE's internal world — a perfect rectangle. z (along the field line) on the horizontal axis, x (radial) on the vertical. Every cell is the same size. This is where GENE solves its equations.

Bottom: What that rectangle maps to in real space after applying g^xx, g^xy, g^yy at each z-position. The cells stretch radially where g^xx is large, tilt where g^xy is nonzero, and stretch binormally where g^yy is large. The parallelogram tilt that grows from the center outward is magnetic shear — that's g^xy doing its thing.

Why This Matters

When GENE computes "how far apart are two grid points?" it can't just subtract their coordinates — the answer depends on where along z you are. At the outboard midplane, 1 unit of x might equal 2 cm. At the inboard side, 1 unit of x might equal 3 cm. And the x and y directions might not even be perpendicular anymore because of g^xy.

The metrics handle all of this. Every time GENE takes a spatial derivative (which it does millions of times per timestep), it multiplies by the appropriate metric coefficient to convert from "coordinate distance" to "real distance." Without this correction, GENE would think turbulent eddies are a different size and shape than they actually are — and the whole simulation would be wrong.

The punchline: g^xx, g^xy, g^yy are a lookup table indexed by z that says "at this point along the field line, here's how to convert my nice uniform grid into the actual curved, twisted, stretched reality of the tokamak geometry."
