|B| and the Metric Coefficients

Field strength tells GENE how strong the magnetic field is at each point. The metrics tell it how to measure real distances in its weird coordinate system.

What |B| is

|B| is just how strong the magnetic field is at each point along the field line. It's not constant — it's stronger on the inboard side and weaker on the outboard side of the torus.

🧲

Why it varies: the 1/R effect

The toroidal field is created by external magnets. On the inboard side you're closer to the center of the torus (smaller R), so the field is stronger — it goes roughly as 1/R. On the outboard side, bigger R, weaker field. This is fundamental to how a tokamak works.

Why GENE cares about |B|

Particle trapping

Particles bouncing between the strong-field (inboard) and weak-field (outboard) sides get "trapped" — they can't make it past the high-|B| region. The |B| profile determines where particles get trapped and how deep the magnetic well is. Trapped particles drive Trapped Electron Modes (TEM), a major turbulence source.

Mirror force

The variation in |B| creates a force on particles along the field line — like a magnetic mirror. Particles moving toward stronger |B| get pushed back. GENE needs the exact |B|(z) profile to compute this force correctly.

Drift velocities

The gradient of |B| causes particles to drift perpendicular to the field (∇B drift). This drift combines with the curvature drift and together they drive turbulence on the outboard side. GENE computes both from the |B| profile.

It's simple but critical

|B| is probably the easiest geometry quantity to understand — strong on inside, weak on outside. But it enters almost every equation GENE solves. Get it wrong and particle orbits, trapping, drifts, and the effective mass of fluctuations are all off.

Compared to curvature

|B| and curvature are closely related but not the same thing. Where |B| is weak (outboard), curvature is bad. Where |B| is strong (inboard), curvature is good. They're correlated because both come from the same 1/R geometry of the torus. But they enter GENE's equations differently — |B| controls particle trapping, curvature controls the turbulence drive — so they're stored as separate arrays.

The problem metrics solve

GENE works in a field-aligned coordinate system (x, y, z) where x = radial, y = binormal, z = along the field line. But this coordinate system is curved and stretched — one unit of x doesn't mean the same real-world distance everywhere. The metric coefficients tell GENE the conversion.

🗺️

Map analogy

Think of a Mercator map projection. On the map, Greenland looks huge, but it's actually much smaller than Africa. That's because the map distorts distances differently at different latitudes. The metric coefficients are like a table that says "at this latitude, 1 cm on the map = X km in reality." GENE's coordinate system has the same kind of distortion — the metrics correct for it.

The three coefficients

g^xx — radial stretching

How much real-world radial distance corresponds to one unit of x in GENE's coordinates. Changes along the field line because the flux surfaces are closer together on the outboard side and farther apart on the inboard side. This directly scales the effective radial wavenumber k_x that GENE uses.

g^yy — binormal stretching

Same idea but for the y (binormal) direction — perpendicular to both the field line and the radial direction. This scales the effective binormal wavenumber k_y. It varies because the flux surface width changes as you go around poloidally.

g^xy — the cross-term (this is the shear one)

This is where magnetic shear shows up geometrically. Because neighboring field lines twist at different rates (that's shear), the x and y directions aren't perpendicular everywhere. g^xy measures how much they're tilted relative to each other. At the outboard midplane it's zero (x and y are perpendicular), but as you move along z it grows — proportional to ŝ. This is literally the geometric manifestation of the "twist-and-shift" we talked about with shear.

Why you should care

What the metrics actually do in GENE

Every time GENE computes a spatial derivative, it multiplies by a metric coefficient. The perpendicular Laplacian — which determines how fluctuations spread — uses all three: k_perp² = g^xx · k_x² + 2·g^xy · k_x · k_y + g^yy · k_y². So the metrics set the effective wavenumber at every point along the tube. Wrong metrics = wrong mode structure = wrong turbulence spectrum.

The key insight: g^xy is the one that grows with z because of shear. It starts at zero at the outboard midplane and keeps growing as you go along the field line. This means the effective perpendicular wavenumber increases away from the midplane, which is one of the main mechanisms by which shear stabilizes turbulence — it makes eddies "feel" like they're at shorter wavelengths, where damping is stronger.

What each piece does — one sentence each

q — how many long-way loops per short-way loop. Sets the twist-and-shift boundary condition.

ŝ — how fast q changes between surfaces. Determines mode coupling and g^xy growth.

κ — radial bending of the field line. Positive outboard drives turbulence, negative inboard stabilizes.

|B| — magnetic field strength. Controls particle trapping and mirror force.

g^xx, g^xy, g^yy — convert GENE's coordinates to real distances. Set the effective perpendicular wavenumber.

Jacobian — how big each grid cell is in real space. Needed for integrals and volume averaging.
