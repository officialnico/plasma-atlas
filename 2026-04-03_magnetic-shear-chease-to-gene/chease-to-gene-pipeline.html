What CHEASE Actually Computes

The real pipeline: 2D cross-section solve → pick a surface → trace the field line → hand GENE 1D arrays. Step by step.

CHEASE'S WORLD — THE 2D CROSS-SECTION ψ(R,Z)

Step 1: Solve Grad-Shafranov on the cross-section

CHEASE works entirely in this 2D plane (R, Z). It solves the Grad-Shafranov equation to find ψ(R,Z) — the magnetic flux at every point. The colored contours are lines of constant ψ — those are your flux surfaces. The heatmap shows the ψ values on CHEASE's computational grid.

This is the only simulation CHEASE runs. Because the tokamak is symmetric going around toroidally, this one slice contains all the information. There's no 3D computation anywhere.

TRACE AROUND THE SURFACE — SAMPLE GEOMETRY ANIMATED

Step 3: Walk around the surface, measure everything

CHEASE traces around the selected surface from θ = −π to +π (outboard → top → inboard → bottom → back to outboard). At each grid point along the way, it computes:

• Curvature κ — how much the field line bends, and which way
• Metric coefficients gxx, gxy, gyy — how distances work in field-aligned coordinates
• Jacobian — how big the grid cell is in real space
• |B| — magnetic field strength along the line

Each of these becomes a 1D array indexed by position along the field line. The dot traces the path and the panel below shows what's being measured in real time.

Step 4: This is it — what GENE gets

Two single numbers (q and ŝ) plus a handful of 1D arrays along the field line coordinate z. That's the entire geometry file. No 2D grids, no 3D torus — just curves.

GENE reads this once at startup and uses it for the entire simulation. The curvature array tells it where turbulence is driven (outboard, κ > 0) and where it's stabilized (inboard, κ < 0). The metric arrays tell it how to translate its field-aligned coordinates into real distances. The Jacobian tells it how much real-space volume each grid cell represents.
