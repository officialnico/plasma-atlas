Magnetic Curvature

How the magnetic field lines bend in space — and why the direction they bend either stabilizes or destroys your plasma.

01 · What is curvature?

A field line in a tokamak isn't straight — it curves. Curvature κ measures how much the field line bends at each point, and which direction it bends toward. That's it — it's the "bendiness" of the field line.

🛣️

Think of a road

Driving on a straight highway: zero curvature. Going around a tight bend: high curvature. The tighter the bend, the bigger κ. Same idea for field lines.

📐

It has a direction

Curvature isn't just a number — it's a vector. It points toward the center of the curve. A field line bending outward has κ pointing outward. This direction is what matters for stability.

03 · The key difference from shear

Shear (ŝ) was just a number per flux surface — same everywhere on that surface. Curvature changes as you go around the surface. On the outboard side it bends one way, on the inboard side it bends the other way. That's why it's a 2D quantity: it depends on both which surface (ρ) AND where on that surface (θ).

Good curvature vs bad curvature

Not all bending is equal. The direction the field line curves relative to the pressure gradient determines whether curvature stabilizes or destabilizes the plasma.

💥

Bad Curvature (outboard side)

On the outboard (low-field) side, the field line curves away from the plasma center. The curvature vector and the pressure gradient point in the same direction. This is like balancing a ball on top of a hill — any perturbation grows. This drives turbulence.

🛡️

Good Curvature (inboard side)

On the inboard (high-field) side, the field line curves toward the center. Curvature opposes the pressure gradient. Like a ball in a valley — perturbations get pushed back. This is stabilizing.

Why the outboard side matters most

In a tokamak, a field line spends time on both sides as it spirals around. But it spends more time on the outboard side (because the path is longer there — bigger radius). So the bad curvature region gets more "weight." This is why tokamak turbulence is mostly driven from the outboard midplane — it's called ballooning, because perturbations "balloon out" on the low-field side like a bulge in a weak spot.

Shaping fights bad curvature

Elongation

Stretching the plasma vertically reduces the curvature on the outboard midplane — the field line bends less sharply there. Less bad curvature = less turbulence drive.

Triangularity

Positive triangularity (D-shape pointing outward) helps stability. Negative triangularity (D pointing inward) can be even better — it moves the bad curvature region to where the pressure gradient is weaker. Try the sliders on the first tab!

What the data looks like

If you have 200 flux surfaces and 128 poloidal points per surface, curvature is a 200 × 128 array. Compare to shear which is just a 200-element array. This is why curvature is more expensive to compute and store — and why CHEASE needs to do a proper equilibrium solve to get it right, rather than just differentiating a 1D profile.

What GENE does with curvature

Remember: GENE simulates a thin flux tube following a field line. As that tube follows the line around the torus, the curvature changes. GENE needs to know κ at every point along the tube because it directly enters the equations that drive turbulence.

🌊

Curvature drives interchange modes

Bad curvature + pressure gradient = energy source for turbulence. The term in GENE's equations is literally κ · ∇p. Bigger bad curvature → stronger turbulence drive → more heat escaping the plasma.

🔧

It enters the drift velocities

Charged particles in a curved magnetic field drift sideways (curvature drift). The drift speed depends on κ. GENE needs this drift to correctly evolve the particle distribution — wrong κ means wrong drifts means wrong transport.

📊

It varies along the field line

Unlike shear (one number for the whole tube), curvature changes as GENE steps along the tube from outboard to top to inboard to bottom and back. So GENE stores κ as an array indexed by the parallel coordinate z — one value per grid point along the field line.

⚖️

Good regions partially cancel bad

A turbulent eddy extending along the field line samples both good and bad curvature regions. The competition between them determines whether the mode actually grows. That's why the full poloidal profile of κ matters — not just the outboard value.

Plain English version

GENE's flux tube follows a field line. At the outboard midplane the field bends outward — that's like a hill for the plasma, and perturbations roll downhill (grow). At the inboard side the field bends inward — that's a valley, perturbations get pushed back (shrink). GENE needs the exact curvature profile along the entire field line to know how much "hill" vs "valley" the turbulence sees. Get it wrong and your turbulence prediction is off.

How CHEASE computes it

From equilibrium to curvature

CHEASE solves the Grad-Shafranov equation to get the full 2D magnetic field B(R,Z). Then curvature is computed as κ = (b̂ · ∇)b̂ — that is, how the unit vector along the field line changes as you move along it. This requires second derivatives of the magnetic flux ψ, which is why CHEASE uses high-order Hermite finite elements — crude numerics would give noisy garbage for κ.

⚠️

Sensitive to equilibrium quality

Because κ involves second derivatives, it's much more sensitive to equilibrium errors than q or shear (which only need first derivatives). A slightly noisy EQDSK → slightly wrong ψ(R,Z) → wildly wrong κ. This is the main reason CHEASE re-solves the equilibrium rather than just reading EQDSK directly.

📝

Written as κ(z) for GENE

CHEASE traces a field line on the chosen flux surface and evaluates κ at each point along it. The poloidal angle θ maps to a parallel coordinate z. The output is a 1D array κ(z) — one value per point along the field line. This is what GENE actually reads.
