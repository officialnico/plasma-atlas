Deep Dive · Geometry Quantities

The Safety Factor q

How many times does a field line go the long way around the torus before it comes back? That number is q — and everything else (shear, stability, transport) flows from it.

01 · The idea

Pick a field line. Follow it as it spirals around the torus. Count: how many toroidal (long way) loops does it make before it completes one poloidal (short way) loop? That count is q.

q = 1

Tight spiral

The field line goes around the torus once and it's already completed a full poloidal circuit. Very aggressive twist. This is where kink instabilities live.

q = 2

Moderate twist

Two toroidal trips per poloidal loop. A common value at mid-radius in a standard tokamak. Generally stable territory.

q = 4

Gentle spiral

Four trips around the long way before one short-way loop. Typical near the plasma edge. The field line is almost going straight toroidally.

q = 1.5

Not an integer

The field line never closes on itself! It keeps winding and eventually fills the entire flux surface. This is actually the generic case — rational q (integers, simple fractions) are the special ones.

03 · q changes with radius

q isn't one number — it's a profile. Near the core it's low (~1), near the edge it's high (~4-5). The shape of this profile is what determines shear.

q PROFILE → SHEAR PROFILE HOVER / TAP
q(ρ) — rises from core to edge
ŝ(ρ) = (ρ/q)·dq/dρ — the slope, normalized
Where q rises steeply → high ŝ

The connection to shear

Shear is literally "how fast is q changing?" If the q profile is flat, ŝ ≈ 0. If q ramps up steeply (as it does near the edge), ŝ is large. The profile shape encodes all the shear information — that's why CHEASE just needs to differentiate q(ρ) to get ŝ(ρ).

04 · Why it's called "safety"
⚡

The q = 1 danger zone

When q drops to 1, the field line closes on itself after a single toroidal loop. This creates a resonance — perturbations can reinforce themselves on every pass. The result is the kink instability, which can violently disrupt the plasma.

Tokamaks are designed to keep q > 1 everywhere (the Kruskal-Shafranov limit). The edge q is typically 3–5. Higher q = safer, hence the name. But there's a tradeoff: higher q means weaker poloidal field, which means weaker confinement.

05 · Multiple surfaces at once

Now put it all together. Different surfaces have different q, so their field lines puncture the cross-section at different rates. This is exactly the differential twist we saw in the shear animation — but now you can see why it happens: because q increases outward.
