Why a nucleus falls apart — and the math of when.

Every alpha, beta, and gamma ray we've met came out of an unstable nucleus. But what makes one nucleus sit unchanged for billions of years and another vanish in microseconds? The answer is a tug-of-war between two forces, refereed by energy — and once you accept it's random, the timing collapses into one beautifully simple equation.

A / THE CAUSEA tug-of-war inside every nucleus

Two forces fight over every nucleus. The strong force is the glue — ferociously powerful, but so short-ranged it only grabs nucleons that are practically touching, so its pull grows roughly with the number of nucleons. Electromagnetic repulsion pushes every proton away from every other proton — weaker, but long-range, so it piles up with the square of the proton count. Add nucleons and watch repulsion catch the glue.

The real rule underneath: a nucleus decays only if some other arrangement has lower total energy (equivalently, less mass). When repulsion strains the glue, splitting off a chunk or rearranging protons into neutrons drops the energy — so it happens. Stability isn't "the forces balance," it's "there's no cheaper place to fall."

B / WHICH WAY IT FALLSThe valley of stability

Plot every nucleus by its protons and neutrons and the stable ones trace a narrow belt. Stray off it and you're unstable — and the belt tells you which decay you'll use to get back. Too many neutrons? Convert one to a proton (β⁻). Too many protons? The reverse (β⁺). Too big entirely? Dump a whole chunk (α). Tap anywhere on the chart.

C / THE REFEREEEnergy is the boss — the binding curve

Here's the master chart of all nuclear physics: binding energy per nucleon. Higher means more tightly bound, more stable. It climbs fast, peaks at iron-56, then gently falls. Everything rolls downhill toward that peak.

That single hump explains the whole story we've built: light nuclei release energy by fusing upward toward iron; heavy nuclei release energy by splitting down toward it (fission); and an unstable nucleus decays because its products sit higher on the curve than it does. The energy released is just the mass that goes missing:

Q = ( mparent − mproducts ) c²if Q is positive, the decay is allowed — and Q becomes the kinetic energy of what flies out
Why an alpha specifically? Look at the curve's far left — helium-4 sits on a sharp spike, unusually tightly bound for its size. So when a heavy nucleus sheds mass, ejecting a ready-made, rock-solid alpha is the most energy-efficient package available. That's why alpha decay, not "proton drizzle," is the heavy-element exit.

D / THE STRANGE PARTIt's pure dice — and ageless

You cannot predict when a given nucleus will go. Quantum mechanics only hands you a constant probability per second. A uranium atom that's waited 4 billion years is no more "due" than a fresh one — nuclei don't age, don't wear out, carry no memory. Each second is an independent coin flip with the same tiny bias.

That single fact — constant probability, no memory — is enough to derive exactly how a whole population thins out. Watch.

E / THE MATHThe decay law, from one assumption

Call the per-nucleus probability of decaying per unit time λ (the decay constant). For N nuclei, the number that go in a sliver of time dt is just λ times how many you have:

dNdt = −λNthe more you have, the faster you lose them — the signature of exponential decay

Solve that (it's the equation whose own derivative is itself) and you get the famous result:

N(t) = N0 e−λtstart with N₀, and the count melts away on an exponential curve

"Half-life" t½ is just the time for N to halve. Set N = N0/2 and solve — the N₀ cancels, which is why half-life never depends on how much you started with:

t½ = ln 2λ ≈ 0.693λand the average lifetime is τ = 1/λ, so τ ≈ 1.44 × t½ — they're related but not equal
λ decay constant (per sec)t½ half-lifeτ = 1/λ mean lifetimeA = λN activity (becquerels)
Reconciling our neutron: earlier I said a free neutron lasts "about 15 minutes." That's its mean lifetime τ ≈ 880 s ≈ 14.7 min. Its half-life is shorter — t½ = τ × ln2 ≈ 10.2 min. Same particle, two different (often-confused) numbers, now you can see exactly why they differ by that factor of 0.693.

G / THE PAYOFFWorked example: carbon-14 dating

This is the math earning its keep. Living things keep a fixed trace of radioactive carbon-14 (t½ = 5,730 years) topped up from the air. The instant something dies, top-ups stop and the C-14 starts its exponential slide. Measure how much is left, run the equation backward, and you've dated it.

Rearranging N(t) = N₀e−λt to solve for the time elapsed:

t = 1λ · lnN0N = t½ln 2 · ln100%% left

Try it — slide to how much C-14 a bone still has:

—
  1. A bone has 25% of its original C-14 left.
  2. 25% is one half (50%) then half again — that's 2 half-lives.
  3. 2 × 5,730 = ≈ 11,460 years old. The equation gives the same: (5730/ln2)·ln(100/25) ≈ 11,460 yr.
The catch built into the curve: after about 10 half-lives (~57,000 years) less than 0.1% of the C-14 survives — too little to measure reliably. That's literally why radiocarbon dating runs out around 50,000 years, and why older things need slower clocks like uranium-238 (t½ = 4.5 billion years), the very nucleus from where this whole conversation started.

WHY NUCLEI DECAY · THE MATH OF WHEN

Full chain of reasoning: two forces compete → energy decides whether a cheaper configuration exists → if so, the nucleus decays, but only on quantum dice with a fixed per-second odds → those odds, applied to a crowd, give N₀e^(−λt) and a half-life of ln2/λ. From a uranium atom outlasting the Earth to a free neutron gone in ten minutes, it's all the same single equation with a different λ.
