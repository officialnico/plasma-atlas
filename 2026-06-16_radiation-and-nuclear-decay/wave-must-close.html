The wave that has to meet itself.

An electron bound to an atom is a wave wrapped into a loop. For it to keep existing, it has to join up smoothly with itself where it comes back around. Only a whole number of wavelengths can do that — a fraction comes back out of step and erases itself on every lap. Drag the dial and watch which waves survive.

Below: the loop is the electron's path around the nucleus (a cartoon of a real 3-D orbital, but the logic is exact). The dial sets how many wavelengths you try to fit around it. Land on a whole number and the wave locks into a bright, steady standing wave. Land in between and watch every trip around land in a new place — the laps pile up out of phase and blur into nothing.

wavelengths around the loop: 3.00
That's the whole reason energy is discrete. The surviving waves carry whole numbers of wavelengths — 1, 2, 3, 4 — never 2½. And a wave's wavelength sets its energy, so the allowed energies are a discrete list too, with gaps where no wave survives. Discreteness isn't a rule blocking certain energies; it's what's left over after every non-fitting wave cancels itself away. (Same logic as the nuclear gamma ladder — confined wave, whole-number fits, discrete rungs.)

THE WAVE THAT HAS TO MEET ITSELF

No probabilities here — just a wave that either closes on itself and lasts, or comes back out of step and wipes itself out. The whole-number survivors are the energy levels; the gaps between them are the energies whose waves couldn't hold together.
