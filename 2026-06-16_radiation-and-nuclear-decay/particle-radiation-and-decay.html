The matter that flies — and why it flies at all.

Light is a wave; the other half of radiation is real, physical matter in motion. The questions compound quickly: What is it built from — quarks, or something genuinely indivisible? What experiments established that? Why does a flying proton count as radiation when it isn't decaying? And the structural question beneath them all — what destabilizes a nucleus, and what sets the timescale on which it sheds these particles? The answer to the last one collapses to a single separable differential equation; the rest is the physics that feeds it.

A / FIRST, A WORD FIX"Radiation" isn't "radioactive"

A flying proton being called "radioactive" feels off — and it is. These are two different ideas people blur together:

a property of a source

Radioactive

An unstable nucleus that will, on its own, fall apart and spit something out. Uranium is radioactive. It describes the thing that decays.

stuff in flight

Radiation

The energy or particles travelling through space — whatever got spat out, plus anything else moving fast and carrying energy. It describes the stuff in motion.

So the flying proton? It's radiation — a fast, charged particle that rips electrons off atoms as it passes (ionizing). But it is not radioactive: a lone proton is stable and isn't decaying into anything. It became radiation because something accelerated it — the Sun, a supernova, a hospital's proton-therapy machine — not because it's unstable. Being radiation is about motion and energy, not about being broken.

C / EXHIBIT ONEThe alpha particle, dissected

An alpha (α) is simply a helium nucleus torn loose: two protons + two neutrons, clamped together. Each of those four nucleons is itself a bag of three quarks glued by the strong force. So an alpha is, all the way down, twelve quarks — six "up," six "down" — plus the gluons binding them. Here it is with the lid off.

Charge check: proton (+1) + proton (+1) + neutron (0) + neutron (0) = +2, exactly the alpha's measured charge.

The quark arithmetic — an up quark carries +⅔, a down quark −⅓. A proton is up+up+down = +⅔+⅔−⅓ = +1. A neutron is up+down+down = +⅔−⅓−⅓ = 0. The charges you measure on whole particles fall straight out of the fractional quark charges underneath — one of the cleanest pieces of evidence that quarks are really there.

But two of those four nucleons are positive protons — so why doesn't the electrostatic repulsion blow it apart? Toggle between a bare diproton (two protons, no neutrons) and the real alpha, and watch what the neutrons do.

Neutrons are the rescue. The strong force is charge-blind — the green bonds pull every pair (p–p, p–n, n–n) together equally — while Coulomb repulsion (red) acts only between the two protons. With two protons alone, one attractive bond is set against that repulsion, and a real diproton is in fact unbound: it has no bound state and flies apart. Add two neutrons and you get six attractive bonds against the same single repulsion, so the cluster drops into a deep energy well — about 28 MeV below four free nucleons. Reversing that (α → 2p + 2n) has Q ≈ −28 MeV, so it would need 28 MeV supplied and never happens spontaneously. Helium-4 is also "doubly magic," which is why it's the exceptionally stable fragment heavy nuclei prefer to eject.

D / EXHIBIT TWOBeta decay — where the electron is born

A beta particle is just a fast electron — and unlike the alpha, the electron is elementary: no quarks, no parts, a true point as far as we can measure. But here's the twist that surprises everyone: that electron was not hiding inside the nucleus. It's created on the spot. Watch a neutron do it.

What you just saw — the weak force flipped one down quark into an up quark (turning the neutron into a proton), and the leftover energy materialized as a brand-new electron plus a near-invisible antineutrino. The electron didn't escape; it was conjured from energy at the instant of decay. And note: a free neutron does this all by itself in about 15 minutes — meaning the neutron, alone, is genuinely radioactive.

F / THE CAUSESo why does a nucleus fall apart?

We've catalogued the particles; now the mechanism that emits them. Nuclear stability is a competition between two terms in the binding energy. The strong force saturates — its range is so short that each nucleon binds only its immediate neighbours, so the attractive (volume) contribution scales linearly, ∝ A. Coulomb repulsion does not saturate: every proton pair contributes, and for a roughly uniform charged sphere of radius R ∝ A⅓ the electrostatic energy goes as Z²/A⅓. Because Z² outpaces A as nuclei grow, repulsion inevitably gains on the glue — the core of the liquid-drop model. Drag A upward and watch the two terms converge.

The variational principle underneath: a nucleus is unstable whenever some accessible configuration has lower total rest-energy — equivalently, greater binding energy. A decay proceeds spontaneously precisely when its Q-value is positive. It isn't that "the forces balance," but that the system can descend to a lower point on the energy surface; the forces only set the shape of that surface.

G / WHICH WAY IT FALLSThe valley of stability

Plot every nuclide on the (Z, N) plane and the stable species trace a narrow belt: light nuclei hug N ≈ Z, while heavier ones bend toward a neutron excess — the extra neutrons supply binding without adding to the Coulomb term. Off the belt, the favoured decay is whichever moves a nuclide back toward it. A neutron-rich nuclide converts a neutron to a proton (β⁻); a proton-rich one does the reverse via β⁺ emission or electron capture; a nuclide beyond Z ≈ 83 sheds an α, lowering both Z and A. H / THE REFEREEEnergy is the boss — the binding curve

The master diagram of nuclear physics: binding energy per nucleon, B/A — higher means more tightly bound. It climbs steeply, peaks near iron-56 / nickel-62 at ≈ 8.8 MeV, then declines, a profile reproduced term-by-term by the semi-empirical mass formula (volume − surface − Coulomb − asymmetry + pairing). Every nucleus runs downhill toward that maximum.

That hump organizes everything: light nuclei liberate energy by fusing rightward toward iron, heavy nuclei by fissioning leftward toward it, and an unstable nucleus decays because its products sit higher on the curve. The binding energy is literally the mass defect made energy — the shortfall between the assembled nucleus and its separated constituents:

B = [ Z·mp + N·mn − M(Z,N) ] c²Δm = (sum of the parts) − (mass of the whole), converted by E = Δm c²

The energy a decay releases is the corresponding difference in rest-mass between parent and products — equivalently, the gain in binding:

Q = ( mparent − Σmproducts ) c²Q > 0 is the condition for spontaneous decay; Q appears as the kinetic energy of the emitted products
Why an alpha, specifically? Helium-4 sits on a local spike in B/A — doubly magic, exceptionally bound — so ejecting a pre-formed α maximizes Q among the fragments a heavy nucleus could shed. The α must still tunnel through the Coulomb barrier it is classically trapped behind; the exponential sensitivity of that tunneling probability to Q is the Geiger–Nuttall relation, which is why α half-lives range across some thirty orders of magnitude for a modest spread in Q.

I / THE STRANGE PARTIt's pure dice — and ageless

Quantum mechanics refuses to tell you when a given nucleus will decay; it supplies only a constant hazard rate λ — a fixed probability per unit time, independent of the nucleus's entire history. This is the defining property of a Poisson process: the waiting time to decay is memoryless,

P( T > s + t  |  T > s ) = P( T > t )having already survived a time s tells you nothing — the conditional distribution resets

A uranium nucleus that has persisted for 4 billion years is statistically indistinguishable from one minted this morning: no internal clock, no aging, no accumulated wear. That single premise — a constant, memoryless rate — is sufficient to derive the entire macroscopic decay law. Here it is.

J / THE MATHThe decay law, from one assumption

Encode that single assumption. With N nuclei each carrying hazard rate λ, the expected number decaying in an interval dt is λN dt, which gives an autonomous first-order linear ODE:

dNdt = −λNthe rate of loss is proportional to the population — separable and autonomous

Separate the variables and integrate both sides:

∫ dNN = −λ ∫ dt  ⟹  ln N = −λt + C

Exponentiate and fix the constant of integration with the initial condition N(0) = N0:

N(t) = N0 e−λtthe unique solution — exponential decay falls directly out of the constant-rate hypothesis

Define the half-life by N(t½) = N0/2. The factor of N₀ cancels — which is precisely why the half-life is independent of how much you started with:

12 = e−λt½  ⟹  t½ = ln 2λ

The decay time of a single nucleus is a random variable T with exponential probability density f(t) = λe−λt. The mean lifetime τ is its expectation — one integration by parts:

τ = E[T] = ∫0∞ t · λe−λt dt = 1λhence t½ = τ ln 2 ≈ 0.693 τ — the half-life is the distribution's median, the lifetime its mean

Reconciling our neutron: the "about 15 minutes" from section D is the mean lifetime τ ≈ 880 s ≈ 14.7 min — the expectation computed just above. The half-life is the median of the same distribution, t½ = τ ln2 ≈ 10.2 min. Mean and median diverge for any skewed distribution; for the exponential, they differ by exactly that factor of ln2 ≈ 0.693.

L / THE PAYOFFWorked example: carbon-14 dating

The math earning its keep. Living things keep a fixed trace of radioactive carbon-14 (t½ = 5,730 years) topped up from the air. The instant something dies, top-ups stop and the C-14 starts its exponential slide. Measure how much is left, run the equation backward, and you've dated it.

Invert N(t) = N0e−λt for the elapsed time, using λ = ln2 / t½ = 1.21 × 10−4 yr−1:

t = 1λ · lnN0N = t½ln 2 · ln100%% left

  1. A bone has 25% of its original C-14 left.
  2. 25% is one half (50%) then half again — that's 2 half-lives.
  3. 2 × 5,730 = ≈ 11,460 years old. The equation gives the same: (5730/ln2)·ln(100/25) ≈ 11,460 yr.
Why the method has a ceiling — by differentiation. Differentiate the inverse: δt = (1/λ)·(δN/N), so a given fractional error in the surviving C-14 maps to a fixed absolute age error of (1/λ) ≈ 8,270 yr per unit fractional error. The problem is that N decays exponentially, so for old samples the counts run out and δN/N — limited by Poisson statistics ~1/√N — blows up. By ~10 half-lives (≈ 57,000 yr) under 0.1% remains and the signal drowns in noise. That ceiling near 50,000 years is structural; older samples demand a slower clock such as uranium-238 (t½ = 4.5 billion years).

M / THE BIG ONECosmic "rays" — the great misnomer

One last particle, and the name is a historical accident. When they were found in 1912 nobody knew what they were, so they got filed under "rays" alongside light. They are not electromagnetic. They're matter — about 90% bare protons, ~9% alpha particles, the rest heavier nuclei and electrons — screaming in from the Sun, supernovae, and unknown corners of the cosmos.

How do we know they're particles and not light? The magnet test: they bend in Earth's magnetic field. More cosmic rays arrive near the magnetic poles than the equator (the "latitude effect"). Light would sail straight through a magnet untouched — so anything a magnet steers must be charged matter. Case closed.

When one slams into the top of the atmosphere, it shatters air molecules and triggers an air shower — a branching cascade of secondary particles raining down. Most of what reaches the ground (and passes through your body right now) is muons, born kilometers up. Watch one come in:

A single primary becomes millions of secondaries. The muons (pink) survive all the way to the ground.

The "Oh-My-God" particle — in 1991 a detector in Utah clocked a single subatomic particle carrying ~3×10²⁰ electron-volts: the kinetic energy of a fast-pitched baseball, packed into one proton. We still don't fully know what could accelerate a thing that small to that energy. Particle radiation at its most extreme — and as un-electromagnetic as it gets.

PARTICLE RADIATION & NUCLEAR DECAY · A DEEP DIVE

The full throughline: a particle is "radiation" because it's moving with energy, not because it's broken. Some (alpha, proton, neutron) are little quark assemblies; one (the beta electron) is elementary and gets born mid-decay. Nuclei fire these out when two forces compete and a lower-energy arrangement exists — and exactly when is pure quantum dice, which for a crowd gives N₀e^(−λt) and a half-life of ln2/λ. From a uranium atom outlasting the Earth to a cosmic-ray proton thrown across the universe, it's all the same physics with a different number plugged in.
