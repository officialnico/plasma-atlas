01 / THE MASTER RULEEverything reduces to one principle

Before the cases, the rule they all obey. There is really only one, and every mode below is just a different route for satisfying it:

A nucleus changes only when a different arrangement has lower total energy — and it always moves toward the valley of stability. The mode it picks is simply the fastest available step downhill.

Two corollaries make the whole system predictable. First, direction is set by the energy balance (the Q-value): a decay runs spontaneously only if Q > 0, i.e. the products weigh less than the parent. Second, the goal is always the same place — the narrow belt of stable nuclei — so where a nucleus sits relative to that belt tells you exactly which move it will make. That's the entire logic of the next section.

02 / THE DECISIONThe five cases — diagnose any nucleus

Hand me a nucleus and I can tell you what it does by asking where it sits relative to the belt. There are only five answers. Tap each to see the rule, the reaction, and what changes.

An unstable nucleus asks: which single step drops my energy the most?
Rare cases exist too. The very heaviest nuclei can skip the ladder and spontaneously fission (split in two), and a few exotic nuclides emit a lone proton, a lone neutron, or even a small cluster like carbon-14. These are uncommon — the five above cover almost everything you'll meet — but they obey the same Q > 0 rule.

04 / THE AFTERMATHWhat happens right after a decay

A single decay is rarely "one event." It sets off a short, ordered sequence — on wildly different clocks — and it usually isn't the end of the road.

1
~INSTANT

The conversion fires

A quark flips (β) or a chunk is ejected (α), and the emitted particle — plus any neutrino — flies off carrying the Q-value as kinetic energy.

2
~10⁻¹² SECONDS

The daughter relaxes → γ

The new nucleus is usually left in an excited state, like a struck bell. It sheds that energy almost instantly as a gamma photon, dropping to its ground state. This is why β and α are so often shadowed by a γ.

3
~10⁻¹⁵ – 10⁻⁹ SECONDS

The atom is now a new element

Z changed by one, so it's a different element with the wrong-sized electron cloud. The electrons resettle; after electron capture an inner-shell hole fills and releases a characteristic X-ray (or an Auger electron).

4
ITS OWN HALF-LIFE

Stable? If not — repeat

If the daughter still sits off the belt, it's radioactive too. It waits out its own half-life, then decays again. One step becomes a chain (section 6), ending only when a stable nucleus is reached.

05 / THE CLOCKWhen, and how fast, it happens

"When" has two layers. Which nucleus decays and exactly when is pure quantum chance — but the rate, the half-life, is fixed and spans an almost unbelievable range. Here are real nuclides on a logarithmic time axis.

From a fraction of a millisecond to far longer than the age of the universe — roughly twenty-plus orders of magnitude. What sets the rate?

For alpha: the particle is trapped behind a Coulomb barrier and must quantum-tunnel out. Tunneling probability is exponentially sensitive to the energy Q, so a small change in Q swings the half-life by many orders of magnitude — the Geiger–Nuttall relation. That's why α half-lives alone run from microseconds to billions of years. For beta: it's governed by the feeble weak force and rises steeply with available energy (rate ∝ ~Q⁵), so low-energy betas can be extremely slow. For gamma: electromagnetic and fast — usually under a nanosecond.

06 / THE CASCADEDecay chains — one step leads to the next

When a daughter is itself unstable, you get a chain: a staircase of decays, each alternating α (down-left: Z−2, N−2) and β⁻ (down-right: Z+1, N−1), marching toward the belt until it hits something stable. Notice step 7: radium-226 alpha-decays into radon-222 — a gas. That's the one link in the chain that can drift out of rock and soil and accumulate in basements, which is why radon is a real-world concern even though the uranium it came from is locked in the ground. The chain also shows the timescale whiplash: a 4.5-billion-year first step, but a polonium-214 step lasting 164 millionths of a second.

The reading order for any nucleus: ① Is it on the belt? → done, stable. ② Is it very heavy? → α. ③ Off the belt — too many neutrons → β⁻, too many protons → β⁺/EC. ④ Whatever it lands on, if left excited → γ instantly. ⑤ If the daughter is still unstable → repeat from ①. That loop is radioactivity.

THE RADIOACTIVE DECAY PLAYBOOK

One rule (descend toward the valley of stability), five routes (α, β⁻, β⁺/EC, γ, and rare fission), one aftermath sequence (relax → re-element → maybe repeat), and a rate set by tunneling and the weak force that spans the lifetime of a polonium isotope to the age of the cosmos. Every radioactive thing you'll ever meet is some walk through this same diagram.
