Zero → hero · the Ontario CANDU reactor

How Ontario splits the atom.

Most of the world's reactors run on enriched uranium cooled by ordinary water. Ontario's don't. The CANDU — CANada Deuterium Uranium — makes an unusual bet: burn natural, unenriched uranium by moderating it with heavy water. That one choice ripples into everything — the machine's shape, how it's refuelled, how it's shut down, and what its waste becomes. Here's the whole chain, with the reactor live in your hands.

01 / THE EVENTThe split — and where the energy comes from

A uranium-235 nucleus is barely holding together — huge, crammed with mutually-repelling protons. Fire a neutron at it and, because the neutron has no charge, it isn't repelled; it slips in and is swallowed, briefly becoming U-236. But to see why that releases energy, you need one idea first.

Binding energy is how tightly a nucleus is held together — the energy you'd have to pour in to rip it back into loose protons and neutrons. Picture it as the depth of a well: a deeply-bound nucleus sits low, and climbing back out (taking it apart) is expensive. By the same token, when pieces fall into a deeper well, that drop in energy is released. And because a bound nucleus is lower in energy, it weighs slightly less than its separate parts — that missing mass, times c², is the binding energy (the mass-defect from the proton-weight panel, run in reverse).

Now watch fission as two drops down the well:

Drop 1 — capture. A free neutron sits "up high," unbound. When it falls into the uranium and binds, that fall releases its binding energy — about 6.5 MeV. The energy doesn't escape; it's dumped into the nucleus, leaving it sloshing and wobbling — the "excitation." And ~6.5 MeV of slosh is just past the ~6.2 MeV it takes to wobble to the breaking point, which is why even a near-motionless neutron sets off U-235.

Drop 2 — the split. The wobbling nucleus stretches until proton repulsion beats the strong force and it snaps in two. The fragments are far more tightly bound than uranium was — they sit in a much deeper well. That big drop, the difference in binding between uranium and the fragments, is cashed out as ~200 MeV of kinetic energy: fragments and 2–3 fresh neutrons flying apart. When they slam to a stop in the fuel, that motion is simply heat.

n + ²³⁵U → ²³⁶U* → two fragments + 2–3 n + ~200 MeVcapture releases ~6.5 MeV as wobble · the split cashes the binding difference as ~200 MeV of motion
So binding energy isn't the kinetic energy — it's the well's depth. What becomes the fragments' speed is the change in binding: they fall into a deeper well than uranium sat in, and the difference is released. A reactor is a machine for cashing that one drop, over and over, into heat.

02 / THE BALANCEThe chain reaction live

Each split releases 2–3 neutrons that can split more nuclei. If, on average, exactly one goes on to cause the next fission, the chain is steady — "critical" (k = 1). Below one it dies; above one it grows. Drive the control rods below: they're made of neutron-hungry material that drinks up free neutrons. Pull them out and the reaction runs away; push them in and it starves. Find the knife-edge in the middle.

rods OUT — reaction growsrods 45%rods IN — reaction dies
Criticality is a knife-edge — and that's the danger and the trick. Notice how narrow the "steady" band is. The only reason a reactor is controllable by humans is delayed neutrons: ~0.65% of fission neutrons don't appear instantly but trickle out seconds later from decaying fragments. That tiny lag is what gives operators (and the rods) time to react. Without it, the reaction would run faster than anything could catch.

03 / THE CANDU BETNatural uranium & heavy water

Here's the choice that makes a CANDU a CANDU. Fission-born neutrons come out fast, but uranium splits far more readily when hit by a slow neutron — so you need a moderator: light nuclei the neutrons bounce off, shedding speed each collision. The question is which light nuclei — and it comes down to neutron economy.

Ordinary "light" water (H₂O) slows neutrons beautifully, but its hydrogen nuclei (single protons) are also hungry — they frequently capture a passing neutron to become deuterium, eating neutrons the chain needs. So a light-water reactor must enrich its fuel to ~3–5% U-235 to have neutrons to spare.

Heavy water (D₂O) is water whose hydrogen is deuterium — already carrying the extra neutron. So it almost never captures another; it slows neutrons without eating them. That superb neutron economy means a CANDU can sustain a chain on natural uranium straight out of the ground — 0.7% U-235, no enrichment plant required. The trade-off: deuterium is heavier, so it slows neutrons slightly less per bounce (more collisions needed), and heavy water is expensive to produce — but skipping enrichment is the prize.

Why this matters strategically: enrichment is the hard, sensitive, proliferation-relevant step. By designing around it, CANDU trades an enrichment plant for a heavy-water plant — and gains the ability to run on fuel that needs only mining and milling.

04 / THE MACHINEInside a CANDU

Natural-uranium fuel and heavy water reshape the whole reactor. Three features set it apart from the single-pressure-vessel reactors used elsewhere:

The calandria

cool, low-pressure moderator tank

A big horizontal tank of cool heavy water at near-atmospheric pressure. Hundreds of fuel channels run through it. The moderator (which slows neutrons) is kept separate from the hot pressurised coolant — and being a large cool mass, it doubles as an emergency heat sink.

Pressure tubes

~480 individual fuel channels

Instead of one giant pressure vessel, each fuel channel is its own pressure tube carrying hot, pressurised heavy-water coolant past the fuel. Fuel is bundled natural-uranium ceramic (UO₂) in zirconium-alloy cladding.

On-power refuelling

refuel without shutting down

Natural-uranium fuel has little reactivity to spare, so it's refuelled often — and the channel design lets fuelling machines push fresh bundles in one end and spent ones out the other while the reactor runs. Light-water reactors must shut down to refuel.

Then: a steam plant

the nuclear part just makes heat

Hot coolant boils ordinary water in steam generators; the steam spins a turbine; the turbine spins a generator. Everything past the core is a conventional thermal power station. A reactor is an exotic, very dense way to boil water.

05 / THE PROTOCOLSHow Ontario keeps it safe

CANDU safety is built on diversity and independence — never relying on one system. The headline features:

Shutdown System 1

SDS1 · shutoff rods

Neutron-absorbing rods held above the core by clutches. On a trip signal the clutches release and the rods drop in by gravity (spring-assisted), killing the chain reaction in well under a second.

Shutdown System 2

SDS2 · liquid poison

A completely separate, independent system that injects gadolinium nitrate — a powerful neutron poison — straight into the moderator. Two diverse shutdown methods means no single failure can prevent shutdown.

The vacuum building

shared passive containment

A signature of Ontario's multi-unit stations: a huge building held under vacuum, ducted to every reactor's containment. If an accident over-pressurises a containment, the vacuum sucks in and condenses the steam, dousing the pressure — no power or operator action needed.

The cool moderator

a built-in heat sink

Because the big calandria of heavy water sits cool and separate from the fuel, it can absorb decay heat in accident scenarios — a passive safety margin baked into the geometry. All of it runs under CNSC regulation.

An honest caveat: CANDU's coolant has a slightly positive void reactivity — if the coolant boils away, reactivity can briefly rise rather than fall. This is a known, regulated characteristic, which is exactly why the two fast, independent shutdown systems above are mandatory and why the separate cool moderator matters so much.

06 / THE FLEETOntario's reactors, 2026

Nuclear supplies roughly half of Ontario's electricity, almost all of it from three CANDU stations. As of 2026 the fleet is in the middle of a generational wave of refurbishment — replacing pressure tubes, feeders and steam generators to add ~30 years of life per unit.

Bruce operating · refurbishing

Tiverton, Lake Huron · 8 units

The largest operating nuclear station on Earth. A unit-by-unit mid-life refurbishment is underway — one unit returned to service in 2026, with the rest sequenced through the late 2020s.

Darlington refurbished

Bowmanville, Lake Ontario · 4 units

Four CANDU units (~3,512 MW), enough for ~20% of Ontario's power. Its four-unit refurbishment finished in early 2026 — completed on budget and ahead of its original schedule.

Pickering closing 2026 → refurb

Pickering, Lake Ontario

The oldest station. Units 5–8 are slated to shut down at the end of 2026 and enter a major refurbishment (work ramping from 2027, back in service by the mid-2030s). The older A-side units have been retired.

Darlington SMR under construction

a different beast — not a CANDU

Beside the CANDU station, Ontario is building the G7's first grid-scale small modular reactor — a GE Hitachi BWRX-300, a boiling-water design that uses enriched fuel and light water. Reactor-building construction is underway in 2026, targeting around 2030.

07 / THE WASTEWhat it becomes, and why live

Spent fuel is dangerous because of what fission makes. When a heavy nucleus splits, the two fragments inherit its high neutron-to-proton ratio — but a mid-sized nucleus needs far fewer neutrons to be stable. So the fragments are born deep in neutron-rich territory, far from the valley of stability, and they have to beta-decay their way back — converting neutrons to protons, firing off electrons and gammas — until they reach a stable nucleus. Watch one fall.

That's the whole reason spent fuel is radioactive: it's a soup of these neutron-rich fragments grinding down toward stability. Different ones take different times — cesium-137 and strontium-90 (~30-year half-lives) dominate the heat and radioactivity for the first few centuries; technetium-99 and iodine-129 (hundreds of thousands to millions of years) dominate the very-long-term hazard.

There's a second source. U-238 (most of the fuel) doesn't fission easily but it does capture neutrons: U-238 + n → U-239, which beta-decays to neptunium, then to plutonium-239, and further captures breed Pu-240, americium, curium. These transuranics are long-lived alpha emitters and drive the radiotoxicity that lasts tens to hundreds of thousands of years — the reason deep, long-term isolation is needed.

08 / THE CATCHWhy we can't just reuse it

Here's the counterintuitive part: spent fuel is called "spent" even though most of its energy is still there. The uranium is barely touched and it has bred fresh plutonium. So why pull it out?

Because the neutron balance can no longer be sustained. Two things choke it: the U-235 has depleted below what the reactor's neutron economy needs, and — crucially — the accumulating fission fragments include ferocious neutron poisons, above all xenon-135, the strongest neutron absorber known, and samarium-149. They soak up the very neutrons the chain depends on. Bred plutonium helps for a while (a CANDU burns some of its own), but eventually poisons plus depletion tip k below 1, and the chain can't hold. The energy isn't gone — the neutrons are being stolen.

You can reclaim that energy, in principle. Reprocessing chemically separates the leftover uranium and plutonium to make fresh fuel (France does this); fast/breeder reactors use fast neutrons to fission U-238 and bred plutonium, unlocking many times more of uranium's energy. Canada chose a once-through cycle — no reprocessing — for reasons of proliferation risk, economics, and policy, and because CANDU spent fuel is already so lean in fissile material that reprocessing it is unattractive.

The CANDU twist: that same superb neutron economy means a CANDU can eat other reactors' leftovers — it can run on the recovered uranium from light-water spent fuel (still richer than natural uranium), on mixed-oxide fuel, even on thorium. CANDU is unusually good at burning what other designs throw away; it just can't easily re-burn its own already-lean fuel without reprocessing.

09 / THE DESTINATIONWhere it ends up

For now, every spent CANDU fuel bundle stays in Ontario. Fresh out of the reactor it's intensely radioactive and hot, so it cools for ~7–10 years in water pools (the water shields radiation and carries off decay heat), then moves to dry storage in robust concrete containers on the station site.

The long-term plan is a deep geological repository. In November 2024, after a 14-year consent-based search, the Nuclear Waste Management Organization selected a site in northwestern Ontario — the Wabigoon Lake Ojibway Nation–Ignace area (the Revell rock formation, ~250 km northwest of Thunder Bay), with an Indigenous-led regulatory process as part of the approval. It's designed to hold roughly 5.9 million used fuel bundles, 500–800 metres down in stable rock, with operations expected to begin around 2040–2045.

The logic is that no single barrier has to be perfect — the fuel form, the container, the clay, and half a kilometre of ancient rock each independently slow any escape, so that no harmful radiation reaches the surface even over the hundreds of thousands of years the transuranics need to decay away. It's the same defence-in-depth philosophy as the reactor's shutdown systems, stretched across geological time.

CANDU · ZERO TO HERO

One design choice — burn natural uranium by moderating it with heavy water — explains the whole machine: the calandria and pressure tubes, on-power refuelling, the twin shutdown systems and vacuum building, and a once-through waste cycle headed for a repository in northern Ontario. The atom-splitting is the easy part; the engineering is all about controlling a knife-edge and isolating what's left when it's done.
