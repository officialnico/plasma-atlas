Deep dive · companion to “the particle zoo”

The other branch: what's actually flying at you.

Light is a wave. But the particle side of radiation is real, physical matter moving fast — and almost every question about it (Is it made of quarks? Has it been proven? Why is a flying proton "radiation"?) has a concrete, visualizable answer. Let's open up the particles.

A / FIRST, A WORD FIX"Radiation" isn't "radioactive"

Your instinct that a flying proton being "radioactive" feels off is correct — it isn't. These are two different ideas people blur together:

a property of a source

Radioactive

An unstable nucleus that will, on its own, fall apart and spit something out. Uranium is radioactive. It describes the thing that decays.

stuff in flight

Radiation

The energy or particles travelling through space — whatever got spat out, plus anything else moving fast and carrying energy. It describes the stuff in motion.

So the flying proton? It's radiation — a fast, charged particle that rips electrons off atoms as it passes (ionizing). But it is not radioactive: a lone proton is stable and isn't decaying into anything. It became radiation because something accelerated it — the Sun, a supernova, a hospital's proton-therapy machine — not because it's unstable. Being radiation is about motion and energy, not about being broken.

C / EXHIBIT ONEThe alpha particle, dissected

An alpha (α) is simply a helium nucleus torn loose: two protons + two neutrons, clamped together. Each of those four nucleons is itself a bag of three quarks glued by the strong force. So an alpha is, all the way down, twelve quarks — six "up," six "down" — plus the gluons binding them. Here it is with the lid off.

Charge check: proton (+1) + proton (+1) + neutron (0) + neutron (0) = +2, exactly the alpha's measured charge.

The quark arithmetic — an up quark carries +⅔, a down quark −⅓. A proton is up+up+down = +⅔+⅔−⅓ = +1. A neutron is up+down+down = +⅔−⅓−⅓ = 0. The charges you measure on whole particles fall straight out of the fractional quark charges underneath — one of the cleanest pieces of evidence that quarks are really there.

D / EXHIBIT TWOBeta decay — where the electron is born

A beta particle is just a fast electron — and unlike the alpha, the electron is elementary: no quarks, no parts, a true point as far as we can measure. But here's the twist that surprises everyone: that electron was not hiding inside the nucleus. It's created on the spot. Watch a neutron do it.

What you just saw — the weak force flipped one down quark into an up quark (turning the neutron into a proton), and the leftover energy materialized as a brand-new electron plus a near-invisible antineutrino. The electron didn't escape; it was conjured from energy at the instant of decay. And note: a free neutron does this all by itself in about 15 minutes — meaning the neutron, alone, is genuinely radioactive.

"But we've never seen a quark." True — the strong force won't let one travel alone (it's called confinement). We prove them the same way Rutherford found the nucleus: fire something hard inside and watch how it ricochets. Shoot high-energy electrons into a proton and they scatter off three distinct point-like lumps. That, plus the fractional charges adding up perfectly, plus a thousand other tests of the Standard Model, is why quarks are considered as real as atoms.

F / THE BIG ONECosmic "rays" — the great misnomer

You smelled something off, and you're right: the name is a historical accident. When they were found in 1912 nobody knew what they were, so they got filed under "rays" alongside light. They are not electromagnetic. They're matter — about 90% bare protons, ~9% alpha particles, the rest heavier nuclei and electrons — screaming in from the Sun, supernovae, and unknown corners of the cosmos.

How do we know they're particles and not light? The very test from the family tree: they bend in Earth's magnetic field. More cosmic rays arrive near the magnetic poles than the equator (the "latitude effect"). Light would sail straight through a magnet untouched — so anything a magnet steers must be charged matter. Case closed.

When one slams into the top of the atmosphere, it shatters air molecules and triggers an air shower — a branching cascade of secondary particles raining down. Most of what reaches the ground (and passes through your body right now) is muons, born kilometers up. Watch one come in:

A single primary becomes millions of secondaries. The muons (pink) survive all the way to the ground.

The "Oh-My-God" particle — in 1991 a detector in Utah clocked a single subatomic particle carrying ~3×10²⁰ electron-volts: the kinetic energy of a fast-pitched baseball, packed into one proton. We still don't fully know what could accelerate a thing that small to that energy. Particle radiation at its most extreme — and as un-electromagnetic as it gets.

PARTICLE RADIATION · A DEEP DIVE

The throughline: a particle is "radiation" because it's moving with energy, not because it's broken. Some (alpha, proton, neutron) are little quark assemblies; one (the beta electron) is elementary and gets born mid-decay. And the thing called a cosmic "ray" is the least ray-like of all — a fleck of matter thrown across the universe, which we caught red-handed by watching a magnet bend its path.
