01 / THE FAMILY TREETwo ways energy travels

Every kind of radiation belongs to one of these two branches. The cleanest way to tell them apart: what a magnet does to them.

〰️

Electromagnetic

A wave of energy — no mass, no electric charge — always moving at light speed. Light itself, plus radio, microwaves, X-rays, gamma rays.

radio · micro · infrared · visible · UV · x-ray · gamma
🧲 In a magnetic field: unmoved. With no charge, a magnet can't grab it — it travels dead straight. It carries its own electric & magnetic fields as part of the wave, but it doesn't follow an outside one.
⦿

Particle

Actual chunks of matter — bits of atoms — hurled out fast. They have mass, and many carry electric charge.

alpha · beta · neutron · proton · cosmic rays
🧲 In a magnetic field: the charged ones (alpha, beta, protons) curve and spiral along the field lines — that's the aurora, and how accelerators steer beams. Neutral ones like the neutron ignore it and fly straight.

06 / STOPPING ITWhat blocks what

Each type is halted by something different. Watch them launch and hit their wall.

The neutron twist — it sails straight through lead, the very thing that stops gamma. No charge means it ignores the metal's electrons. It's stopped by water, plastic, or concrete — hydrogen-rich stuff — because a neutron and a proton weigh nearly the same, so a head-on bounce drains its energy fast. Remember this: it's also why neutrons are perfect for the next part.

07 / THE PAYOFFFission — splitting the atom

Here's where every neutron and gamma ray on this page is born. Fire a slow neutron at a uranium-235 nucleus and it doesn't bounce — it gets swallowed. The bloated nucleus wobbles and tears itself in two, flinging out:

💥

~200 MeV of energy

Mostly the kinetic energy of the two fragments flying apart. This becomes heat — the entire point of a reactor.

⦿⦿⦿

2–3 fresh neutrons

The magic ingredient. Each can go split another nucleus. One event becomes two, becomes four… a chain reaction.

That self-feeding cascade is the whole game. Control it and you have a power station; let it run wild and you have a weapon. Everything from here is just how fast you let the chain go. The fragments are themselves radioactive and the burst throws off gamma rays and spare neutrons — exactly the radiation from sections 04–06.

08 / THE REACTORTaming the chain reaction

A reactor's only trick is keeping the chain reaction exactly balanced: on average, each fission causes precisely one more. Physicists call this number k. Below 1 the reaction dies; at 1 it holds steady (a working reactor); above 1 it grows. The throttle is a set of control rods — made of neutron-hungry materials like boron — that you slide in to soak up spare neutrons. Move the slider and watch the regime change.

control rods inserted: 50% live neutrons: 0
k < 1rods deep → dies out
k = 1balanced → steady power
k > 1rods out → runs away
The moderator — fresh neutrons come out too fast to be reliably caught. So reactors run them through water or graphite (the moderator) to slow them down to the speed where U-235 readily absorbs them. Pull the coolant away or jam k too high and you get the runaway heat behind Chernobyl and Fukushima.

09 / MAKING POWERIt's secretly just a steam engine

The anticlimax of nuclear power: after all that exotic physics, the energy is harvested the same way a coal plant does it — by boiling water. The reactor is just a very strange furnace. Heat → steam → spin a turbine → spin a magnet → electricity.

The used steam is cooled back into water (those giant curved towers are just condensers venting waste heat) and pumped around again. So a 1,000-megawatt reactor and a coal plant have nearly identical back halves — the only difference is whether the heat comes from burning carbon or splitting uranium. Uranium just packs millions of times more energy per gram, and emits no CO₂ doing it.

10 / REFINEMENTEnrichment — the hard part

Here's the catch that shapes the entire nuclear world. The uranium you dig up is 99.3% uranium-238, which mostly won't sustain a chain reaction, and only 0.7% uranium-235, the good stuff. To run a reactor you must raise that 0.7% to a few percent. The two isotopes are chemically identical twins — the only difference is that U-238 is three neutrons heavier.

centrifuge passes: 0
U-235 concentration0.7%
Natural uranium · straight out of the ground

Because the mass gap is so tiny, you can't filter them chemically — you spin uranium gas in centrifuges at enormous speed so the slightly heavier U-238 drifts outward, skimming off a fractionally richer center. The gain per spin is minuscule, so you chain thousands of centrifuges into cascades. This difficulty is the whole story of nonproliferation: the physics of a bomb has been public for 80 years, but enriching enough material is the genuine barrier, which is why international attention focuses on who has centrifuge cascades.

11 / THE BOMBThe chain reaction unleashed

A weapon is conceptually the reactor's evil twin: instead of holding k at 1, you slam it far above 1 using fast neutrons and a lump of nearly pure fissile material. The whole cascade — dozens of doublings — happens in under a millionth of a second, releasing in one instant what a reactor spreads over years.

The deep engineering challenge (and the reason this stays conceptual here) is brutal: the reaction releases so much energy it blows itself apart almost immediately, which halts it. So the entire problem is assembling a supercritical mass and holding it together long enough — a race measured in microseconds. Get the timing wrong and it "fizzles." Historically there were two solutions:

Gun-type

Little Boy · Hiroshima · uranium-235

Fire one subcritical slug of uranium into another. Together they exceed critical mass and the chain runs away. Mechanically simple — never even tested before use.

Implosion

Fat Man · Nagasaki · plutonium-239

Surround a subcritical sphere with shaped chemical explosives that crush it inward, squeezing it to a denser, supercritical state. Far harder, but the only route that works for plutonium.

Why plutonium forces the hard path — plutonium spits out stray neutrons on its own, so the slow gun method would trigger the chain too early and blow the pieces apart before they fully assembled — a fizzle. Only implosion is fast enough. This is conceptual physics that's been in encyclopedias since the 1950s; the real-world barrier was never the idea, it was the enrichment and precision engineering behind it.
