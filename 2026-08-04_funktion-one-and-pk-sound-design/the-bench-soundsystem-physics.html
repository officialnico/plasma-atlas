Anechoic bench · finite-difference acoustic solver

A horn is not a megaphone. It is an impedance transformer.

Air is a terrible load. A bare cone flails against almost nothing and converts about 1% of what you feed it into sound. A correctly flared duct matches the diaphragm's high mechanical impedance to the low impedance of open air, and that number goes up by ten times or more. Everything good downstream falls out of that one fact.

What to look for — cutoff

Pick Exponential and drag frequency below the stated fc. The wave stops propagating as a travelling wave and sloshes back and forth in the throat instead. Below cutoff a horn is a spring, not a transformer — it stops loading the diaphragm and the driver goes straight back to flailing. This is why a horn that's too short simply cannot go low, no matter how much EQ you throw at it.

fc = m·c / 4π  ·  m = ln(Smouth/Sthroat) / L

What to look for — higher-order modes

Set frequency to 4–6 kHz with a big mouth. Watch the field stop being clean arcs and start showing diagonal cross-corridor ripples. Those are higher-order modes — energy bouncing across the horn instead of along it. They arrive at the mouth late and out of phase with the main wavefront.

HOMs are the actual physical cause of "horn honk." Not a tonal balance problem, a time-domain one. It's why tractrix and carefully-computed flares exist: they're shapes chosen to keep the wavefront planar so cross-modes never get excited in the first place.

Criteria

What "good" even means

Almost every spec sheet shows frequency response. Frequency response is a steady-state measurement — it tells you what happens after a tone has been playing forever. Music is not made of tones playing forever. Music is made of starts and stops, and the thing that ruins starts and stops is energy that arrives late.

The trap

Drag Q up and watch the two numbers move at completely different rates. The magnitude bump roughly triples; the decay time grows tenfold. Only one of those is on the spec sheet, and it is the one that matters least.

A 4 dB bump is nearly inaudible as tonal colour on dense material. The same resonance ringing for 70 ms is devastating on percussive music, because the reverberant smear of one kick is still sounding when the next transient arrives.

So the real criteria are

1 · Low distortion at level. Not at 1 W. At the level you actually run it.

2 · Nothing arrives late. No panel ringing, no cross-modes, no reflections from the array summing incoherently, no group-delay smear at the crossover.

3 · Energy goes on people, not walls. Directivity control, so the direct sound wins over the room.

4 · Dynamics survive. No thermal compression eating the transients as the night goes on.

Flat frequency response is table stakes. It is item zero, and it's the only one most systems get right.

Stage 01

The excursion problem

Everything ugly a loudspeaker does, it does because the coil left the gap. Efficiency isn't about saving power — it's a distortion strategy. Get the same SPL with a quarter of the cone movement and most of the nonlinearity simply never gets excited.

The four nonlinearities

Bl(x) — force factor falls off as the coil leaves the magnetic gap, and it falls off asymmetrically. That asymmetry is your second harmonic, and it rectifies into a DC offset that pushes the coil even further out of the gap. It runs away from itself.

Kms(x) — suspension stiffness rises steeply at the extremes. Third harmonic.

Le(x,i) — voice coil inductance varies with both coil position and instantaneous current. Hugely under-appreciated. Cured with shorting rings and copper caps, not with EQ.

Doppler / IM — a cone reproducing 40 Hz while also carrying 2 kHz is physically frequency-modulating that 2 kHz. It is literally a moving source. Cut excursion 4× and it near-vanishes.

Thermal compression is the sneaky one

Drive a coil hard and it reaches 150–250 °C. Copper's resistance climbs roughly 0.39% per °C, so the driver draws less current for the same voltage and its sensitivity drops 3–6 dB. Your dynamics collapse mid-set, gradually, so nobody notices it happening — the system just gets progressively duller and everyone turns it up.

Watch the coil temp readout climb over ~20 seconds at high SPL on the direct radiator, then flip to horn-loaded. Same output, a fraction of the input power, coil stays cool, transients stay intact. That "effortless at volume" quality is not a vibe, it's a thermal measurement.

Stage 02

Two sources are never just louder

The moment you have more than one source, geometry starts writing the frequency response — differently for every seat in the room. Spacing, delay and polarity are the only three knobs, and every array topology ever built is some combination of them.

The quarter-wavelength rule

Two sources sum coherently everywhere only while they are within about λ/4 of each other. Set the topology to line array, put frequency at 10 kHz, and look at what λ/4 actually is: 8.5 mm. That is the tolerance for a seamless high end from adjacent boxes.

Miss it and you get comb filtering: deep notches that move with listener position, and — worse — a smeared transient, because the same click now arrives twice a fraction of a millisecond apart.

This is the objection Tony Andrews built Funktion-One around. His answer was hand-aimed point-source clusters with deliberately minimal overlap, rather than trying to hit an impossible spacing tolerance. Other camps accept the compromise and manage it with tight waveguide coupling. Both positions are defensible physics; they optimise different terms.

Mutual coupling: the free 3 dB

Put two subs close together, well under λ/4, and you get +6 dB, not +3. Three of those decibels come from doubling the power. The other three are free: each cone now radiates into an air load partly stiffened by its neighbour, so the mutual radiation impedance goes up and each driver converts more of its motion into pressure.

Below about 100 Hz everything within a couple of metres couples, which is why sub arrangement is a geometry problem, not a "buy more subs" problem.

Cardioid and end-fire use delay and polarity to make the rear radiation cancel — bass off the stage, off the neighbours' windows, and out of the back wall that would otherwise send it back at you 80 ms late.

FIR vs IIR is a real argument, not a marketing one

IIR filters are minimum-phase. Nearly zero latency, but they rotate phase through the crossover region — the woofer and the horn hand over with a time offset that varies with frequency. The magnitude sums flat; the step response doesn't reconstruct.

FIR filters can be linear-phase: perfect transient reconstruction through the crossover, at the cost of latency proportional to tap count, and pre-ringing — energy that arrives before the transient, which does not occur in nature and which some ears find more objectionable than the phase rotation it cured.

Andrews has historically been latency-averse and stayed minimum-phase. Others go all-in on FIR. Drag the tap count and watch the pre-echo appear as the latency grows — you're looking at the actual trade.

Damping factor is a voltage divider, not mysticism

When the signal stops, the cone is still moving, and a moving coil in a magnetic field is a generator. That back-EMF drives current back through the amplifier's output — which is nearly a short circuit — and the resulting force brakes the cone. Electrically. That's what stops a kick drum dead instead of letting it wobble.

The braking strength is Bl² / (Re + Rcable + Ramp). Cable resistance sits right there in the denominator, in series, and a driver's Re is only about 5 Ω to begin with. Run 60 m of thin cable and you've added a meaningful fraction of that.

This is why the people who care about this get intense about copper. It isn't audiophile jewellery — it's the only term in the equation you can still change on site.

Stage 04

Directivity is the clarity lever

The single number that predicts "can I hear detail from here" is the direct-to-reverberant ratio. You cannot EQ your way to a better one, because EQ cannot un-excite a room — reflected energy has the same spectrum as the direct sound that caused it. The only real controls are pattern control and where you point it.

The equation everything hangs on

rc ≈ 0.141 · √(Q · R)   R = Sα / (1 − α)

Double Q and critical distance moves out by √2. Beyond rc the room is louder than the speaker and detail is gone — you're listening to a smeared copy of the music arriving from every wall at once.

Note what this means for a hard room: at α = 0.06 (concrete warehouse, empty), rc collapses. A crowd of bodies is genuinely one of the best acoustic treatments available, which is why a rig that sounded harsh at soundcheck can come right at doors.

Why PK put motors in the waveguides

A fixed-pattern box is a compromise chosen at the factory for an average venue. Every real room deviates from average, so you're always either spraying the side walls or leaving the edges of the crowd underserved.

PK's Trinity and Gravity cabinets carry motorised waveguide vanes — the horizontal pattern of each box changes mechanically, roughly 30° to 120°, driven from their control software after modelling the venue geometry. Vertical splay adjusts too.

Flip motorised vanes on and drag the room width. The pattern tracks the geometry instead of the geometry having to tolerate the pattern. It's a mechanical solution to a problem the industry usually attacks with DSP, which structurally cannot solve it: once energy is in the room, no filter can retrieve it.

Stage 05

The ear you're tuning for isn't the one in the control room

Human hearing is not a flat, level-independent instrument. Its frequency response changes shape dramatically with level — which means a system tuned to sound "right" at conversation volume will be wrong at show volume, and vice versa.

Why the bass seems to "arrive" as it gets loud

At 40 phon the ear needs roughly 32 dB more at 40 Hz than at 1 kHz to hear them as equally loud. At 100 phon that gap has closed to about 17 dB. The low end does not get proportionally louder — the ear stops attenuating it, and the bass that was always in the recording finally reaches you at something like its true weight.

Practical consequence: a good system tech tunes at show level, not at chat level, and treats the house curve as level-dependent rather than a fixed target.

The 3 kHz problem

The ear canal is a quarter-wave resonator about 25 mm long, which gives a broad sensitivity peak around 3–4 kHz. That's where hearing is most acute and where fatigue and pain live. It's also exactly where horn cross-modes and crossover phase errors tend to land.

Which is the whole reason the horn work in the hero above matters: the frequency band where a badly-behaved waveguide misbehaves is the band the ear is most brutally sensitive in.

Verdict

Why this music specifically exposes all of it

Synthesised A/B, 3 s, headphones help. Sub content won't render on a phone speaker — that's the point.

The four properties

Ultra-fast transients → expose group delay, crossover phase behaviour and cabinet ringing. There's nothing to hide behind.

Extreme microdynamics → expose thermal compression the instant it starts, because the quiet detail between hits is the first thing to get swallowed.

Deep sub layered under detailed mids → the textbook excitation for Doppler and intermodulation distortion. A cone doing both at once modulates one with the other.

Wide psychoacoustic imaging, objects moving across the field → gets shredded by comb filtering, because the notches sit at different frequencies in each ear and the localisation cues fall apart.

Which is why it's the same track and a different piece of music

On a rig where excursion stays inside the linear region, cross-modes are controlled, phase is coherent through the crossover, and the direct field actually reaches the back of the room — all four survive intact.

On a normal rig each one degrades independently and they compound: the transient softens, the sub smears into the mids, the compression eats the microdynamics, and the imaging collapses to a vague wide blur. Nothing sounds obviously "broken." It just stops being the thing.

That gap — between a system that's technically working and one that's actually preserving the information — is the entire reason any of the engineering above gets done.

What's actually being computed

The horn is discretised into 44 short segments. Each gets a plane-wave transmission matrix; they're chained from the mouth's radiation impedance back to the throat to give Zthroat(f). The objective rewards a high, flat real part of that impedance across the band — real part is radiated power, ripple is uneven loading — and penalises mouth area and kinked profiles.

Differential evolution mutates a population of eight control-point radii. No gradient, no shape prior. It doesn't know what a horn is supposed to look like.

Watch it beat the exponential reference on ripple almost immediately, and note that the winning shape usually isn't smooth — the optimiser discovers that small deliberate area perturbations cancel reflections at specific frequencies, which is exactly the trick a human designer would call "wrong."

Where the real 50× is, and isn't

Not in the cone. Diaphragm shape is already near a hard wall. You're bounded by material stiffness-to-mass ratio, by the fact that any finite cone has modal breakup, and by the fact that pistonic radiation efficiency is set by physics, not geometry. Shape optimisation on a diaphragm buys you percent, not multiples — the useful work there is FEA-guided rib placement and material choice, and that's been ground down for decades.

Genuinely open: waveguides. Higher-order mode suppression is a hard, high-dimensional, wave-physics shape problem with a well-defined objective — structurally the same class of problem as a stellarator. BEM plus evolutionary search is already used; adjoint methods and full topology optimisation mostly aren't, because the industry is small and the compute wasn't cheap when the shapes were fixed. There is real room here.

The biggest space is the system + room objective. Cabinet geometry, array topology, delay, and pattern jointly optimised against a real venue with a real crowd. That's a huge, barely-explored design space with a measurable objective — which is precisely the condition that made stellarator optimisation pay off. A 50× improvement in a single driver? No. A step change in what a whole rig delivers to the last row of an ugly room? That one's still on the table.

None of the above is a secret. It's just that most of the industry optimises frequency response because that's what fits on a spec sheet, and the people who make rigs that stop conversations optimise distortion, time, and where the energy lands — which don't.
