The Schrödinger equation

iℏ ∂Ψ/∂t = ĤΨ
Time-dependent Schrödinger equation

This equation describes how the wave function Ψ(x,t) of a quantum particle evolves over time. It plays the same role in quantum mechanics that F = ma plays in classical mechanics — it tells you what the system does next.

Ψ — the wave function

A complex-valued function. It contains everything knowable about a quantum system.

ℏ — reduced Planck constant

Sets the scale where quantum effects matter. About 10⁻³⁴ J·s.

Ĥ — the Hamiltonian

The total energy operator: kinetic + potential. Shapes how Ψ evolves.

|Ψ|² — probability density

Born's rule: the square of Ψ gives the probability of finding the particle at each location.

What the equation actually says

Pull the time-dependent equation apart and it claims something specific: the rate of change of Ψ in time equals (up to a factor of i/ℏ) the Hamiltonian acting on Ψ. The factor of i is the crucial piece. It means time evolution doesn't grow or decay Ψ — it rotates it through the complex plane. That's why probability is conserved: |Ψ|² stays constant in magnitude even as Re(Ψ) and Im(Ψ) oscillate.

For energy eigenstates (like the ones above), Ψ separates into a spatial part times e^(-iEt/ℏ). The probability density |Ψ|² is then perfectly stationary — which is why these states are called stationary states, even though Ψ itself never stops moving.
