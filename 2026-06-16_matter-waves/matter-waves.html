Phase, not stuff

Nothing is
actually jiggling.

A water wave is water moving up and down. A sound wave is air squeezing. A matter wave is neither — what waves is an abstract angle that winds around as the particle travels. You can never picture it as the thing wobbling. But you can watch the angle. Here it is.

01  /  The rotating arrow

One full turn per wavelength

Strap a tiny spinning arrow to the particle. As it travels, the arrow rotates — and the distance it covers for one complete turn is the wavelength. That's the whole definition. The arrow's height as it spins traces out the wave below it. Nothing is moving up and down; the arrow is just winding around.

λ = h / p wavelength = planck's constant ÷ momentum

Momentum sets the spin rate. Crank it up and the arrow winds tighter — shorter wavelength. It was never about going fast; a heavy thing has huge momentum even when slow, so its arrow spins into a blur. Drag the slider, or drop in a real particle.

02  /  Why that counts as a wave

Two arrows, added

A spinning arrow only earns the word "wave" because of what happens when you give the particle two ways to reach the same spot. Each path delivers its own arrow. Line them up: pointing the same way they reinforce — a bright spot. Pointing opposite they cancel to nothing — darkness. That adding-and-cancelling is interference, the fingerprint of a wave, and it's the phase doing every bit of it.

03  /  So, the baseball

Real, and hopelessly scrambled

Run the baseball through section 01 and its arrow spins so violently that one full turn takes about 10−34 metres — a hundred quintillion times smaller than a proton. The wave is genuinely there. The formula never switches off; it's the same one confirmed for electrons, then atoms, then molecules of thousands of atoms, each heavier than the last.

But the instant a baseball touches anything — air, light, your hand — that contact scrambles where the arrow points. You could never line up two clean arrows to watch them add or cancel. Same rulebook as the electron. Just the far, unobservable end of one continuous line.

The universe doesn't keep two rulebooks — one for the tiny and one for the large. It keeps one, and the waviness is in there for everything.

And the honest floor: physicists predict the arrow's behaviour to absurd precision. But what the phase is — a physical thing out in the world, pure information, or something we lack the words for — nobody has settled. You didn't find a hole in your understanding. You found a hole in everyone's.
