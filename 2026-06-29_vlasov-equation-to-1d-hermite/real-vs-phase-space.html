On the left, you see two crowds (cyan moving right, magenta moving left) slide through each other and then bunch into vertical clumps as the instability grows. That bunching is real and visible — it's the density wave. But you cannot see why it happens, because the mechanism is about velocity, and velocity isn't drawn. Without the arrows, a frozen frame tells you nothing about how fast anything moves.
On the right, the same instant shows the whole story: two clean velocity bands that wind up and trap each other into the cat's-eye. That swirl is a pattern in position-paired-with-velocity — it simply has no shape in real space. This is the payoff of spending a drawable axis on velocity.
The takeaway. Your instinct gives a truer-to-life movie, and there's nothing wrong with it — that's basically a particle (PIC) simulation watched in real space. But plasma physics keeps asking questions about the shape of the velocities, and phase space is the one view that makes that shape something you can look at. The "weird" axis is weird on purpose.
Both panels are the identical particle-in-cell two-stream run (k=0.5, beams at ±1.5). Only the drawing differs: left plots (position, display-y); right plots (position, velocity).
