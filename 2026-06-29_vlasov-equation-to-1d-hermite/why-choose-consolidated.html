So when you see a heat-map below, read it as: across = where particles are, up = how fast they're going, brightness = how many. That's phase space. The methods differ only in how they store and push that picture.

B The three strategies

Everyone agrees on the goal.
Nobody agrees on how.

Every method is trying to evolve that phase-space picture. They differ on one question: how do you put a smooth picture inside a computer that only holds numbers?

sample it · PIC

Track particles

Represent the picture with millions of sample "super-particles" and push each by Newton. Intuitive and flexible.

+ simple, handles wild geometry

— statistical noise; needs huge counts to get quiet

grid it · Eulerian

Store values

Lay a mesh over the whole (x,v) plane and store the picture's height in every cell. Direct and noise-free.

+ no noise, very accurate

— cost grows fast; velocity mesh must be very fine

encode it · spectral

Store a recipe

Don't store values — store the weights of a few smooth building-block humps whose sum is the picture.

+ no noise; a calm plasma costs one number

— sharp velocity structure needs many terms

The lesson in one line: all three agree on the physics, and each pays its bill somewhere different — PIC in noise, grid and Hermite in how much fine velocity detail they can hold. There's no free method; you choose which bill to pay.

D So which do you choose?

Pick the bill
you'd rather pay.

There's no universal winner — the right choice follows from your plasma and your question. A practical guide:

reach for

PIC (particles)

  • Your geometry is complicated or messy — odd boundaries, many species, 3-D.
  • You can tolerate (or average away) noise.
  • You want something robust that's relatively easy to write and scale.
  • Most large production and astrophysical codes live here.
reach for

Grid (Eulerian)

  • You need a clean, noise-free answer in low dimensions (1-D/2-D velocity).
  • You're studying delicate effects — damping rates, fine velocity structure — where noise would drown the signal.
  • You're willing to spend memory on a fine velocity mesh.
reach for

Hermite (spectral)

  • Your plasma stays close to thermal (near-Maxwellian) most of the time — then a few modes are enough.
  • You want a clean dial between a cheap fluid model and full kinetic detail.
  • You value exact conservation and a direct link to fluid moments.
  • You can add a touch of collisionality to tame filamentation.

And the deep tie that links the last two: truncating the Hermite recipe at N modes is the same as keeping N fluid moments. So Hermite is the bridge — turn the dial down and it's a fluid model; turn it up and it's a full kinetic grid. That flexibility is exactly why it's worth the extra delicacy.
