Zero → Hero · no statistics required

The Vlasov equation,
starting from a traffic jam.

Eight short, playable steps. We begin by counting cars on a road — nothing harder than that — and end at the equation that runs every modern plasma simulation.

Before we start, one promise. You will not need a single idea from probability or statistics on this whole page. Here, the word distribution means exactly one thing: how thick the stuff is, spot by spot. A heat map. That's the entire concept. Everything else is just watching it move.
Start at zero
1 A map that counts, not names

You already understand
a distribution.

Here's a stretch of road with cars on it. Don't track any one car — just look at how packed the road is at each point. The curve underneath is that packing, drawn out. Drag the slider toward rush hour and watch a thick clump appear downtown.

evenly spread out

That curve is a distribution. No car has a name, nothing was tracked, and yet the picture tells you everything about where the crowd is. When physicists say "distribution function," this homely density curve is all they mean — just for particles instead of cars.

2 One axis can't predict the future

Where isn't enough.
You also need how fast.

Imagine two spots on the road, equally jammed. In one, every car is doing 70. In the other, everyone's parked. A minute later those two spots look completely different — but the packing curve from Stage 1 drew them identically. To see what happens next, the map needs a second piece of information: speed.

The fix is almost insultingly simple: add a second axis. Left–right stays position. Up–down becomes speed. Now a dot's height tells you how fast it's going. Press the button below and watch the road lift into that second dimension.
3 The move everything depends on

Watch the map grow
a second axis.

Same cars. We're just letting each one rise to a height equal to its speed. Fast cars float up, parked cars stay on the floor. The flat road becomes a two-dimensional map called phase space.

currently: the flat road

This 2-D map is f(x, v) — the star of the whole show. Brightness/crowding at a point still just means "lots of stuff here." The only new thing is that "here" now means here in position and here in speed. Two stacks can be equally crowded yet sit at different heights — and that's exactly the difference between the doing-70 spot and the parked spot.

4 How the map changes — without tracking anyone

The whole map flows
by two simple rules.

Here is the part that answers your actual question. The map evolves on its own, and neither rule ever requires following an individual. Press play and try both modes.

Rule 1 only: sliding Add Rule 2: pushing

Rule 1 — sliding (because things move)

Every piece of the map slides sideways at a rate equal to its height. High-up pieces (fast) slide quickly; floor pieces (parked) barely move. Nothing is created or destroyed — the stuff just flows along. A compact blob gets sheared into slanted streaks, because its top edge outruns its bottom edge.

Rule 2 — pushing (because forces act)

A force at some location nudges every piece there up or down the speed axis — speeding things up or slowing them down. Crucially, everyone sitting at that spot feels the same push, so the whole stack shifts together. For charged particles the force is the electric field. Sliding + pushing together makes the blob orbit and curl into a swirl.

Read what just happened. You predicted how the entire crowd rearranges — shearing, swirling, trapping — and you never once asked "where is car number four-million?" The map has its own self-contained rules. That is how something tracks a distribution without tracking particles.

5 Where the rules come from

Both rules are just
Newton, in disguise.

You might worry the two rules were pulled from thin air. They weren't — they're literally what one particle does, applied everywhere on the map at once.

A single particle obeys two baby facts you already know:

· Its position changes at a rate equal to its speed. (Going faster ⇒ you cover ground faster. That becomes Rule 1, sliding.)

· Its speed changes when a force pushes it. (That's just "a push speeds you up or slows you down" — i.e. F = ma. That becomes Rule 2, pushing.)

Take those two one-particle facts, apply them to every point of the map simultaneously, and add the bookkeeping rule that stuff is never created or destroyed. The result is the Vlasov equation. So it secretly contains all the single-particle physics — it just records it as "how the whole map flows" instead of "where each particle is." Same information. A sane amount of work.

6 The shape of the speed-spread

"Maxwellian" is just
a shape, not a destination.

Slice the 2-D map vertically at one spot and you get a little curve: how the speeds are spread right there. That curve has a shape, and the shape has a name or two. None of this involves probability — it's just the silhouette of a stack.

A Maxwellian is the single smooth hump: most things near the average speed, fewer very fast or very slow. It's the calm, natural shape — not a place the system is heading. You're equally allowed two humps (two packs cruising at different speeds — the two-stream case) or a shifted hump (a beam). The shape is whatever the physics makes it.

7 The equation, finally — and it's friendly

Now the symbols
can't scare you.

Here it is. Every piece maps onto something you just played with. Click the two coloured terms.

$\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial t}$=− $v\,\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}$ − $\dfrac{q}{m}E\,\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial v}$

In words, with zero jargon: the map's change over time equals the sliding plus the pushing. The left side asks "how is the heat map changing?" and the right side answers "because every piece slides by its speed and gets pushed by the force." You already knew this equation before you could read it.

And the electric field E isn't handed down from outside — the particles make it themselves through their own charge (that's the companion Poisson equation). Field pushes particles, pushed particles reshape the field. A closed, self-feeding loop. That loop is the entire plasma.

8 You're at hero

Look how far
you just climbed.

Trace it back: a density curve on a road → add a speed axis → a 2-D phase-space map → two flow rules (slide and push) → those rules are just Newton for the whole crowd → the speed-spread has shapes like the Maxwellian → and the bookkeeping for all of it is the Vlasov equation. That's the entire conceptual spine of kinetic plasma physics, and you built it from counting cars.
