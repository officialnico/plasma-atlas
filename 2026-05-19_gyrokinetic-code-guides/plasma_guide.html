Chapter One — the setup

The plasma, and why we need a six-dimensional movie

A fusion plasma is a gas so hot that its electrons have been ripped off their atoms. What's left is a soup of charged particles — bare nuclei (ions) and free electrons — at hundreds of millions of degrees. At those temperatures the particles move fast enough to slam together hard enough to fuse, releasing energy. That's the whole point.

The problem is keeping the soup contained. You can't touch it with a wall — the wall would melt instantly, and the soup would cool. So you trap it with magnetic fields. Charged particles can't cross magnetic field lines easily; they spiral around them like a bead on a wire. Build the right cage of field lines and the plasma stays inside.

This works almost. The leak is the subject of this guide.

What we actually want to simulate

Imagine you wanted to make a movie of the plasma. What would each frame need to contain?

Your first thought is probably: the position of every particle. Three numbers per particle, billions of particles, easy. But that misses something crucial. At any one point in space, the plasma contains particles moving in all directions at all speeds. The fast ones and the slow ones, the ones moving along the field and the ones moving across it — they all behave differently. A "hot spot" doesn't just mean "more particles here"; it can also mean "the particles here are moving faster on average."

So at every point in space, we need to know not just how many particles are there, but how their velocities are distributed.

The distribution function

For every point in space (x, y, z) and every possible velocity (v_x, v_y, v_z), the distribution function f tells you the density of particles at that position moving with that velocity. It's a function of six variables — three space, three velocity — plus time.

A six-dimensional function. That's the object we're tracking. If you split each dimension into 100 grid points, you'd need 10¹² (a trillion) numbers just for one frame. This is why plasma simulation is hard.

The gyrokinetic trick

Here's a saving grace. Inside a strong magnetic field, particles don't drift around lazily — they spiral tightly around the field lines. The spiral is small and fast (a millionth of a second to go around once, a millimeter wide). The interesting physics — the turbulence that leaks heat out — happens on much slower, larger scales.

The orange dot is a charged particle. The cyan line is the magnetic field. Turn the field up — the spiral gets tighter and faster, and the particle's guiding center (the average position it spirals around) drifts smoothly. Gyrokinetics throws away the fast spiral and tracks only the guiding center, plus how fast the particle is spinning. That cuts six dimensions down to five, and a million tiny time steps down to one.

So the simulation tracks five things at every grid point: three spatial coordinates, the speed along the field line (v∥), and how energetically the particle is spinning around the field (the "magnetic moment", μ). Plus time. That's the five-dimensional gyrokinetic distribution function.

Where the buzzwords come in

Every concept in the rest of this guide is about how to actually compute with this five-dimensional function. Eulerian vs. semi-Lagrangian: how do you move it forward in time? Grids vs. spectral: how do you store it? Landau damping, ITG, Rosenbluth–Hinton: what physical phenomena should it reproduce, so we know we got it right?

Chapter Two — how to track motion

Eulerian, semi-Lagrangian, and the two ways to follow a river

You want to simulate something flowing — a blob of dye in a river, a region of hot plasma, anything. There are two completely different philosophies for doing this, and they each go back centuries.

Two thought experiments

The Lagrangian view: imagine you're a boat. You sit on the river and let it carry you. To know where the dye goes, you label each blob of dye, follow it as it moves, and write down its position over time. You go with the flow.

The Eulerian view: imagine you're standing on a bridge, looking down. You don't follow anything. Instead, you stare at fixed points in space — the river underneath the third pillar, say — and write down what you see passing through. Sometimes there's dye, sometimes there isn't. The flow happens; you just observe.

Both descriptions are valid. They contain the same information. But computationally they ask completely different questions:

  • Eulerian: "How is the stuff at this grid point changing?" You discretize space into a fixed grid and compute time-derivatives there.
  • Lagrangian: "Where does this particle go next?" You move tracer points along the flow.

A semi-Lagrangian scheme is a clever hybrid. You keep a fixed grid (Eulerian) but at each step you ask: "to compute the new value at this grid point, I need to know where this stuff came from." So you trace backwards along the flow to find the origin point, then interpolate from the old grid values at that origin. You get the stability of a grid with the geometric honesty of following the flow.

Both schemes advect the same blob to the right. Watch what happens with a big time step. The Eulerian scheme starts oscillating and goes unstable — because in one step, the wave has moved further than one grid cell, and the local "what's my neighbor doing" calculation breaks down. The semi-Lagrangian scheme just calmly traces back to where the blob came from. That's why semi-Lagrangian is the workhorse of GYSELA/gyselalib++: it stays stable at much larger time steps than a naive Eulerian solver.

Why the new code chose Eulerian anyway

Both approaches are widely used. GYSELA / gyselalib++ (the comparison above) uses semi-Lagrangian. The new code, like the codes GENE, GX, and stella, uses an Eulerian approach (advanced with a method called Runge–Kutta — basically: estimate the time derivative at a few cleverly chosen sub-steps and combine them).

Why? Because:

  • Eulerian schemes are easier to make conservative (energy and particles don't slowly leak into rounding errors). With turbulence that runs for tens of thousands of time steps, this matters a lot.
  • Once you've moved to a spectral velocity-space basis (next chapter), Eulerian fits naturally.
  • Modern GPUs love regular grid operations. Semi-Lagrangian needs irregular memory access (trace-back can land anywhere), which is harder to make fast.
In one sentence

Eulerian watches fixed points in space; Lagrangian follows particles; semi-Lagrangian uses a fixed grid but traces back along the flow at every step.

Chapter Three — how to store a function

Grids vs. spectral methods

Say you have a function — temperature along a metal bar, sound pressure in a room, the velocity distribution of plasma particles. You want a computer to remember it. There are two basic strategies, and they look very different.

The grid approach

Cut the domain into evenly spaced points. At each point, store the value of the function. You now have a list of numbers. To differentiate the function, you do "the value here minus the value next door, divided by the spacing" — a finite difference. To find the function's value somewhere between grid points, you interpolate.

This is what you instinctively do, and it's what most simulation codes do most of the time. It's local (each grid point only needs to know its neighbors), it's flexible (you can refine the grid wherever interesting things happen), and it works on any shape of domain.

The spectral approach

Pick a set of pre-made wave shapes — sines and cosines, or polynomials, or other building blocks. Then ask: how much of each shape does it take to add up to your function? Store those amounts.

This is exactly what a Fourier series does. The function "lives" not as a list of point-values, but as a list of how much of each wave. You're recording the recipe, not the cake.

At low resolution, both representations are crude. Crank it up. The grid points pile on linearly — twice as many points means twice as accurate. The spectral reconstruction does something dramatic: for the smooth wave, just a few wave-coefficients capture it almost perfectly. This is "spectral accuracy" — error drops exponentially with resolution. But look at the sharp step: spectral methods ring (Gibbs phenomenon) and grids handle it more gracefully. Smoothness matters.

Why this matters for plasma simulation

Recall: the gyrokinetic distribution function lives in five dimensions. Three spatial, two velocity. We can choose different representations for each.

The velocity coordinates have a special structure. At rest, a plasma in thermal equilibrium has a Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution — a Gaussian bell curve in velocity, centered at zero. This is the background; the interesting physics is the small deviation from it.

Now: there exists a special family of polynomials, the Hermite polynomials, that are tailor-made for functions multiplied by a Gaussian. They're orthogonal under exactly that weight. A Maxwellian, expanded in Hermite functions, collapses to a single coefficient — the ℓ=0 one. The first deviation (a small flow) is the ℓ=1 coefficient. The next (a temperature perturbation) is the ℓ=2 coefficient. Each Hermite coefficient is a physical moment of the distribution.

So the new code uses a Hermite–Laguerre spectral basis for velocity space, and finite-difference grids for the spatial directions. The choice is per-axis — use whichever fits the math best. Gyselalib++ uses grids for everything.

In one sentence

A grid stores function values at points. Spectral stores the function as a recipe of pre-made shapes. Spectral is wildly accurate for smooth functions and a natural fit when the math already looks like those shapes.

Chapter Four — a famous result from 1946

Landau damping — a wave that dies without friction

In 1946 the Soviet physicist Lev Landau predicted something that everyone thought was wrong. He claimed that an electric wave in a plasma — a collective oscillation, similar to a sound wave — would decay over time, even though there was no friction, no collisions, no obvious source of dissipation. The wave's energy would just… leak away. People didn't believe him for years. Then they measured it. He was right.

The intuition: surfers on a wave

Imagine an electric wave rolling through a plasma. The wave has a phase velocity — the speed at which its crests travel. Call it v_φ.

Now imagine you're a particle in the plasma. You're moving with some velocity v.

  • If v is much less than v_φ: the wave just rushes past you. You barely feel it.
  • If v is much more than v_φ: you fly past the wave. You barely feel it.
  • If v ≈ v_φ: you surf. You sit on the wave for a long time. You can exchange a lot of energy with it.

Particles slightly slower than the wave get a "push" from the wave's electric field and speed up — they take energy from the wave. Particles slightly faster get pushed in the direction they're already going slower — they give energy to the wave. Whether the wave grows or dies depends on which group is more numerous.

In thermal equilibrium, the velocity distribution is a Gaussian — peaked at zero, with a smooth tail. There are always more slow particles than fast ones at any given speed (the curve is sloping down). So more particles steal energy from the wave than give it back. The wave loses energy. It decays. With no collisions, no friction.

Why it matters for the test suite

Landau damping has an exact mathematical formula for the damping rate, derived in textbooks. So if you build a new plasma simulation code, you can run a simple test: start a small wave, let it evolve, measure how fast it decays. If your measured decay rate matches the textbook formula to four significant figures, your code is doing the physics correctly. If not, something is wrong — and the bug is hiding in something subtle, because Landau damping requires every part of your machinery to work: the velocity-space basis, the field solve, the time-stepping, the boundary conditions. Landau damping is the canonical "does your code work at all" test.

In one sentence

An electric wave in a plasma decays because particles moving at almost the wave's speed (the "surfers") tend to steal more energy than they give back — and the simple math of this decay is one of the most reliable tests for a gyrokinetic code.

Chapter Five — the leak

ITG growth — a wave that feeds on temperature

If Landau damping is "waves naturally die in a stable plasma", then the Ion Temperature Gradient mode — ITG for short — is its evil twin. ITG is a wave that grows, fed by the very thing that makes fusion work: a hot core and a cooler edge.

The setup

A working fusion plasma must be hot in the middle (where fusion happens) and cool at the edge (where it touches the wall). So there's always a temperature gradient pointing outward — the temperature drops as you move from the core to the edge.

That gradient is a stored source of energy. It's also thermodynamically unstable. Nature wants to even it out. The way it does so, in a magnetized plasma, is by growing small wiggles that swap hot stuff from the inside with cold stuff from the outside. These wiggles are called drift waves; the temperature-gradient-driven variety is the ITG mode.

An ITG mode is a kind of mirror image of Landau damping: instead of a stable distribution that steals energy from a wave, you have a temperature-gradient-shaped distribution that gives energy to a wave. The wave gets bigger. As it gets bigger, it transports more heat out. As it transports more heat out, the gradient tries to flatten — but the fusion reactions keep refilling the core. So the wave keeps growing, until something else (nonlinear effects, the next chapter) stops it.

A(t)  =  A0 · eγ t ITG amplitude grows exponentially at rate γ — the growth rate depends on temperature, gradient, and the wavelength of the mode

Notice that there's a "most dangerous" wavelength — usually around kyρi ≈ 0.5 — where the growth rate peaks. This is the wavelength turbulence prefers.

Why this matters

ITG is the dominant source of heat loss in tokamaks like ITER and (with some other modes for company) in stellarators like W7-X. Predicting it accurately is the whole point of gyrokinetic simulation. If you can compute the growth rate γ as a function of gradient, wavelength, geometry, etc., you can predict how much heat will leak out of a planned reactor — and whether it will produce more energy than it consumes.

Like Landau damping, the linear ITG growth rate has analytic limits that codes can check against. "Plug in this gradient, this geometry, this temperature, this wavelength — you should get γ ≈ this number." If a code reproduces those benchmarks, you start to trust its predictions in regimes where there is no analytic answer.

In one sentence

ITG is a wave that grows exponentially when the plasma's temperature gradient is steep enough — it's the main mechanism by which fusion plasmas leak heat, and reproducing its growth rate is a primary check on a gyrokinetic code.

Chapter Six — turbulence's safety valve

The Rosenbluth–Hinton residual — zonal flows

ITG turbulence wants to leak heat out as fast as possible. But it doesn't, quite. There's a self-regulating mechanism inside the plasma, discovered theoretically by Marshall Rosenbluth and Fred Hinton in 1998, that puts a brake on it. The residual they predicted has become one of the strictest tests a gyrokinetic code can be put through.

Zonal flows: turbulence eats turbulence

When ITG modes grow and start to turbulize the plasma, something interesting happens. The chaotic eddies don't just transport heat outward; they also organize into large-scale flows that go around the plasma (in the same direction the field lines run around the torus). These are called zonal flows.

Zonal flows are like jet streams in the atmosphere. They form spontaneously out of smaller-scale turbulence (this is called an "inverse cascade" — energy flowing from small scales to large). And here's the key thing: zonal flows shear apart the turbulent eddies that fed them. They cut the eddies up before the eddies can transport heat all the way to the edge.

Zonal flow is not a heat transporter. It just goes around. It doesn't carry heat out of the plasma. But it eats the eddies that would. So turbulence partially regulates itself.

What Rosenbluth and Hinton proved

Suppose you start a tiny zonal flow in an idealized plasma and let it evolve. Without anything to push it, you'd think it should decay back to zero — there's no source. But Rosenbluth and Hinton showed that it doesn't decay to zero. Some fraction of it survives forever as a residual flow. The fraction depends only on geometry — specifically on the inverse aspect ratio of the device and a quantity called the safety factor q. They derived an exact formula.

Aresidual/Ainitial  =  1 / (1 + 1.6 · q² / √ε) Rosenbluth–Hinton residual — what fraction of an initial zonal flow survives forever

For typical fusion-relevant geometry (ε ≈ 0.1, q ≈ 1.5), this fraction is around 15-25%. Quite a lot, considering the alternative would have been "zero".

Codes that get this wrong predict completely wrong levels of turbulence.

The hardest test

Why is the Rosenbluth–Hinton residual such a feared benchmark?

Because it depends on a delicate balance. Getting the right number means your code is handling passing particles, trapped particles, the bounce motion along field lines, the polarization response of the bulk plasma, and the geometry of the magnetic surfaces — all correctly, all consistently. If any one of these is even slightly off, the residual comes out wrong. Worse, it comes out plausibly wrong: still a positive number, still in the right ballpark, but maybe 0.18 instead of 0.22. And since the level of saturated turbulence in a real device is set by the strength of zonal flow regulation, a 20% error in the residual can translate to a factor-of-two error in predicted heat loss.

It's the cleanest example of "gyrokinetic bugs are silent" — a code that fails this test still produces beautiful turbulence movies, just with the wrong physics.

In one sentence

Zonal flows are large self-organizing circulation patterns in plasma turbulence that don't transport heat but do regulate the turbulence that does — and Rosenbluth–Hinton is the analytic prediction for how much zonal flow survives forever, which serves as one of the hardest tests for gyrokinetic codes.

Chapter Seven — the software layer

Kokkos — write once, run on anything

After six chapters of physics, the last buzzword is mercifully simple. Kokkos is a C++ library. It solves a very practical problem: scientific code that needs to run on lots of different hardware.

The problem Kokkos solves

Suppose you wrote your simulation in plain C++ on a normal CPU. It works. Now your new supercomputer arrives, and it has GPUs from NVIDIA (which require code written in CUDA). The supercomputer after that has GPUs from AMD (different language: HIP/ROCm). The one after that has Intel GPUs (yet another language: SYCL/OneAPI). And of course you still need to run on the boss's laptop (a regular CPU). And the postdoc's Mac (Apple GPU).

The naive solution is to maintain four copies of your code — one per hardware platform. This is a nightmare. They drift apart. Bugs get fixed in one and not the others. You spend more time porting than doing science.

Kokkos's solution: write your code once, in a slightly unusual C++ style, using Kokkos's vocabulary for "do this in parallel" and "this is a chunk of memory". At compile time, Kokkos translates that vocabulary into native code for whichever hardware you're targeting. Same source, different binary, optimal performance on each platform.

Why this comes up

Gyselalib++ is built on Kokkos because it's a serious production library aimed at exascale supercomputers — those have a mix of CPU/GPU vendors and the project can't afford to maintain four versions. The new code uses JAX instead, which plays a similar role: write Python that looks like NumPy, and JAX compiles it to run efficiently on CPU or GPU. The trade-off is that JAX is much easier to write (Python) but less performant than carefully-tuned Kokkos on the very largest supercomputers.

So the choice — Kokkos vs JAX — really comes down to "how much of your effort goes into final performance vs. ease of iteration." Gyselalib++ optimized for the former; the new code for the latter.

In one sentence

Kokkos is a C++ library that lets you write a parallel program once and have it run efficiently on CPUs and on GPUs from any vendor.

Coda

All seven, in one breath

A fusion plasma is a six-dimensional thing — three positions plus three velocities, evolving in time. The gyrokinetic trick cuts it down to five by averaging over the fast spiral around magnetic field lines.

To evolve it forward in time, you can either watch fixed points (Eulerian, like the new code/GENE) or trace back along the flow at each step (semi-Lagrangian, like gyselalib++).

To store the function on a computer, you either sample it on a grid, or write it as a recipe of pre-made shapes — a spectral representation. Different axes can use different choices.

To check that your code works, you run three benchmark physics problems with known analytic answers: Landau damping (a wave should decay at a precisely calculable rate), ITG growth (a wave should grow at a precisely calculable rate when fed a temperature gradient), and the Rosenbluth–Hinton residual (an initial zonal flow should settle to a precisely calculable surviving fraction).

Finally, to run the code on whatever computer is available, you either lean on Python+JAX (the new code) or on C++ + Kokkos (gyselalib++).

None of these terms should feel like a buzzword anymore.
