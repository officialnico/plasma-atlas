How eddies actually emerge from the equation

The eddy size, location, and frequency aren't programmed in. They're instabilities of the gyrokinetic equation. Tiny random noise grows, the geometry decides which modes grow fastest, the nonlinear term saturates them. Here's the demo.

Demo: watch eddies grow from nothing

This is a real simulation of a simplified instability — a 2D fluid with a temperature gradient and a magnetic curvature term, very close to the heart of how ITG (Ion Temperature Gradient) turbulence works in a tokamak.

What you start with: a smooth temperature field that drops linearly from left (hot) to right (cold). Plus tiny random fluctuations of size 10⁻³ — like static. No eddies anywhere.

What you watch: hit play. The gradient is steep enough that the equation is unstable. The random noise grows exponentially. After a while, structure emerges — and that structure has a characteristic size set by physics, not by you.

What just happened:

  • Phase 1 — linear growth. The noise grows exponentially in amplitude. Each ky mode grows at its own rate γ(ky). The fastest one wins.
  • Phase 2 — saturation. Once amplitudes get big enough, modes scatter into each other. Energy stops piling up at the fastest-growing mode and spreads across the spectrum.
  • Phase 3 — steady turbulence. The eddies you see now have a characteristic size set by where this energy spreading balances dissipation.

Where each piece comes from in the equation

The gyrokinetic equation has roughly four terms. Each one is responsible for something specific:

∂f/∂t + v∥ ∇∥ f + v_drift · ∇⊥ f + nonlinear = sources/sinks ↑ ↑ ↑ ↑ evolution parallel drift eddy-eddy in time streaming through B coupling

v∥ ∇∥ f — parallel streaming. "Particles slide along field lines." This term knows about z (poloidal angle). On the inboard side B is bigger, so particles bounce around more. This is why eddies have parallel structure — they extend along the field but don't extend uniformly. Trapped vs passing particles, ballooning structure.

v_drift · ∇⊥ f — drift through B. The drift velocity v_drift is where the magnetic geometry shows up most directly. Concretely:

v_drift = (v∥² + v⊥²/2) · (b̂ × ∇B + curvature) / (q B)

The b̂ × ∇B piece points in the binormal direction (yes, the y direction!) and depends on whether you're inboard or outboard. Outboard: ∇B points outward, so v_drift points one way. Inboard: ∇B flips, so v_drift flips. Combined with the temperature gradient, this drives instability on the outboard side and stabilizes the inboard side. This is the equation-level reason for ballooning.

The nonlinear term — eddy-eddy coupling. Schematically {φ, f} (a Poisson bracket). It looks like ∂φ/∂x · ∂f/∂y - ∂φ/∂y · ∂f/∂x. This term is zero when there are no fluctuations (small δφ, small δf). Once fluctuations grow, this term kicks in and lets modes scatter into each other. This is what saturates the instability and sets eddy sizes.

The equilibrium gradients. The driving term comes from the equilibrium gradients. There's a piece in the linear part that looks like ω*_T · δφ where ω*_T ~ ky · (dT₀/dx) / T₀. This is what makes higher-ky modes grow faster (up to a point) — it scales linearly with ky.

The bottom line None of these terms says "make eddies of size L." They say "particles drift like this," "energy can scatter like that," "gradients drive growth." The eddy size, the spectrum peak, the ballooning shape — all of these emerge from the equation responding to the geometry and gradients you fed it. That's the whole point. GENE doesn't simulate eddies; it simulates the equation, and eddies fall out.

The growth rate spectrum γ(ky) — the smoking gun

Here's the most concrete test: solve the linearized gyrokinetic equation (drop the nonlinear term, keep everything else). For each ky, find the rate γ at which a mode of that ky grows or decays.

Plot γ vs ky. The curve has a peak — there's a fastest-growing ky. When you start with random noise (which has a flat spectrum across all ky), only the modes near the peak grow. Everything else decays or grows too slowly to compete. After a few growth times, the surviving modes ARE your eddies, with size 1/ky_peak.

FLR damping 0.60

Drag ∇T: stronger gradient → modes grow faster. Drag FLR damping (Finite Larmor Radius): higher → small-scale modes (high ky) get killed by gyro-averaging. The peak position is where these two effects balance — that's where ky_peak comes from.

For a typical tokamak: ky_peak corresponds to eddies of size ~ a few ion gyroradii. The geometry sets the gyroradius. The gradient sets the drive. Together they fix eddy size. No magic, just two competing physical effects.

Recap: where each thing in the eddies comes from

Walk through the question "what determines [X]":

  • What determines eddy size? The growth rate spectrum γ(ky). Peak ky → eddy size ~ 1/ky_peak. Set by drive (∇T) vs damping (FLR, Landau damping).
  • What determines where eddies are concentrated (outboard, ballooning)? The drift term v_drift · ∇⊥f. Outboard has destabilizing curvature; inboard has stabilizing curvature. The equation amplifies fluctuations only where it's destabilized.
  • What determines how often eddies form? The nonlinear saturation rate vs the linear growth rate. Stronger gradients → more rapid eddy turnover.
  • What determines eddy lifetime? Decorrelation rate from the nonlinear scattering — eddies live ~ 1/γ_nonlinear.
  • What determines parallel structure? The interplay of v∥ ∇∥ f (smooths along z) and the drift term (localizes at outboard). Result: long along z, but with ballooning envelope.

None of these are programmed in. All emerge from solving the equation with the given geometry and gradients.
