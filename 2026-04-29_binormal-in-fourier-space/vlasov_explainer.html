The Vlasov equation, step by step

From "what is a distribution function" to "how does GENE solve plasma turbulence." Each step has a small interactive visualization. No prior physics required — just curiosity.

step 1 / 8

Forget particles for a second. Track density.

Here are 200 particles bouncing around in a 1D box. Each red dot is a particle.

Now imagine asking, "how many particles are between x=0.3 and x=0.4 right now?" You'd count them. Do that for every little chunk of space, and you get a density — a function n(x) that says how many particles are at each location.

The density is the smooth blue curve below the particles. We're going to track that, not the individual particles.

red = individual particles · blue = density n(x), the histogram of where they are

Why? Because once you have billions of particles, tracking them individually is hopeless. But the density evolves smoothly and predictably.

step 2 / 8

But position alone isn't enough — particles also have velocity.

Two particles at the same location can be doing wildly different things: one moving fast right, one drifting slowly left. They'll be in totally different places a second from now.

So instead of asking "how many particles are at x?", we ask "how many particles are at x AND moving with velocity v?". That's a function of two variables: f(x, v).

Below: the same particles, but plotted in phase space — horizontal axis is position, vertical axis is velocity. Each dot is a particle. The grid behind shows the cells where we count them.

phase space (x, v): each dot is one particle; the grid is the bookkeeping cells

Phase space has a beautiful property: a particle's future is fully determined by where in phase space it is. Position tells you where you are; velocity tells you where you're going next. So if you know the density at every (x, v), you know everything.

step 3 / 8

The distribution function f(x, v): counting particles per cell.

Now let's count. Divide phase space into a grid of cells. For each cell, count how many particles are inside. Divide by the cell's area to get a density per cell. That's f(x, v).

Below: same particles, but now we color each cell by how dense it is. Bright = lots of particles. Dark = empty. This colored grid IS the distribution function.

f(x, v) as a color field: this is the actual quantity GENE stores. The dots become almost irrelevant.
Cell size — your question "How big is each cell?" You pick. It's a tradeoff: smaller cells = more accurate but more compute. GENE typically uses ~128 cells in x, ~16-32 in y (Fourier modes), ~16-32 in z, ~32 in v∥, ~16 in μ. Multiply: that's ~30 million cells, each holding one floating-point value of f. The continuous distribution function is approximated by these 30 million numbers. Cell size IS the resolution of the simulation.
step 4 / 8

Particles flow through phase space — that's the Vlasov equation.

Watch what each particle does in phase space:

  • If it has positive velocity (v > 0), it moves right in x. So in (x, v) space, it drifts to the right.
  • If a force F is acting on it (like an electric field), the velocity changes. So the dot drifts up (if F > 0) or down (if F < 0).

Below, watch the dots flow through phase space. Notice the systematic flow pattern — it's like a fluid in (x, v) space.

phase-space "wind": particles drift right when v>0, left when v<0; force changes v

The Vlasov equation just says the density f follows this flow. If a cell sends 100 particles per second to the cell on its right (because they have positive v there), that cell loses 100 and the right neighbor gains 100. Bookkeeping.

∂f/∂t + v · ∂f/∂x + (F/m) · ∂f/∂v = 0   [change in time] = [flux in space due to v] + [flux in velocity due to force]

Don't be intimidated by the equation. Read it as: "the change in f at a point equals what flows in minus what flows out." Same as water flowing in and out of buckets.

step 5 / 8

The Vlasov equation in action.

Here's the full Vlasov picture: f(x, v) as a colored field, evolving in time. No more individual particles — just the density flowing.

Watch what happens when we add a "potential well" at x=0.5 (a region where particles get pulled in, like gravity at the bottom of a hill). Bright streaks form as particles oscillate back and forth.

f(x, v, t) evolving under a potential. The swirly structures are real physics — Landau damping, particle trapping.

The swirls and stripes are how the Vlasov equation captures things like Landau damping and particle trapping — physics where some particles oscillate back and forth in a wave's potential while others zoom past. You can SEE this in the structure of f(x, v).

step 6 / 8

Real plasma: 6D phase space (3 position + 3 velocity).

Real particles in a tokamak live in 3D, with 3 velocity components. So f is a function of 6 variables: f(x, y, z, vx, vy, vz, t). That's a 6-dimensional grid.

Even at modest resolution — say 64 cells per dimension — that's 64⁶ ≈ 70 billion cells. Each one holds a floating-point number. Each one updates every timestep. Even modern supercomputers can't do that for a real tokamak.

Below: a slice through 6D, showing one (x, vx) face at a fixed (y, z, vy, vz). That's already a 2D field — and there are millions of other slices.

one 2D slice through 6D phase space. The full f has trillions of values.
Why this is impossible at brute force ITER (the next-gen tokamak) has ~10²² particles. Tracking them individually: impossible. Tracking f at brute-force 6D resolution: also impossible. We need a clever trick to reduce the dimensionality.
step 7 / 8

The gyrokinetic trick: integrate out the gyration.

Charged particles in a strong magnetic field don't fly straight — they spiral around field lines, like a kid skating in tight circles. The circle (called the gyro-orbit) is small compared to anything interesting.

Insight: we don't care where on the circle the particle is right now. We just care about (a) where the center of the circle is, and (b) how fast the particle is moving along B and around its circle.

So we average over the gyration angle. That kills one velocity dimension. We're left with f(x, y, z, v∥, μ): 5D instead of 6D. μ is the "magnetic moment," basically how big the gyro-circle is.

left: spiraling particles in 3D. right: gyro-averaged "rings" — gyrokinetics tracks just these.

This 5D vs 6D may not sound dramatic, but it cuts the cost by ~64x at typical resolutions, AND removes the fastest timescale (the gyration), which lets you take much bigger time steps. Combined: ~10,000x speedup.

step 8 / 8

How turbulence shows up.

How does the Vlasov equation capture turbulent eddies? Here's how:

The fluctuating quantities (δT, δn, δφ that we've been visualizing) are all derived from δf — the perturbation of the distribution function. To get δT at a point, you do a velocity-space integral:

δT(x, y, z) = ∫∫ ½ m (v∥² + 2μB) · δf(x, y, z, v∥, μ) dv∥ dμ

So when GENE evolves f under the gyrokinetic equation (which is just Vlasov's bigger sibling, with extra terms from the gyro-averaging), the eddies and stripes seen here all emerge from the way f develops fine structure in velocity space and then gets integrated back to physical quantities.

Below: a side-by-side. Left, f(x, v) shows fine swirls — the velocity-space structure. Right, the integrated δT(x) shows the eddies you'd actually measure. Same physics, two views.

f(x, v) → integrate over v → δT(x). The turbulent eddies emerge from the integral.

Big picture: Vlasov is bookkeeping in 6D. It tracks where particles are and how fast they're going, as a smooth density. Gyrokinetics drops one dimension to make it tractable. From the resulting 5D field, you compute the things you care about (temperature, density, potential) by integrating over velocity. Turbulent eddies are what you see when you do this in a tokamak.

The "magic" was just: don't track particles, track where particles are likely to be.
