The full force in a gyrokinetic flux-tube simulation

From the bare Lorentz force on a charged particle, to what GENE actually computes for guiding centers, plus the self-consistent electric field. Equations rendered properly.

The starting point: bare Lorentz force

For a single charged particle in 3D with charge $q$, mass $m$, velocity $\mathbf{v}$:

Lorentz force on a single particle $$\mathbf{F} = q\,(\mathbf{E} + \mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B})$$

Two pieces:

  • $q\mathbf{E}$ — straight-line acceleration along the electric field.
  • $q\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}$ — always perpendicular to $\mathbf{v}$. This is what bends particles into circles around field lines (gyration).

This is what governs every individual particle. But GENE doesn't track real particles — it tracks gyro-averaged guiding centers. So this raw Lorentz force gets replaced by an effective set of drifts.

Guiding-center motion: the drifts

Once you average over the gyration angle, the guiding center moves with this velocity:

Total guiding-center velocity $$\mathbf{v}_{gc} = v_\parallel\,\hat{\mathbf{b}} + \mathbf{v}_{\nabla B} + \mathbf{v}_{\text{curv}} + \mathbf{v}_{E\times B}$$

Four pieces. Let's unpack each.

1. Parallel streaming

$$\mathbf{v}_\parallel = v_\parallel\,\hat{\mathbf{b}}$$

The guiding center slides along the field line at speed $v_\parallel$. Here $\hat{\mathbf{b}} = \mathbf{B}/|\mathbf{B}|$ is the unit vector along $\mathbf{B}$.

This is the dominant motion in a tokamak. Particles fly along field lines way faster than they drift across them. It's the leftover of "particle moves along its velocity" after averaging away the perpendicular spinning.

2. Grad-B drift

$$\mathbf{v}_{\nabla B} = \frac{\mu}{q B^2}\,(\mathbf{B} \times \nabla B)$$

where $\mu = \tfrac{1}{2} m v_\perp^2 / B$ is the magnetic moment (essentially "how big the gyro-orbit is"). When $|\mathbf{B}|$ varies in space, the guiding center drifts perpendicular to both $\mathbf{B}$ and $\nabla B$.

In a tokamak, $|\mathbf{B}|$ is bigger inboard, smaller outboard, so $\nabla B$ points roughly radially inward. Combined with the toroidal direction of $\mathbf{B}$, this drift is vertical — pushes ions up, electrons down. This is one place where the magnetic geometry directly enters the dynamics.

3. Curvature drift

$$\mathbf{v}_{\text{curv}} = \frac{m v_\parallel^2}{q B^2}\,(\mathbf{B} \times \boldsymbol{\kappa})$$

where $\boldsymbol{\kappa} = (\hat{\mathbf{b}} \cdot \nabla)\hat{\mathbf{b}}$ is the field-line curvature vector.

When field lines are curved (which they always are in a tokamak), particles flying along them feel an effective centrifugal effect. They drift perpendicular to the curvature. Same direction as the grad-B drift — they're often combined into a single magnetic drift:

$$\mathbf{v}_d = \mathbf{v}_{\nabla B} + \mathbf{v}_{\text{curv}}$$

4. E×B drift

The drift that drives turbulence $$\mathbf{v}_{E\times B} = \frac{\mathbf{E} \times \mathbf{B}}{B^2}$$

Independent of charge and mass — all species drift identically. Crucially, $\mathbf{E}$ here is not external. It's the self-consistent electric field generated by the plasma's own charge separation.

This is the engine. Density bumps create $\mathbf{E}$ fields, which cause $\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{B}$ drifts, which move plasma around, which changes the density. The feedback loop in the equation creates eddies.

The parallel force on the guiding center

The guiding center also accelerates along the field due to two forces:

$$F_\parallel = q E_\parallel - \mu \nabla_\parallel B$$

The first term is the parallel electric force. The second is the mirror force — particles approaching a region of stronger $B$ get pushed away, like a magnetic mirror. This is what causes some particles to be trapped, bouncing back and forth between high-$B$ regions on the inboard side.

The gyrokinetic equation, assembled

Plug all the velocities and forces into the Vlasov form. The full gyrokinetic equation looks like:

The gyrokinetic equation $$\frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \left(v_\parallel \hat{\mathbf{b}} + \mathbf{v}_d + \mathbf{v}_{E\times B}\right) \cdot \nabla f + \frac{1}{m}\left(q E_\parallel - \mu\,\nabla_\parallel B\right) \frac{\partial f}{\partial v_\parallel} = 0$$

Same shape as Vlasov. Same bookkeeping ("rate of change = inflow − outflow"). Just with the effective velocities and forces for guiding centers in a magnetic field.

$f$ here is a function of 5 variables: $f(\mathbf{x}, v_\parallel, \mu, t)$. The 6th variable (gyrophase angle) was integrated out by gyro-averaging.

The self-consistency: where E comes from

The $\mathbf{E}$ field in the $\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{B}$ drift is not given — it's computed every timestep from $f$ via Poisson's equation:

Poisson's equation for the potential $$\nabla^2 \phi = -\frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0} = -\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}\sum_s q_s \int f_s\, dv_\parallel\, d\mu$$ $$\mathbf{E} = -\nabla \phi$$

The sum is over all particle species $s$ (ions, electrons). The integral is the charge density at each point in real space.

So you have two coupled equations:

  • Gyrokinetic equation for $f$, given $\mathbf{E}$ (or $\phi$).
  • Poisson's equation for $\phi$, given the density derived from $f$.

Each step: compute $f \to$ integrate to get density $\to$ solve Poisson to get $\phi \to$ take gradient to get $\mathbf{E} \to$ use $\mathbf{E}$ in next $f$ update. Loop forever.

This coupling IS the turbulence engine

A small density bump $\to$ small $\mathbf{E}$ $\to$ drives $\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{B}$ drift $\to$ moves more density $\to$ bigger $\mathbf{E}$ $\to$ bigger drift. The feedback isn't in any single term — it's in the system of equations. Eddies emerge from this loop.

The big picture

Lorentz force $\mathbf{F} = q(\mathbf{E} + \mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B})$ governs single particles.

Gyro-averaging replaces it with guiding-center velocities — parallel streaming + magnetic drifts ($\nabla B$, curvature) + $\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{B}$ drift.

Geometry enters through the magnetic drifts: $\nabla B$ and $\boldsymbol{\kappa}$ depend on the tokamak's D-shape, $q$-profile, ballooning, etc. This is the equation-level reason for outboard ballooning of turbulence.

Self-consistency via Poisson: the $\mathbf{E}$ field isn't external, it's generated by the plasma's own density fluctuations. This coupling is the engine of turbulent feedback.

Turbulence emerges when the equilibrium gradients ($\nabla T_0$, $\nabla n_0$) are steep enough that the equation's own dynamics amplify small fluctuations exponentially. The geometry sets which fluctuations grow fastest, the gradients set how fast, and the nonlinear coupling sets where it saturates.
