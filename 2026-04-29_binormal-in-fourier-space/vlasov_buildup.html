Building up the Vlasov equation, layer by layer

Start with one particle. Add complications one at a time. Each layer answers "what's wrong with the previous one?" By the end, the full equation is just the sum of physics you'll already understand.

Layer 1 of 7

One particle, no forces.

Forget plasma. Forget physics. Just: a single particle moving in 1D. It has a position x and a velocity v. No friction, no force, nothing — just inertia.

The rule is the simplest one in physics: position changes at the rate of velocity.

dx/dt = v

That's it. If v=2 and x=0, then a second later x=2. A second after that, x=4. Easy.

That's our entire physics, for one particle, in one dimension, no forces. Let me complicate it.

but real particles experience forces
Layer 2 of 7

One particle, with a force.

Now the particle has a force F on it (like gravity, or a spring, or an electric field). Newton's second law: force changes velocity.

dx/dt = v (position changes at the rate of velocity) dv/dt = F/m (velocity changes at the rate F/m)

Two equations. They're coupled — the position equation needs v, the velocity equation needs F (which often depends on x).

Below, the particle sits in a "potential well" — there's a force pushing it back toward x=0. So when you push it to the right, it slows down, comes back, oscillates. Same equation that governs a pendulum or a spring.

Notice the phase-space picture below the particle: position on horizontal, velocity on vertical. As the particle oscillates, its (x, v) point traces out an ellipse. Same physics, different drawing.

one particle is easy. real plasma has 10²² of them.
Layer 3 of 7

Many particles. We give up tracking them individually.

Tracking 10²² particles individually is impossible. But notice: at any given moment, every particle has its own x and v. So we can plot all of them at once in (x, v) phase space — a cloud of dots.

Here's the trick: instead of tracking each dot, just track how dense the cloud is at each location. We define a function f(x, v, t) = "density of particles around the point (x, v) at time t."

If f is high somewhere, lots of particles are there. If f is zero, no particles. f is the distribution function.

Why this is a huge win

f is a smooth, continuous function. It doesn't have 10²² entries — it has, say, 100×100 = 10,000 grid cells. Every particle's behavior is captured by how f flows in time. We've gone from impossible to merely difficult.

how does f change in time?
Layer 4 of 7

The flow of f in phase space.

Here's the key insight. From layer 2: each particle's (x, v) point moves in phase space according to dx/dt = v and dv/dt = F/m. So in (x, v) space, particles drift like a current of water:

  • If v > 0 → particle drifts right in x. Top half of phase space flows right.
  • If v < 0 → particle drifts left. Bottom half flows left.
  • If F > 0 → particle drifts up in v.
  • If F < 0 → particle drifts down.

This creates a "wind" in phase space. f flows along this wind. If a region of phase space has lots of particles right now, but the wind is blowing them to the right, then a moment later the high-density region will have shifted right too.

That's the picture. Now let's write it as an equation.

writing it as an equation
Layer 5 of 7

The Vlasov equation, finally.

Here it is:

∂f/∂t + v · ∂f/∂x + (F/m) · ∂f/∂v = 0

Three terms. Read them one at a time:

∂f/∂t = "how fast f is changing at a point."

v · ∂f/∂x = "how f is being carried along x by the velocity v." If at one (x, v) the particles have v=2, and to the left of that point f is bigger, then f at this point is increasing because that bigger-f region is sweeping in.

(F/m) · ∂f/∂v = same thing, but in the v direction. Force is sweeping particles up or down in v.

The whole equation says: the change in f equals what's flowing in minus what's flowing out. It's just bookkeeping for a fluid in (x, v) space.

An analogy

Imagine snow falling in a windy field. f is the snow density at each point. ∂f/∂t is how fast snow piles up. The wind blowing it sideways is the v · ∂f/∂x term. Vertical accumulation/lift is the F-term. Equation: rate of change = inflow − outflow. That's Vlasov.

but real plasma isn't 1D, and B does weird things
Layer 6 of 7

Adding 3D and a magnetic field.

Now particles live in 3D. Position is a vector x = (x, y, z). Velocity is a vector v = (vx, vy, vz). And the force becomes the Lorentz force, which depends on B and the electric field E:

F = q (E + v × B)

The Vlasov equation generalizes naturally to 6D phase space:

∂f/∂t + v · ∇x f + (q/m)(E + v×B) · ∇v f = 0

Same equation, just with vectors. Same idea: rate of change = stuff flowing in minus stuff flowing out, but now the "wind" has 6 components.

The magnetic term v × B is what makes charged particles spiral around field lines. They get a force perpendicular to both their velocity and the field — that always-sideways force is what bends them into circles.

⚠ The 6D problem

f is now a function of 6 variables. Even at modest resolution (64 cells per dimension), that's 64⁶ ≈ 70 billion grid cells. Brute-force solving this is impossible. We need to throw away one dimension — and that's where gyrokinetics comes in.

the trick: average out the gyration
Layer 7 of 7

Gyrokinetics: the trick that makes it tractable.

Charged particles in a strong magnetic field spiral tightly around field lines. The radius of the spiral (called the gyroradius or Larmor radius) is small — for a typical tokamak ion, about 1mm. The eddies we care about are 100x bigger. So we don't care exactly where on its little circle the particle is right now.

Insight: average over the gyration angle. Replace each spiraling particle with the guiding center of its circle, plus two numbers: how fast it's moving along B (call this v∥) and how big its circle is (called μ, the magnetic moment).

This kills one velocity dimension. f goes from 6D to 5D: f(x, y, z, v∥, μ).

The new equation has new terms because the guiding center isn't just sitting still — it drifts:

  • Parallel motion: guiding center slides along B at speed v∥.
  • ∇B drift and curvature drift: when B varies in space (which it does in a tokamak — stronger inboard, weaker outboard), the guiding center drifts perpendicular to B. This is the term that knows about ballooning.
  • E×B drift: if there's an electric field, the guiding center drifts perpendicular to both E and B. This is the term that drives turbulent eddies.
∂f/∂t + (v∥ b̂ + v_drift) · ∇f + (parallel force) · ∂f/∂v∥ = 0

Same shape as Vlasov, just with the proper "wind" for guiding centers in a magnetic field. This is what GENE solves.

Recap of the whole journey

Start: one particle, dx/dt = v, dv/dt = F/m. Just Newton.

Many particles: too many to track individually. Define f(x, v, t) as their density in phase space.

Vlasov: f flows in phase space according to the same dx/dt, dv/dt for each (x, v) point. Three-term equation = bookkeeping of inflow/outflow.

3D + B: generalize to 6D, replace force with Lorentz force. Same equation, more terms.

Gyrokinetics: average over the gyration to drop a dimension. Now 5D. Pick up new drift terms that depend on the magnetic geometry.

Where the geometry enters: through B in the drift terms. The shape of B (∇B, curvature) determines where particles drift, where instabilities grow, where eddies live. The geometry is part of the wind.

Where the eddies come from: not from the equation itself, but from the equation's instability when fed the right gradients. The equation is the canvas. The geometry and gradients are the brushes. The eddies are the painting that emerges.
