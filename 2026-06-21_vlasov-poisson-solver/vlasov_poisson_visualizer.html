01 — the object

First, what are we even solving for?

A plasma has far too many particles to track one by one. So we describe it statistically with a distribution function f(x, v, t) — a density in phase space. Read it as: how many electrons are near position x with velocity v, at time t.

Even though this is a "1D" plasma — one direction of space — phase space is 2D: position across, velocity up. A plasma sitting in thermal equilibrium is a horizontal stripe: uniform in x, and a bell curve (a Maxwellian) in v.

02 — the motion

The Vlasov equation just says: f is carried along orbits

Nothing is created or destroyed — phase-space density is conserved as it flows. Two things move it:

∂ₜf  +  v ∂ₓf  −  E ∂ᵥf  =  0
streaming: fast particles drift right · acceleration: the field pushes particles up/down in v
Streaming. Higher-v particles move right faster → any ripple shears over.
Acceleration. An electric field shifts the whole sheet up or down in velocity.

That's the whole content of Vlasov: the term v∂ₓf shears structure in space, and E∂ᵥf shoves it in velocity. The hard part is that E isn't given — the particles make it themselves.

03 — the feedback

Poisson closes the loop

Where electrons pile up, charge builds; charge makes an electric field; the field pushes the electrons; which changes where they pile up. Self-consistency is what makes this interesting rather than just free-streaming.

04 — the difficulty

Why velocity space fights back

Streaming never stops shearing the distribution. A smooth ripple winds into ever-finer velocity filaments — structure that gets thinner and thinner forever. Put v on a grid and you'd need impossibly many points to keep resolving it.

Free-streaming phase mixing: the same total "stuff", wound into finer and finer velocity stripes.

This filamentation is not a bug — it's the microscopic engine of Landau damping (section 8). But it means a naïve velocity grid is wasteful. We need a basis that captures Maxwellian-like shapes and their fine wiggles efficiently. Enter Hermite.

05 — the trick

Expand velocity in Hermite functions

Instead of sampling v on a grid, write the velocity dependence as a sum of special basis functions Ψₙ(v) — Hermite polynomials wrapped in a Gaussian. Each one is a Maxwellian multiplied by an oscillation of increasing frequency:

f(x, v, t) = Σn=0N−1 Cₙ(x, t) Ψₙ(v)

Choose the scale so that Ψ₀(v) is exactly the Maxwellian. Then equilibrium is just C₀ = 1 and every other coefficient zero — the basis is centred on the physics. And the low coefficients are the familiar fluid moments:

C₀

density

how many particles — the only thing Poisson needs.

C₁

momentum / flow

net drift of the particles.

C₂

pressure / temperature

spread in velocity; higher n = finer structure.

06 — the equations

Plug it in, and the PDE collapses to a ladder

Substituting the Hermite sum into Vlasov, every velocity integral is done analytically. Two clean facts — multiplying by v couples mode n to n±1, and the v-derivative couples n to n+1 — turn the whole thing into one tidy equation per mode (with scale α=√2):

∂ₜCₙ = − √n ∂ₓCn−1 − √n+1 ∂ₓCn+1 − √n E Cn−1 − ν n Cₙ
streaming up & down the ladder · field acceleration · optional collisions · closure: C−1 = CN = 0
The mode ladder: streaming hops amplitude between neighbours, so a kick in C₀ cascades upward to ever-higher n.

That cascade up the ladder is filamentation in disguise — fine velocity structure = high Hermite modes. Keep N modes, set the two ends to zero, and you have a finite, solvable system.

07 — the march

March it forward with RK4

Now it's just a big system of ODEs, dC/dt = RHS(C). The classic 4th-order Runge–Kutta scheme samples the slope four times per step and blends them — accurate, explicit, no linear solves. Push the step too far and it goes unstable (the CFL limit); the page's live run stays safely inside it.
