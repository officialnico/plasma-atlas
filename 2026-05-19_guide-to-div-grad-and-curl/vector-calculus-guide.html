01

Scalar & Vector Fields

Functions that live on space itself

Before we can speak of gradients or curls, we need the things they act on. A scalar field assigns a single number to every point in space — think temperature in a room, or elevation across a landscape. A vector field assigns an arrow: think wind, water flow, or the magnetic field around a wire.

In two dimensions, a scalar field is a function $f(x, y)$. A vector field is a pair of functions packaged together, $\mathbf{F}(x, y) = \big(P(x,y),\, Q(x,y)\big)$, telling you the components of the arrow at each point.

Key Idea

A field is not a single quantity but a rule: give it a point, it gives you back a value. Vector calculus is the study of how those rules change from place to place.

Visualizing fields

Below is your first interactive figure. Pick a vector field from the menu. Hover the canvas: the readout shows the field's value at that point. Notice how some fields swirl, some radiate outward, and some flow uniformly.

  1. Sketch each field by hand on the same axes: $\mathbf{F}_1 = (1, 0)$, $\mathbf{F}_2 = (0, x)$, $\mathbf{F}_3 = (-y, x)/(x^2+y^2)$. Describe in one sentence what each "does" physically.

    Solution sketch
    The first is uniform horizontal flow (like a steady breeze). The second is purely vertical, but its strength grows with $x$ — like wind that gets stronger as you move east. The third is a circulation that gets weaker as you move outward; this is the velocity field around an idealized vortex line.

  2. Where does the field $\mathbf{F}(x,y) = (x - y,\; x + y - 2)$ vanish? Find the single point where both components are zero.

    Solution sketch
    Set $x - y = 0$ and $x + y - 2 = 0$. The first gives $x = y$; substituting into the second, $2x = 2$, so $x = y = 1$. The field vanishes at $(1, 1)$.

  3. For the scalar field $f(x,y) = x^2 + y^2$, sketch three level curves $f = 1$, $f = 4$, $f = 9$. What shape are they?

    Solution sketch
    Concentric circles of radius $1$, $2$, $3$. Level curves of $f$ are circles centered at the origin — $f$ is the squared distance from origin.
02

The Gradient

The direction of steepest ascent

Stand on a hillside. The ground around you slopes. The gradient at your feet is the arrow pointing in the direction of steepest uphill, and its length tells you how steep that direction is. The remarkable thing: this single arrow encodes all the directional information about the surface near you.

Formally, the gradient of a scalar field $f(x, y, z)$ is the vector field $$\nabla f \;=\; \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x},\; \frac{\partial f}{\partial y},\; \frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\right).$$ In two dimensions we drop the last component. The symbol $\nabla$ ("nabla" or "del") is a vector of partial-derivative operators — a piece of bookkeeping that will pay off enormously.

The gradient is perpendicular to level curves. If $f$ is your altitude, walking along a contour line keeps you at constant height — and that direction is the one $\nabla f$ does not point in.

A small example

Let $f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2$. Then $\partial f / \partial x = 2x$ and $\partial f / \partial y = 2y$, so $\nabla f = (2x, 2y)$. At the point $(1, 0)$ the gradient is $(2, 0)$ — pointing radially outward, which makes sense: to climb the paraboloid $f = x^2 + y^2$ from $(1,0)$, you walk away from the origin.

The 2D view above flattens the picture into contour lines. But $f$ really lives in three dimensions — its graph is a surface, and the gradient at every point tells you which tangent direction on that surface climbs fastest. The default view below shows $-\nabla f$ in blue: the direction a marble would roll. Toggle to see the true gradient $\nabla f$ in red — exact opposites, since uphill and downhill point opposite ways. Drag to rotate, scroll to zoom.

Three things to remember

(1) $\nabla f$ points toward steepest ascent. (2) $|\nabla f|$ is the rate of that ascent. (3) $\nabla f$ is perpendicular to level sets of $f$.

  1. Compute $\nabla f$ for $f(x,y,z) = x^2 y + y z^3$.

    Solution sketch
    $\partial_x f = 2xy$, $\partial_y f = x^2 + z^3$, $\partial_z f = 3 y z^2$. So $\nabla f = (2xy,\; x^2 + z^3,\; 3yz^2)$.

  2. For $f(x,y) = e^{-(x^2 + y^2)}$, find the gradient and the point where the field's magnitude is largest.

    Solution sketch
    $\nabla f = e^{-(x^2+y^2)}(-2x, -2y)$, so $|\nabla f| = 2\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\, e^{-(x^2+y^2)}$. Maximize over $r = \sqrt{x^2+y^2}$: $g(r) = 2r e^{-r^2}$, $g'(r) = (2 - 4r^2) e^{-r^2} = 0$ at $r = 1/\sqrt{2}$.

  3. Show that $\nabla f$ is perpendicular to the level curve $f = c$. (Hint: parametrize the level curve as $\mathbf{r}(t)$ and differentiate $f(\mathbf{r}(t)) = c$ using the chain rule.)

    Solution sketch
    If $\mathbf{r}(t)$ stays on $f = c$, then $f(\mathbf{r}(t)) = c$. Differentiate: $\nabla f \cdot \mathbf{r}'(t) = 0$. Since $\mathbf{r}'(t)$ is tangent to the level curve and the dot product is zero, $\nabla f$ is perpendicular to it.
03

Directional Derivatives

How fast does $f$ change if I walk that way?

The gradient gives the steepest direction. But what if I want to know the slope in some other direction — say, due northeast? The answer is a single number called the directional derivative, and it falls out of the gradient through a single dot product.

If $\mathbf{u}$ is a unit vector, the rate of change of $f$ at the point $\mathbf{p}$ in the direction $\mathbf{u}$ is $$D_{\mathbf{u}} f(\mathbf{p}) \;=\; \nabla f(\mathbf{p}) \cdot \mathbf{u}.$$ When $\mathbf{u}$ aligns with $\nabla f$ you recover $|\nabla f|$ — steepest ascent. When $\mathbf{u}$ is perpendicular to $\nabla f$ you get zero — that's the level curve direction.

  1. Find $D_{\mathbf{u}} f$ at $(1, 2)$ for $f(x,y) = x^2 y$ in the direction $\mathbf{u} = (3, 4)/5$.

    Solution sketch
    $\nabla f = (2xy, x^2) = (4, 1)$ at $(1, 2)$. Then $D_{\mathbf{u}} f = (4, 1) \cdot (3/5, 4/5) = 12/5 + 4/5 = 16/5$.

  2. At which point on the ellipse $x^2/4 + y^2 = 1$ does $f(x,y) = xy$ change fastest as you walk along the ellipse?

    Solution sketch
    Along the ellipse, you move tangent to it; the rate of change of $f$ is $\nabla f \cdot \mathbf{T}$ where $\mathbf{T}$ is the unit tangent. This is maximized where $\nabla f$ is tangent to the ellipse, i.e. parallel — found via Lagrange multipliers or parametrize $x = 2\cos t, y = \sin t$ and maximize $f = 2\sin t \cos t = \sin 2t$, peak at $t = \pi/4$, giving $(\sqrt 2, \tfrac{1}{\sqrt 2})$.

  3. Show that the maximum value of $D_{\mathbf{u}} f$ over all unit $\mathbf{u}$ equals $|\nabla f|$, and the minimum equals $-|\nabla f|$.

    Solution sketch
    $D_{\mathbf{u}} f = \nabla f \cdot \mathbf{u} = |\nabla f|\, |\mathbf{u}| \cos\theta = |\nabla f| \cos\theta$. Max at $\theta = 0$ (aligned), min at $\theta = \pi$ (anti-aligned).
04

Divergence

Sources, sinks, and the breath of a field

Imagine the vector field $\mathbf{F}$ as the velocity of a fluid. Place a tiny imaginary balloon at a point. Does the fluid push out of the balloon faster than it flows in? Then the point is a source. The other way — net flow inward — and it's a sink. The number that measures this net outflow per unit volume is the divergence.

For $\mathbf{F} = (P, Q, R)$ in three dimensions, $$\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} \;=\; \frac{\partial P}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial Q}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial R}{\partial z}.$$ It's a scalar: positive where the field expands, negative where it contracts, zero where the flow is incompressible.

The notation $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F}$ pretends $\nabla$ is a vector and $\cdot$ is a dot product. It is a mnemonic, but a remarkably good one — the answer always comes out right.

Two flagship examples

For the radial field $\mathbf{F} = (x, y)$, we have $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = 1 + 1 = 2$ everywhere. The field expands uniformly — every point is a source. For the rotation $\mathbf{F} = (-y, x)$, we have $\partial_x(-y) + \partial_y(x) = 0$. A pure rotation has no divergence: fluid swirls around without compressing.

Divergence is most intuitive when you watch actual particles move with the flow. In the figure below, dots are released into a 3D vector field and carried along by it. Wherever divergence is positive, dots spread out and the air gets thin; wherever it's negative, they pile up.

  1. Compute $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F}$ for $\mathbf{F}(x,y,z) = (x^2, xy, xyz)$.

    Solution sketch
    $\partial_x(x^2) + \partial_y(xy) + \partial_z(xyz) = 2x + x + xy = 3x + xy = x(3 + y)$.

  2. The vector field $\mathbf{F} = (x, y)/(x^2 + y^2)$ models 2D flow from a point source. Show $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = 0$ away from the origin, yet the total outward flux through any circle around the origin equals $2\pi$.

    Solution sketch
    Direct computation: $\partial_x \frac{x}{x^2+y^2} = \frac{y^2 - x^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2}$; by symmetry $\partial_y \frac{y}{x^2+y^2} = \frac{x^2 - y^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2}$. They sum to zero away from origin. But on a circle of radius $r$, $\mathbf{F} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}} = 1/r$, and circumference is $2\pi r$, so flux is $2\pi$. The origin hides a "delta-function source.''

  3. If $f$ is any scalar field, prove $\nabla \cdot (\nabla f) = \nabla^2 f$, the Laplacian. Compute it for $f = x^2 + y^2 + z^2$.

    Solution sketch
    $\nabla f = (\partial_x f, \partial_y f, \partial_z f)$, and divergence of that is $\partial_x^2 f + \partial_y^2 f + \partial_z^2 f$ — the Laplacian. For $f = x^2 + y^2 + z^2$: each second partial is $2$, sum $= 6$.
05

Curl

The local rotation of a field

Drop a tiny paddle-wheel into a flowing fluid. Does it spin? If yes, in which direction and how fast? The answer at every point is captured by the curl of the velocity field. Where divergence measures expansion, curl measures rotation.

In three dimensions, $$\nabla \times \mathbf{F} \;=\; \left(\frac{\partial R}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial Q}{\partial z},\;\; \frac{\partial P}{\partial z} - \frac{\partial R}{\partial x},\;\; \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y}\right).$$ In two dimensions only the $z$-component survives: $$(\nabla \times \mathbf{F})_z \;=\; \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y},$$ a single scalar telling you how the field rotates around each point. Counterclockwise rotation is positive; clockwise is negative.

The trio so far

Gradient acts on a scalar and gives a vector. Divergence acts on a vector and gives a scalar. Curl acts on a vector and gives a vector. All three are encoded by the single operator $\nabla$.

Surprising example: pure shear

Take $\mathbf{F} = (y, 0)$ — a horizontal flow that gets faster as $y$ grows. There is no visible swirl, but $(\nabla \times \mathbf{F})_z = 0 - 1 = -1$ everywhere. A paddle-wheel in this flow spins clockwise because the top of the wheel feels faster flow than the bottom. Curl detects shear, not just rotation in the colloquial sense.

In 3D, curl is itself a vector — its direction is the axis of rotation, given by the right-hand rule, and its length is the rate of spin. The figure below puts a tiny paddle wheel into a 3D flow; the wheel orients itself along the curl vector and spins at the curl's magnitude.

Why gradients can't curl

Here is the theorem made tangible: $\nabla \times (\nabla f) = \mathbf{0}$ for any scalar field $f$. The figure below shows a real landscape — hills and valleys — with the gradient field $\nabla f$ drawn on the surface (red, uphill) and paddle wheels scattered throughout. Switch the field type to see the difference. Drag to rotate, scroll to zoom.

  1. Compute $\nabla \times \mathbf{F}$ for $\mathbf{F} = (y, -x, 0)$.

    Solution sketch
    $P = y, Q = -x, R = 0$. Curl is $(\partial_y R - \partial_z Q,\, \partial_z P - \partial_x R,\, \partial_x Q - \partial_y P) = (0, 0, -1 - 1) = (0, 0, -2)$. Clockwise rotation in the $xy$-plane.

  2. For any twice-differentiable scalar $f$, prove $\nabla \times (\nabla f) = \mathbf{0}$.

    Solution sketch
    Each component is of the form $\partial_y(\partial_z f) - \partial_z(\partial_y f) = 0$ by equality of mixed partials. Gradient fields are irrotational.

  3. For any vector field $\mathbf{F}$ with continuous second partials, prove $\nabla \cdot (\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) = 0$.

    Solution sketch
    Expand: $\partial_x(\partial_y R - \partial_z Q) + \partial_y(\partial_z P - \partial_x R) + \partial_z(\partial_x Q - \partial_y P)$. Each mixed partial appears twice with opposite signs — total zero. Curls are divergence-free.

  4. Find a vector field $\mathbf{F}$ with $\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = (0, 0, 1)$. Is it unique?

    Solution sketch
    Try $\mathbf{F} = (-y/2, x/2, 0)$: curl-$z$ is $1/2 - (-1/2) = 1$. Not unique — add any gradient field $\nabla \phi$, since its curl is zero.
06

Line Integrals

Adding things up along a path

If $f(x,y)$ is the density of a thin wire bent along a curve $C$, the wire's mass is $\int_C f\, ds$. If $\mathbf{F}$ is a force field and a particle traces $C$, the work done is $\int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}$. Two flavors, both built by chopping the curve into infinitesimal pieces and summing.

To compute, parametrize $C$ as $\mathbf{r}(t)$ for $t \in [a, b]$. Then: $$\int_C f\, ds \;=\; \int_a^b f(\mathbf{r}(t))\,|\mathbf{r}'(t)|\, dt, \qquad \int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} \;=\; \int_a^b \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(t)) \cdot \mathbf{r}'(t)\, dt.$$

The fundamental theorem for line integrals

If $\mathbf{F} = \nabla f$ for some scalar potential $f$ — we call $\mathbf{F}$ conservative — then for any curve $C$ from $A$ to $B$, $$\int_C \nabla f \cdot d\mathbf{r} \;=\; f(B) - f(A).$$ The integral depends only on the endpoints! Path-independence is the hallmark of conservative fields. Gravity is conservative; friction is not.

  1. Evaluate $\int_C (x^2 + y)\, ds$ where $C$ is the segment from $(0,0)$ to $(1,2)$.

    Solution sketch
    Parametrize $\mathbf{r}(t) = (t, 2t)$, $0 \le t \le 1$, $|\mathbf{r}'| = \sqrt 5$. Integral $= \sqrt 5 \int_0^1 (t^2 + 2t)\, dt = \sqrt 5\, (1/3 + 1) = 4\sqrt 5/3$.

  2. Compute $\oint_C (-y\, dx + x\, dy)$ where $C$ is the unit circle traversed counterclockwise.

    Solution sketch
    Parametrize $x = \cos t, y = \sin t$, $dx = -\sin t\, dt$, $dy = \cos t\, dt$. Integrand becomes $\sin^2 t + \cos^2 t = 1$. Integral over $[0, 2\pi]$ is $2\pi$.

  3. Show that $\mathbf{F} = (2xy + y^3, x^2 + 3xy^2)$ is conservative. Find its potential $f$ and use it to evaluate $\int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}$ from $(0,0)$ to $(1, 2)$.

    Solution sketch
    Check $\partial Q/\partial x = 2x + 3y^2 = \partial P/\partial y$ — equal, so conservative. Integrate $\partial f/\partial x = 2xy + y^3$ to get $f = x^2 y + x y^3 + g(y)$; differentiate w.r.t. $y$ and match $\partial f/\partial y = x^2 + 3xy^2$, giving $g'(y) = 0$. So $f = x^2 y + xy^3$. Integral $= f(1,2) - f(0,0) = 2 + 8 = 10$.
07

Green's Theorem

Curl, but for the plane

Here is our first bridge — a theorem connecting a line integral around a curve to a double integral over the region inside it. Walk the boundary of a region, summing up the field's circulation; you get the same answer as summing curl over every interior point.

For a positively-oriented simple closed curve $C$ enclosing region $D$: $$\oint_C P\, dx + Q\, dy \;=\; \iint_D \left( \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} \right) dA.$$ The integrand on the right is just the $z$-component of curl. Boundary circulation equals integrated curl.

A delightful corollary: setting $P = -y/2, Q = x/2$ gives integrand $= 1$, so the area of $D$ equals $\tfrac{1}{2} \oint_C (x\, dy - y\, dx)$. The shoelace formula's continuous cousin.

Why it works, in a sentence

Imagine tiling $D$ with tiny squares. On each square, line integrate around its boundary; you get curl times area. Now sum across all squares — every interior edge is traversed twice, in opposite directions, and cancels. What remains is the line integral around the outer boundary $C$. Take the tile size to zero and the discrete sum becomes the double integral. Same principle drives Stokes' and the divergence theorem.

  1. Use Green's theorem to compute $\oint_C (x^2 y\, dx + xy^2\, dy)$ where $C$ is the boundary of the unit square traversed CCW.

    Solution sketch
    $P = x^2 y, Q = xy^2$. $\partial Q/\partial x - \partial P/\partial y = y^2 - x^2$. Integrate over $[0,1]^2$: $\int_0^1 \int_0^1 (y^2 - x^2)\, dx\, dy = 1/3 - 1/3 = 0$.

  2. Find the area enclosed by the astroid $x = \cos^3 t,\; y = \sin^3 t$ for $t \in [0, 2\pi]$.

    Solution sketch
    Area $= \tfrac12 \oint (x\, dy - y\, dx)$. With $dx = -3\cos^2 t \sin t\, dt$, $dy = 3 \sin^2 t \cos t\, dt$, the integrand simplifies to $3 \sin^2 t \cos^2 t\, dt = \tfrac{3}{4} \sin^2 2t\, dt$. Integrating over $[0, 2\pi]$ gives $3\pi/4$. Times $\tfrac12$: area $= 3\pi/8$.

  3. Why does Green's theorem fail if $C$ encloses the origin and $\mathbf{F} = (-y, x)/(x^2 + y^2)$?

    Solution sketch
    The field is undefined at the origin; the partials don't exist there, so the region is not simply connected. The "curl is zero" away from origin, but the loop integral is $2\pi$. Green's theorem requires the field to be smooth on all of $D$.
08

Flux & Surface Integrals

Counting field lines through a sheet

Hold a tennis racket in a flowing stream. Water rushes through the strings. The volume per second crossing the racket is the flux of the velocity field through the surface. In symbols: $$\iint_S \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{S} \;=\; \iint_S \mathbf{F} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, dA,$$ where $\hat{\mathbf{n}}$ is the unit normal. Only the component of $\mathbf{F}$ perpendicular to $S$ contributes — sideways flow slides past the strings without crossing.

For a surface parametrized by $\mathbf{r}(u, v)$, the vector $d\mathbf{S} = (\mathbf{r}_u \times \mathbf{r}_v)\, du\, dv$ does double duty: its direction is the normal, its magnitude is the area element.

A canonical computation

Find the outward flux of $\mathbf{F} = (x, y, z)$ through the unit sphere. By symmetry, $\hat{\mathbf{n}} = (x, y, z)$ on the sphere, so $\mathbf{F} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}} = x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 1$. The flux is just the surface area: $4\pi$.

Flux is signed

Choose an "outward" direction for the normal. Positive flux means net outflow through the surface; negative means net inflow. Reverse the normal and the sign flips.

  1. Compute the flux of $\mathbf{F} = (0, 0, z)$ upward through the disk $z = 1$, $x^2 + y^2 \le 1$.

    Solution sketch
    Normal is $(0,0,1)$. $\mathbf{F} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}} = z = 1$. Integrate $1$ over a disk of area $\pi$: flux $= \pi$.

  2. Find the flux of $\mathbf{F} = (x, y, 0)$ outward through the lateral surface of the cylinder $x^2 + y^2 = 1$, $0 \le z \le 2$.

    Solution sketch
    Outward normal on the cylinder is $(x, y, 0)$, so $\mathbf{F} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}} = x^2 + y^2 = 1$. Lateral area $= 2\pi \cdot 1 \cdot 2 = 4\pi$. Flux $= 4\pi$.

  3. Show that for the field $\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{r}/|\mathbf{r}|^3$ (inverse-square law), the flux through any sphere centered at the origin equals $4\pi$, regardless of radius.

    Solution sketch
    On a sphere of radius $R$, $|\mathbf{r}| = R$ and $\hat{\mathbf{n}} = \mathbf{r}/R$, so $\mathbf{F} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}} = 1/R^2$. Surface area $= 4\pi R^2$. Flux $= (1/R^2)(4\pi R^2) = 4\pi$. This is the seed of Gauss's law.
09

Stokes & Divergence

The two grand theorems

Green's theorem was the warm-up. The full machinery extends it to three dimensions in two complementary ways.

Stokes' theorem

Replace the planar region by an oriented surface $S$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$ with boundary curve $\partial S$: $$\oint_{\partial S} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} \;=\; \iint_S (\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) \cdot d\mathbf{S}.$$ Circulation around the rim equals integrated curl through any surface that fills it in. Two surfaces sharing the same boundary give the same answer — the integrand only knows about the rim.

The divergence theorem

For a solid region $V$ with closed boundary $\partial V$: $$\iint_{\partial V} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{S} \;=\; \iiint_V (\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F})\, dV.$$ Net flux out of a closed surface equals total source strength inside. This is why a region with no sources cannot lose flux — it's a conservation law.

The pattern

Each "grand theorem" relates an integral on the boundary of a region to a different integral over the interior. The boundary operator on the left, the differential operator ($\nabla$, $\nabla \cdot$, $\nabla \times$) on the right. They're all special cases of one statement — Stokes' theorem on differential forms, which the next chapter previews.

  1. Use the divergence theorem to compute the outward flux of $\mathbf{F} = (x^3, y^3, z^3)$ through the unit sphere.

    Solution sketch
    $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = 3(x^2 + y^2 + z^2) = 3 r^2$ in spherical. Integrate over unit ball: $\int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^\pi \int_0^1 3 r^2 \cdot r^2 \sin\phi\, dr\, d\phi\, d\theta = 3 \cdot (1/5) \cdot 2 \cdot 2\pi = 12\pi/5$.

  2. Use Stokes' theorem to evaluate $\oint_C (y\, dx + z\, dy + x\, dz)$ where $C$ is the triangle with vertices $(1,0,0)$, $(0,1,0)$, $(0,0,1)$ traversed in that order.

    Solution sketch
    $\mathbf{F} = (y, z, x)$; $\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = (-1, -1, -1)$. The triangle lies in the plane $x + y + z = 1$; oriented normal is $(1,1,1)/\sqrt 3$, area is $\sqrt 3 / 2$. Surface integral: $(-1,-1,-1) \cdot (1,1,1)/\sqrt 3 \cdot \sqrt 3 / 2 = -3/2$.

  3. Use the divergence theorem in reverse to compute $\iiint_V \nabla \cdot \mathbf{F}\, dV$ for $\mathbf{F} = (x, 0, 0)$ over the cube $[0,1]^3$ — first directly, then by flux.

    Solution sketch
    Direct: $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = 1$, integral $= 1$. Flux: only the faces $x = 0$ (normal $(-1,0,0)$, $\mathbf{F} = 0$, contribution $0$) and $x = 1$ (normal $(1,0,0)$, $\mathbf{F} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}} = 1$, area $1$, contribution $1$). Other four faces give zero. Total $= 1$. ✓

The Big Picture

Everything in one frame

All five "fundamental" theorems we've met — for ordinary integrals, line integrals, Green's, Stokes', divergence — say the same thing in different dimensions: the integral of a derivative over a region equals the integral of the original function over the boundary.

Symbolically: $\int_M d\omega = \int_{\partial M} \omega$. Here $M$ is a region, $\partial M$ its boundary, $\omega$ a "differential form," and $d$ a single unified derivative. Specializing to forms of different degrees recovers each theorem in turn.

Theorem Boundary side Interior side
FTC$f(b) - f(a)$$\int_a^b f'(x)\, dx$
Gradient theorem$f(B) - f(A)$$\int_C \nabla f \cdot d\mathbf{r}$
Green's$\oint_C P\, dx + Q\, dy$$\iint_D (Q_x - P_y)\, dA$
Stokes'$\oint_{\partial S} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}$$\iint_S (\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) \cdot d\mathbf{S}$
Divergence$\iint_{\partial V} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{S}$$\iiint_V (\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F})\, dV$

Where this goes next

Maxwell's equations are vector calculus rearranged: $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{E} = \rho/\varepsilon_0$ (charges as sources of the electric field), $\nabla \times \mathbf{B} = \mu_0 \mathbf{J} + \mu_0 \varepsilon_0 \partial_t \mathbf{E}$ (currents and changing fields drive magnetism). Fluid dynamics, electromagnetism, general relativity, gauge theory — all of physics from the 19th century forward sits on this scaffolding.

The mathematical generalization — manifolds, differential forms, de Rham cohomology — extends $\int_M d\omega = \int_{\partial M} \omega$ to spaces of any shape and any dimension. The objects you've just met (gradient, divergence, curl) are the three-dimensional shadow of a more general theory.

    Solution sketch
    FTC: $\int_0^1 2x\, dx = 1 = f(1) - f(0)$. Gradient: $\nabla f = (1,1)$, path from $(0,0)$ to $(1,1)$ — work $= 2$, $f(1,1) - f(0,0) = 2$. ✓ Green's: take $\mathbf{F} = (-y, x)$ on the unit disk — boundary integral is $2\pi$ (chapter 6 hw), interior integral of curl $= 2$ over area $\pi$ gives $2\pi$. ✓ Continue for the others.

  2. Maxwell mini-quiz: $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} = 0$. What does this say in words, using the divergence theorem? What does it forbid?

    Solution sketch
    The magnetic field has zero divergence everywhere, so the magnetic flux out of any closed surface is zero. There are no magnetic monopoles — every field line that enters a region must exit.

  3. Final synthesis. Explain in your own words, in 100 words or less, why the divergence theorem is a "conservation law."

    Solution sketch
    The divergence theorem says: whatever flows out of a closed surface must come from sources inside. If a quantity (mass, charge, fluid) is conserved — meaning it can't appear or disappear except via real sources/sinks — then the net flux through any boundary equals the net source strength enclosed. No flux without a source. This identification of "boundary flow" with "interior generation" is conservation written in calculus.
