A flat region, revolved 360°, is a solid. Every method below is just: how do I add up the slices?

00 the whole game

Slice it. Measure one slice. Add them all up.

That sentence is the entire chapter. Every formula you're about to meet is a different answer to "what does one thin slice look like?" — and the integral sign is just the machine that adds infinitely many of them.

the pattern

Cut the solid into thin slabs of thickness \(dx\). Find the area of one slab's face, \(A(x)\). The slab's volume is \(A(x)\,dx\). Sum them from one end to the other:

\[ V = \int_a^b A(x)\,\,dx \]

Everything else is bookkeeping about the shape of \(A(x)\). Disks, washers, shells — same idea, different slice.

The reason this used to feel like a wall: it's usually taught as four disconnected formulas to memorize. It's one idea with four costumes. Learn the idea and the formulas stop mattering — you can rebuild them at the page.

01 method — disks

When the slice is a solid coin

Take the region under a curve \(y=f(x)\) and spin it around the \(x\)-axis. Each slab is a flat coin — a disk — whose radius is just the height of the curve, \(R=f(x)\).

Area of a circle is \(\pi R^2\), so one slice is \(\pi[f(x)]^2\,dx\). Add them:

\[ V = \int_a^b \pi\,[f(x)]^2\,\,dx \]

That \([f(x)]^2\) is the only thing to get right. Square the height, don't square-then-forget the \(\pi\).

02 method — washers

When the slice has a hole punched out

Spin a region that doesn't touch the axis and each coin becomes a ring — a washer. It has an outer radius \(R(x)\) (the far curve) and an inner radius \(r(x)\) (the near curve). This one matters for you specifically: a tokamak's plasma cross-section is an annulus.

Ring area is the big disk minus the hole: \(\pi R^2-\pi r^2\). So:

\[ V = \int_a^b \pi\Big([R(x)]^2-[r(x)]^2\Big)\,dx \]

The classic trap: it is \(R^2-r^2\), never \((R-r)^2\). Square each radius first, then subtract. Getting this backwards is the single most common washer mistake — flag it now so it never gets you.

03 method — shells

When you'd rather peel it like an onion

Sometimes slicing perpendicular to the axis is ugly (you'd have to solve for \(x\) in terms of \(y\), or the region is stacked weirdly). Instead, cut parallel to the axis into nested cylindrical shells — like tree rings, or the concentric field coils around a tokamak.

Unroll one thin shell and it's a rectangle: height \(f(x)\), length = the circumference \(2\pi x\), thickness \(dx\). So one shell is \(2\pi x\,f(x)\,dx\):

\[ V = \int_a^b 2\pi\, x\, f(x)\,dx \]

Rule of thumb: revolving around the \(y\)-axis but integrating in \(x\)? Shells. It saves you from flipping the whole problem around.

04 method — not everything is round

Sometimes the slice isn't a circle at all

Revolution gives you circles for free, but the core idea doesn't need them. A solid can have a flat base with slices that are squares, triangles, or semicircles standing up on it. Same machine: figure out one slice's area \(A(x)\), integrate.

example setup

Base is the disk \(x^2+y^2\le 1\); every slice perpendicular to the \(x\)-axis is a square. The square's side is the chord width \(2\sqrt{1-x^2}\), so its area is:

\[ A(x)=\big(2\sqrt{1-x^2}\big)^2 = 4(1-x^2)\quad\Rightarrow\quad V=\int_{-1}^{1}4(1-x^2)\,dx \]

No \(\pi\) anywhere. This is the example that proves the method is bigger than the "spin it" trick — it's the reason to trust the slicing idea over the formulas.

05 the payoff — your torus

Compute the volume of a tokamak

Here's the one you actually came for. Take a circle of radius \(a\) (the tube — the plasma cross-section) whose center sits a distance \(R\) from the axis (the major radius of the machine). Spin it. That's a torus. That's the chamber.

the elegant route — Pappus

There's a shortcut that feels like cheating and isn't: a solid of revolution's volume equals the area of the region times the distance its centroid travels. The region is a circle (area \(\pi a^2\)); its center travels one full loop of radius \(R\), distance \(2\pi R\):

\[ V=\underbrace{(\pi a^2)}_{\text{area}}\cdot\underbrace{(2\pi R)}_{\text{centroid path}}=2\pi^2 R a^2 \]

The point isn't to plug into a formula — it's to see it fall out. So set it up as shells and watch it collapse. Revolve the disk \((x-R)^2+y^2\le a^2\) around the \(y\)-axis. Shell radius is \(x\), shell height is the full chord \(2\sqrt{a^2-(x-R)^2}\):

\[ V=\int_{R-a}^{R+a} 2\pi x\cdot 2\sqrt{a^2-(x-R)^2}\;dx \]

Substitute \(u=x-R\) so \(x=u+R\), limits become \(-a\) to \(a\):

\[ V=4\pi\int_{-a}^{a}(u+R)\sqrt{a^2-u^2}\;du = 4\pi\!\left[\int_{-a}^{a}\! u\sqrt{a^2-u^2}\,du \;+\; R\!\int_{-a}^{a}\!\sqrt{a^2-u^2}\,du\right] \]

Now the satisfying part. The first integrand is odd — symmetric region, opposite signs — so it's exactly \(0\), no work. The second integral is just the area under a semicircle of radius \(a\), which is \(\tfrac12\pi a^2\). What's left:

\[ V = 4\pi\left[\,0 + R\cdot\tfrac{1}{2}\pi a^2\right]=2\pi^2 R a^2 \]

Same answer. You didn't memorize it — you built it, and one whole term vanished because you understood symmetry instead of grinding. That's the difference between following math and owning it.

A JET-scale machine with \(R\approx 3\text{ m},\ a\approx 1\text{ m}\): \(V=2\pi^2(3)(1)^2\approx 59\text{ m}^3\) of chamber. You can now compute the volume of the thing you're building simulations for. That's not nothing.

06 cold reps

Now the only part that actually counts

your own rule, restated

Try each one badly, on paper, first — then check. Reading a clean solution and nodding is the comfortable version of learning: it feels like progress and builds nothing. The muscle only grows from you deriving it cold, getting it wrong, and then seeing where it broke. Don't open the check until you've bled on the page.

REP 01disk · warmup

Region under \(y=\sqrt{x}\) from \(x=0\) to \(x=4\), revolved around the \(x\)-axis. Find the volume.

Disk radius is \(\sqrt{x}\), so \([f(x)]^2=x\):

\[ V=\int_0^4 \pi(\sqrt{x})^2\,dx=\pi\int_0^4 x\,dx=\pi\Big[\tfrac{x^2}{2}\Big]_0^4=\pi\cdot 8 \]

V = 8π
REP 02disk · sanity-check a cone

Revolve \(y=x\) from \(0\) to \(3\) around the \(x\)-axis. Get the volume — then check it against the cone formula \(\tfrac13\pi r^2 h\) and confirm they agree.

\[ V=\int_0^3 \pi x^2\,dx=\pi\Big[\tfrac{x^3}{3}\Big]_0^3=9\pi \]

Cone check: radius \(r=3\), height \(h=3\), so \(\tfrac13\pi(3)^2(3)=9\pi\). They match — which is the point: the integral reconstructs the formula you already trusted.

V = 9π ✓
REP 03washer · watch the trap

Region between \(y=x\) and \(y=x^2\) from \(0\) to \(1\), revolved around the \(x\)-axis. (Which curve is the outer radius?)

On \([0,1]\), \(x\ge x^2\), so outer \(R=x\), inner \(r=x^2\). Square each, then subtract:

\[ V=\pi\int_0^1\big(x^2-x^4\big)dx=\pi\Big[\tfrac{x^3}{3}-\tfrac{x^5}{5}\Big]_0^1=\pi\Big(\tfrac13-\tfrac15\Big) \]

V = 2π/15

If you got \((x-x^2)^2\), that's the trap firing — you squared the gap instead of the radii. Now you'll never do it again.

REP 04shell · pick the right tool

Region under \(y=x^2\) from \(0\) to \(2\), revolved around the \(y\)-axis. Use shells.

Shell radius \(x\), height \(x^2\):

\[ V=\int_0^2 2\pi x\cdot x^2\,dx=2\pi\int_0^2 x^3\,dx=2\pi\Big[\tfrac{x^4}{4}\Big]_0^2=2\pi\cdot 4 \]

V = 8π
REP 05boss · the torus, cold

Revolve the circle of radius \(1\) centered at \((3,0)\) around the \(y\)-axis. Do it however you like — Pappus or shells. This is your machine at \(R=3,\ a=1\).

Straight from \(V=2\pi^2 R a^2\) with \(R=3,\ a=1\):

\[ V=2\pi^2(3)(1)^2=6\pi^2\approx 59.2 \]

V = 6π²

If you set up the shell integral and the odd term dropped to zero on its own — you didn't just get the answer, you understood why it's clean. That's the whole thing.

the honest part

The math didn't beat you. The isolation did.

The reason to learn volumes of revolution isn't a transcript — it's that you can't model a plasma in a chamber whose volume you can't write down. This was always load-bearing.

So don't take my word that you've got it. That's the trap we already named — me deriving, you nodding. Put this away, get a blank page and a pencil, and do REP 05 cold with the checks closed. Get it wrong first if that's what happens. Then find out what's real.
