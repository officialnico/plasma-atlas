§ 0. Prelude

First, what is magnetic flux?

A one-page primer on the quantity Φ — the thing every flux tube has the same amount of at every cross-section.

Fig. 0 · Flux through a surface tilt the surface →
flux changes

Flux is a count of how much of something passes through a surface. The classic analogy: hold a fishing net in a river. The water flux through the net is the volume of water per second going through it. Two things matter — how strong the flow is, and how the net is oriented. Face-on to the current you catch the most; edge-on you catch nothing.

Magnetic flux Φ is the same idea, but with magnetic field lines instead of water. Pick a surface (real or imaginary, like a hula hoop hanging in space), and ask: how many field lines pierce through it? That count, weighted by the field's strength, is the flux.

Φ = ∫S B · dA

Decoding the symbols, piece by piece:

  • dA — one tiny patch of the surface. Has an area, and a direction (which way it's facing — the normal to your fishing net).
  • B · dA — the dot product. Math operation that says "only count the part of B going through the patch, not the part sliding along it." Face-on: full contribution. Edge-on: zero.
  • ∫ — the integral sign. Means "add up all these tiny contributions."
  • ∫S — the subscript S just labels which surface we're adding over.
  • ∮ — same thing but with a circle. Signals the surface is closed, like the skin of a balloon. For an open surface (a flat disk), plain ∫ is correct.

Put it together: chop surface S into tiny patches, on each patch take the component of B going through it times the patch's area, add them all up. That total is Φ.

why it matters here Magnetic field lines never end (∇ · B = 0 — Maxwell's no-monopoles equation). So if a bundle of field lines enters one end of a region and the sides are themselves field lines, the same flux Φ must emerge from the other end. That conservation of Φ along the bundle is what makes a flux tube a coherent object with a well-defined "thickness" measured by Φ. The animation shows the same Φ surviving a tilt — what changes is just how we slice it.

With that in hand: onward to flux tubes proper.

§ I. The Object

What a flux tube physically is.

Not the computational domain. The thing itself — a bundle of field lines that hangs together like rope.

Fig. 1 · Flux Tube ∮ B·dA = Φ
constant along tube

A flux tube is a real, physical object — not a region of code memory. Imagine a bundle of magnetic field lines, drawn together like fibres in a rope, all running roughly parallel. Take any cross-section perpendicular to the bundle, integrate the magnetic field through that surface, and you get the magnetic flux Φ enclosed.

Φ = ∮S B · dA · identical at every cross-section

What makes it a tube rather than an arbitrary region: the same flux passes through every cross-section. Because ∇·B = 0, field lines neither begin nor end inside the bundle. They enter one end and emerge from the other — never punching through the sides.

The reason flux tubes behave coherently — why we treat them as a single object that bends, twists, and stores energy — comes from the next section. In a highly conducting plasma, the bundle and the fluid are welded together. The rope is not an analogy. It is the physics.

  • A bundle of field lines, all sharing the same enclosed flux Φ.
  • Side walls are field lines themselves — no flux escapes laterally.
  • Coronal loops, solar prominences, and tokamak field-line bundles are flux tubes.
  • Coherence comes from frozen-in flux (§ II), not from the simulation grid.
distinction In GENE and other gyrokinetic codes, "flux tube" also names the simulation domain — a thin computational slab elongated along the field. The name is borrowed from the physical object. The object exists whether or not anyone simulates it.
§ II. The Theorem

Alfvén's frozen-in flux theorem.

Why flux tubes hold together: in ideal MHD, the plasma and the field are welded.

Fig. 2 · Frozen-in Flux parcels stay on field line
topology preserved

In 1942 Hannes Alfvén proved a startling result about perfect conductors — substances with zero electrical resistance, of which a hot plasma is the universe's main example. The result: in such a substance, magnetic field lines move with the fluid. Pick a tiny parcel of plasma sitting on some field line right now. Let time pass, let the plasma flow and swirl. That parcel stays on the same field line forever, even as the line bends and gets dragged through space. The field and the fluid are welded together. The field is frozen into the plasma.

Where does this come from? From an equation describing how B changes in time:

∂B/∂t = ∇ × (v × B)

You don't need to read it in detail — just notice what's on the right. There's only one term, and it depends on v, the velocity of the fluid. So the rate of change of B is set entirely by the fluid's motion. Move the fluid, the field changes. Hold the fluid still, the field doesn't change at all. That's what physicists mean by a transport equation — an equation that says "this quantity goes wherever the fluid goes." The field is being transported, like a leaf on a stream.

Contrast that with what's missing: a diffusion term. Diffusion is the physics of things spreading out and smearing on their own — a drop of ink spreading through still water, heat leaking out of a hot mug, smoke dispersing in calm air. Mathematically these all have an extra piece in their equations that lets the quantity slowly leak around independent of any bulk flow. Our equation has no such term. The field cannot leak, spread, or rearrange itself relative to the fluid. It can only go where the fluid carries it.

That's the entire mechanism of frozen-in flux. No leakage means field lines can't slip past plasma parcels; plasma parcels can't slip off field lines; and the whole bundle moves as one piece.

From this single fact, an enormous structure follows: magnetic flux through any fluid loop is conserved, field-line topology is preserved (knots remain knots, linkages remain linked), and reconnection — the rearranging of which field line connects to which — is forbidden. Flux tubes exist as coherent objects precisely because frozen-in flux makes them mechanical entities. Bend the fluid, you bend the field. Pinch the fluid, you pinch the field. The "rope" intuition from §I is exactly this: a flux tube behaves like a rope because the field lines inside it are stitched to the plasma at every point.

This is the foundational result of ideal MHD. Not "magnetic wells" — that's a separate idea about confinement geometry. Frozen-in flux is the deeper claim, the one that makes magnetic confinement of plasma possible at all.

in one sentence In a perfect conductor, the field can't slip relative to the fluid — so wherever the fluid goes, the field is dragged along, and two parcels of plasma sharing a field line are bound to each other through it forever.
§ II·a. Interlude

Two viewpoints, one truth.

What the equation ∂B/∂t = ∇ × (v × B) actually means when you move the plasma. Watch from outside; then ride along.

Fig. 2a · Two reference frames drag platter · or press play

First — what do those two words mean? They're 18th-century names for two ways of watching anything that flows.

  • Eulerian · stand still at a fixed point in space and watch stuff go by. The river example: you stand on the bridge and watch the water flow past beneath you. You measure the water's speed, depth, temperature at that fixed point. The water you're measuring is different water every second. Named for Leonhard Euler (1707–1783), Swiss mathematician. This is how thermometers, traffic cameras, and weather stations work.
  • Lagrangian · ride along with a particular parcel of stuff and watch what happens to it. Same river: you jump in a boat, drift downstream with the current, and measure how the water around your boat changes over time. You're tracking the same parcel of water as it moves. Named for Joseph-Louis Lagrange (1736–1813), Italian-French mathematician. This is how a fitness tracker on your wrist works (it follows you, not a fixed location).

Both viewpoints describe the same physics, just measured by a different observer. Sometimes one is easier to use than the other.

A traffic example: the average speed of cars passing this intersection is an Eulerian measurement (fixed point, different cars). The average speed of your commute is a Lagrangian measurement (one car, changing position). Both are meaningful; they answer different questions.

For our plasma physics: Eulerian = lab frame (stand outside, watch the plasma flow past), Lagrangian = co-moving frame (ride with the plasma parcel, watch the field as you carry it around).

Now, back to your question: if I move a chunk of plasma rigidly on a platter, does the field change? The answer is yes and no — and which one depends on which observer is doing the watching.

The figure shows the same physical experiment from both viewpoints. A platter of plasma (the orange rectangle) holds a magnetic field — drawn as little blue arrows attached to the plasma. A fixed observer watches from the lab (Eulerian); a co-moving observer rides along with it (Lagrangian).

You can drag the platter horizontally, or hit play to translate it automatically. Toggle switch frame to change which observer the camera follows.

  • Lab frame (Eulerian). Fix a point in space — the small white crosshair. As the platter slides past, the field at that fixed point changes: empty, then arrow, then empty again. This is ∂B/∂t ≠ 0. The field "changes" because different parts of the pattern arrive at your location. Nothing happened to the field itself — it just drove by.
  • Platter frame (Lagrangian). Ride with the plasma. From here, the platter is stationary, the field arrows are stationary, the lab is the thing moving. The field relative to the fluid never changes. This is DB/Dt = 0.

Both observers are right. They're describing the same physical situation in two reference frames. The equation ∂B/∂t = ∇ × (v × B) is written in the lab frame, so its left-hand side is nonzero whenever the field-bearing fluid sweeps through. But the physics it encodes — the field is welded to the fluid — is most cleanly stated in the platter frame, where the field is provably unchanging.

why both are needed Frozen-in flux is fundamentally a Lagrangian statement ("ride with the fluid; the field is unchanging relative to you"). But our equations are written in lab coordinates because that's where instruments and grids live. The reconciliation: in the lab, B at a point changes only because the fluid is sweeping past — never because the field has a life of its own.

This generalises to the more complicated case where the fluid does deform — stretches, twists, pinches. Then the lab-frame ∂B/∂t reflects both effects (sweeping past plus the field stretching/twisting along with the fluid), while the platter-frame DB/Dt cleanly isolates only the second. Frozen-in flux is the statement that there is no third effect — the field never does anything other than what the fluid forces it to do.

§ III. The Distinction

Ideal vs. resistive MHD.

It isn't about turbulence symmetry. It's about whether field lines can slip.

Fig. 3 · Reconnection η > 0 → field lines slip
X-point forms · topology changes

The induction equation in its full form contains a diffusion term controlled by the magnetic resistivity η:

∂B/∂t = ∇ × (v × B) + (η/μ₀) ∇²B

Ideal MHD sets η = 0. Conductivity is infinite. The transport term dominates: field lines convect with the fluid, frozen-in flux holds exactly, and magnetic topology is invariant. No reconnection is possible — ever.

Resistive MHD keeps finite η. The diffusion term lets field lines slip relative to the fluid. Where gradients in B are steep — in thin current sheets — diffusion can dominate locally even when η is tiny, and field lines reconnect, rearranging topology.

· Ideal MHD ·

  • η = 0
  • frozen-in: exact
  • topology: preserved
  • reconnection: forbidden
  • flux Φ: conserved
  • helicity: conserved exactly

· Resistive MHD ·

  • η > 0
  • frozen-in: approximate
  • topology: can change
  • reconnection: possible
  • flux Φ: not conserved
  • helicity: approximately conserved

This is the same physics as the frozen-in theorem, viewed from a different angle. It's also why reconnection is hard: real plasmas have tiny resistivity, so reconnection requires geometry that concentrates current into vanishingly thin sheets where η ∇²B finally bites. Solar flares, magnetospheric substorms, tokamak sawteeth — all powered by this slow concession of η ≠ 0.

§ IV. The Invariant

Magnetic helicity.

A topological number that counts linkage and twist. Not the safety factor.

Fig. 4 · Helicity H = ∫ A · B dV
twist + linkage

Magnetic helicity is the topological invariant of ideal MHD. It is defined globally over a volume V:

H = ∫V A · B dV · B = ∇ × A

The new symbol A is called the vector potential. It's a mathematical companion to B, defined so that taking its curl (a calculus operation that measures local twisting) gives the magnetic field: ∇ × A = B. Physicists use A because some quantities — including helicity — are much easier to write in terms of A than in terms of B alone.

There's a wrinkle: A is not unique. Many different choices of A give the same B. (Adding any gradient ∇f to A leaves ∇ × A unchanged, since the curl of a gradient is always zero.) This freedom to redefine A is called gauge freedom. So at first glance the formula H = ∫ A · B dV looks broken — its answer would seem to depend on which A you picked, a choice with no physical meaning.

The rescue: if the volume V is bounded by a flux surface — a surface that B is everywhere tangent to, so no field lines poke through it — then a small miracle happens. All the gauge-dependent terms cancel out at the boundary, and H comes out the same number regardless of which A you chose. So on a properly chosen volume, H is gauge-invariant: a definite, unambiguous physical quantity.

And once H is well-defined, it has a clean topological meaning: it measures the total linkage and twist of magnetic field lines inside V.

Two unlinked, untwisted flux tubes: H = 0. Two tubes linked once around each other (Hopf link): H = 2 Φ₁ Φ₂. A single tube twisted N times about its own axis: H = N Φ². The number is a count of how the field knots itself up.

In ideal MHD, H is conserved exactly. This is stronger than energy conservation — it constrains evolution to leave knottedness intact. In resistive MHD, H decays only slowly: reconnection rearranges topology locally but barely touches the global integral. This is the basis of Taylor relaxation: plasmas settle into the minimum-energy state at fixed total helicity, giving force-free configurations (§ VI).

distinction The safety factor q = m/n is the field-line pitch on a magnetic surface — a local geometric quantity. Helicity H is a global topological integral. They're related (twisted fields have nontrivial q and contribute to H) but not the same number, in the same way the slope of a screw and its number of turns are related but distinct.
§ V. The Instabilities

Kink & sausage modes.

m = 1 and m = 0. The two ways a current-carrying plasma column dies.

Fig. 5 · MHD instabilities mode: m = 1 (kink)

Take a straight cylindrical plasma carrying axial current I, with axial field Bz and azimuthal field Bθ(r) from that current. Linearise the ideal MHD equations around this equilibrium. Decompose perturbations into Fourier modes ∼ exp(i mθ + i k z). Two modes are textbook unstable.

The sausage mode (m = 0). Axisymmetric: the column pinches and bulges along its length, like links of sausage. Where the column is squeezed, Bθ ∝ I/r grows stronger, squeezing harder — a runaway pinch. Stabilised by sufficient axial field Bz (the Kruskal–Shafranov condition for m = 0 reduces to Bz² > Bθ²/2).

The kink mode (m = 1). Helical: the column buckles sideways into a corkscrew. A small lateral displacement bunches field lines on the inside of the curve and rarefies them outside, producing a magnetic pressure imbalance that amplifies the displacement. The Kruskal–Shafranov stability condition for a tokamak: q(a) > 1 at the edge.

δW = δWfluid + δWvac < 0 ⇒ unstable

Both are derived the same way: write δW, the perturbed potential energy, as a quadratic form in the displacement ξ. If any allowed ξ makes δW negative, the equilibrium is unstable on the Alfvén timescale. The kink and sausage modes are the lowest-order such displacements of a current-carrying column.

historical note Z-pinch fusion programmes of the 1950s ran straight into the m = 0 sausage and m = 1 kink. They are the reason tokamaks use a strong toroidal field — it is the Bz stabiliser, written into the machine geometry.
§ VI. The Relaxed State

Force-free fields.

When magnetic pressure dominates, the field arranges itself so that J × B = 0.

Fig. 6 · Force-free ∇ × B = α B
solar coronal loops

In a low-β plasma — one where magnetic pressure vastly exceeds thermal pressure, β = 2μ₀p/B² ≪ 1 — the Lorentz force J × B dominates the momentum equation. The only way for such a plasma to sit in quasi-static equilibrium is for J × B itself to vanish:

J × B = 0 ⇔ J ∥ B ⇔ ∇ × B = α(r) B

This is the force-free equation. The current flows along the field, never across it. A scalar function α(r) sets the local twist; taking divergence of both sides shows B · ∇α = 0, so α is constant along each field line.

When α is the same constant throughout the volume, the field is linear force-free, and B satisfies a vector Helmholtz equation ∇²B + α²B = 0 — admitting elegant analytic solutions, the simplest being Taylor states. Plasmas that undergo turbulent relaxation in the presence of weak resistivity (Taylor relaxation, § IV) settle into these states: minimum magnetic energy at fixed total helicity.

The solar corona is the canonical laboratory. β there is ∼ 10⁻⁴; coronal loops are almost perfectly force-free, with their arched, twisted shapes set by ∇ × B = α B and α inherited from the photospheric footpoints. When α grows past a critical value, the loop kink-unstables (§ V) — and a flare ensues.

  • The natural equilibrium of low-β magnetised plasma.
  • Current strictly parallel to the field; no perpendicular component.
  • α(r) is a constant of integration along each field line.
  • The end state of Taylor relaxation under helicity conservation.
  • Drives solar coronal physics: loops, prominences, flare onset.
