An identity in vector calculus / electromagnetism

How does ∇×(v×B) equal −∂B/∂t?

Spoiler: it's not magic, it's bookkeeping. If a magnetic field is moving past you, every "change in time" you measure is just the field's spatial structure sliding by. Let's build it.

$$\nabla \times (\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B}) \;=\; -\,\frac{\partial \mathbf{B}}{\partial t}$$

(when v is a uniform rigid translation and ∇·B = 0)

SCENE 01 — The 1D warm-up

If a shape slides past you, "time change" is just "spatial slope" times speed.

Forget vectors for a sec. Imagine a wavy curve f(x) printed on a long ribbon. The ribbon is dragged to the right at speed v. You're a stationary dot. What do you see over time?

f(x) — the field shape on the ribbon what the stationary observer measures

The observer sits at x = x₀. At time t, the ribbon has shifted by vt, so the value at the observer's location is f(x₀ − vt). Take the time derivative:

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\, f(x - vt) \;=\; -\,v \cdot f'(x - vt) \;=\; -\,v\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}$$
"How fast does the field change here?" = "How steep is it next to me?" × "How fast is it sliding past."

That minus sign is the whole story. If the field is rising in space (positive slope) and moving right, then what's arriving at your point is the lower stuff behind it — so locally, the value drops. Spatial up + motion right = temporal down. Hence the minus.

SCENE 02 — Promote it to 3D vectors

Same idea. Now the "shape" is a vector field, and the slide is a vector velocity.

Let B(r) be a static magnetic-field pattern frozen in space. Now imagine the entire pattern rigidly translates with velocity v. The field "at point r at time t" becomes:

$$\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{r},\, t) \;=\; \mathbf{B}_0(\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{v}\,t)$$

Differentiate with respect to time, exactly like the 1D case but with a vector chain rule:

$$\frac{\partial \mathbf{B}}{\partial t} \;=\; -\,(\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla)\,\mathbf{B}$$

So that's what the time-change is: the directional derivative of B along v, with a minus. We've already crossed half the bridge. All that's left is to show ∇×(v×B) is the same thing.

A non-uniform B pattern (gold arrows) being dragged at v (teal). Watch the field "arriving" at any fixed grid point.
SCENE 03 — The algebra (it's just one identity)

Crack open ∇×(v×B) with the BAC–CAB rule.

There's a standard vector-calculus identity for the curl of a cross product:

$$\nabla\times(\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{B}) = \mathbf{A}(\nabla\!\cdot\!\mathbf{B}) - \mathbf{B}(\nabla\!\cdot\!\mathbf{A}) + (\mathbf{B}\!\cdot\!\nabla)\mathbf{A} - (\mathbf{A}\!\cdot\!\nabla)\mathbf{B}$$

Plug in A = v, B = B:

$$\nabla\times(\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}) = \underbrace{\mathbf{v}(\nabla\!\cdot\!\mathbf{B})}_{\textbf{= 0}} - \underbrace{\mathbf{B}(\nabla\!\cdot\!\mathbf{v})}_{\textbf{= 0}} + \underbrace{(\mathbf{B}\!\cdot\!\nabla)\mathbf{v}}_{\textbf{= 0}} - (\mathbf{v}\!\cdot\!\nabla)\mathbf{B}$$
∇·B = 0 — Maxwell's no-monopole law. Always. Term gone.
∇·v = 0 — for a uniform rigid translation, v is constant in space, so all its derivatives vanish. Term gone.
(B·∇)v = 0 — same reason: v has no spatial variation, so any directional derivative of it is zero. Term gone.
What survives: −(v·∇)B. Exactly the thing we just computed for ∂B/∂t.
$$\boxed{\;\nabla\times(\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}) = -(\mathbf{v}\!\cdot\!\nabla)\,\mathbf{B} = -\frac{\partial \mathbf{B}}{\partial t}\;}$$

The cross product and the curl, taken together, are doing exactly the same thing as a directional derivative. It looks mystical because two operators with intimidating notation conspire to produce a simple geometric fact — the field is sliding past, and the bookkeeping just has to match.

SCENE 04 — Why the curl specifically?

The cross product tilts the field — and the curl reads off how the tilt swirls.

Here's the geometric story, the one that makes it feel inevitable rather than algebraic. Pick a moving conductor sliding through a non-uniform B. In its rest frame, charges feel a force per unit charge of v × B. That's effectively an induced electric field. Now: where the induced field curls, flux is changing.

B (out of / into page, size varies left→right) v (rightward) v × B (the induced E) curl ≠ 0 here

Look at the violet vectors. They all point the same direction (upward, say), but their length varies with x because |B| varies with x. A vector field whose magnitude changes perpendicular to its direction — that's a curl. Stick a tiny paddlewheel in it and it spins. The spin axis points out of the page (or into it, depending on the gradient's sign). And that direction is exactly −∂B/∂t for our sliding field.

The whole equation, in one sentence:

A moving magnetic field configuration registers as a time-changing field at any fixed point, and the cross product of velocity with that field — once you take its curl — packages that same information.

It's a coordinate change between two ways of seeing the same flux: spatial structure carried by motion, or temporal change measured in place.

APPENDIX — Why this identity is named after Faraday

The motional EMF and the transformer EMF are the same EMF.

Faraday's law in its full form is $\nabla \times \mathbf{E} = -\partial \mathbf{B}/\partial t$. In a frame where the field is just sliding by, you can compute the EMF two ways:

  • "Motional" view: charges in the conductor feel v × B, take the line integral around a loop, get an EMF.
  • "Transformer" view: at any fixed point, B is changing in time, so by Faraday there's an induced E with curl −∂B/∂t.

This identity is exactly the statement that these two pictures give the same answer. The motional EMF, viewed as a curl, equals the rate of flux change. That's not a coincidence — it's relativity peeking through 19th-century equations.
