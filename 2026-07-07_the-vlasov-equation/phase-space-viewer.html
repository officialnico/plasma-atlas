You can't see six dimensions.
So you watch one plane of it.

The Vlasov f(x, y, z, vₓ, v_y, v_z) lives on a 6D phase space — three positions, three velocities. No screen shows that. The trick physicists use is to hold most coordinates fixed (or integrate them out) and look at a single 2D slice: one position against its own velocity. Below is that slice, rendered as a glowing density of sample particles. Each obeys the exact dynamics, so what you see is f being carried by the flow — and you can watch df/dt = 0 stretch it without ever destroying a drop.

The two colors tag where each particle started. Watch them: the density gets pulled into ever-thinner threads and sheets, but the threads never merge and never tear. That's Liouville's theorem made visible — phase-space volume is incompressible, so f stays constant along every orbit even as its shape becomes infinitely intricate. If the structure looks like it's smearing into a smooth blur, that's only because your eye (and any real measurement) coarse-grains over the filaments. The true f never loses information; the apparent relaxation is the resolution of your instrument giving up — which is exactly how a collisionless plasma "relaxes" without collisions.
